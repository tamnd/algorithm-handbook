## History

Early computers used many floating-point formats, with different bases, word lengths and rounding rules, and the same program could give different answers on different machines. James H. Wilkinson worked out the rounding error analysis of matrix computations in the 1950s and 1960s. His main idea was [backward error analysis](/backward-error-analysis/): show that the computed answer is the exact answer to a slightly changed problem. Together with the [condition number](/condition-number/), it is still the main way to reason about accuracy, and its definitive treatment is the book by Nicholas Higham.

The first compensated methods appeared in the same period. William Kahan published [compensated summation](/kahan-summation/) in 1965, and Ole Møller described a similar idea in the same year. T. J. Dekker gave the exact product of two floats in 1971, using a splitting due to G. W. Veltkamp. Knuth's *Seminumerical Algorithms* gives the branch-free [TwoSum](/two-sum/), and Arnold Neumaier published his variant of Kahan's method in 1974. Sterbenz's book *Floating-Point Computation* (1974) contains the lemma that bears his name.

The [IEEE 754](/ieee-754/) standard of 1985 fixed the binary formats, correct rounding of the basic operations, the four rounding modes, gradual underflow, the special values and the exceptions. Kahan was its main architect and received the Turing Award in 1989. IEEE 854 (1987) covered other radixes. The revision of 2008 added the fused multiply-add, binary16, binary128 and the decimal formats, and the revision of 2019 made small changes and corrections. David Goldberg's survey of 1991 made these ideas known to programmers outside numerical analysis.

The binary-decimal conversion problem was settled in 1990. That year, at the same conference, Guy Steele and Jon White presented [Dragon4](/dragon4/) for printing and William Clinger presented [correct reading](/clinger-decimal-conversion/). David Gay's `dtoa` library put both into practice. Faster shortest-output printers followed: Florian Loitsch's [Grisu](/grisu/) in 2010 and Ulf Adams's [Ryu](/ryu/) in 2018. In the 2000s, Takeshi Ogita, Siegfried Rump and Shin'ichi Oishi showed that compensated sums and dot products give results as accurate as twice the working precision ([Dot2](/dot2-compensated-dot-product/)). Work on [reproducible summation](/reproducible-summation/) by James Demmel and Hong Diep Nguyen and on correctly rounded function libraries continues the line. The rise of machine learning has brought low-precision formats such as [bfloat16](/bfloat16/), and with them a revival of [stochastic rounding](/stochastic-rounding/).

## Further reading

- Nicholas J. Higham, *Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms*, second edition (SIAM, 2002). The reference for rounding error analysis, from summation to linear systems, with full proofs and extensive notes on sources.
- Jean-Michel Muller and coauthors, *Handbook of Floating-Point Arithmetic*, second edition (Birkhäuser, 2018). It covers the standard, error-free transformations, conversion, elementary functions and hardware in depth.
- David Goldberg, *What Every Computer Scientist Should Know About Floating-Point Arithmetic*, ACM Computing Surveys 23(1), 1991. A long tutorial on rounding, the IEEE standard and cancellation, still a good first text.
- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 2: Seminumerical Algorithms*, third edition (1997). Section 4.2 treats floating-point arithmetic and its error analysis with exercises.
- IEEE Computer Society, *IEEE Standard for Floating-Point Arithmetic*, IEEE Std 754-2019. The normative text, short enough to read for the parts you need.
