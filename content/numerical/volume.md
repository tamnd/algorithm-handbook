Numerical algorithms compute with real numbers, and a computer cannot hold a real number. It holds an approximation with about 16 significant decimal digits, and every operation rounds its result. Almost all of science and engineering runs on this arithmetic. A weather model integrates differential equations over a grid of millions of cells. A circuit simulator solves a large sparse linear system at every time step. A search engine ranks pages with an eigenvector. A statistics package fits a curve by least squares. Each of these is a numerical algorithm, and each one has to give a useful answer even though no step of it is exact.

This volume collects the main numerical algorithms and the theory that says when they can be trusted. It starts with floating-point arithmetic itself, then takes the classical problems one by one: equations in one or many unknowns, linear systems, eigenvalues, the approximation of functions, integrals and differential equations. It ends with random numbers, which simulation needs in large amounts.

## Why numerical computing gets a volume of its own

Elsewhere in this handbook an algorithm is correct or it is not. Here an algorithm almost never returns the exact answer, and the question is how close it comes and at what cost. A few ideas answer that question across the whole volume.

- **Rounding is small but not harmless.** Each operation of [floating-point arithmetic](/floating-point-arithmetic/) has a relative error of at most the unit roundoff $u$, which is $2^{-53}$ in double precision. The [standard model](/floating-point-model/) turns this into a tool for proofs. Errors can still grow: [catastrophic cancellation](/catastrophic-cancellation/) turns the tiny errors of two close numbers into the leading digits of their difference.
- **Conditioning and stability are different things.** The [condition number](/condition-number/) belongs to the problem: it says how much the answer moves when the data move. [Numerical stability](/numerical-stability/) belongs to the algorithm: a stable algorithm loses no more accuracy than the conditioning forces. [Backward error analysis](/backward-error-analysis/) separates the two, and the rule "forward error is at most condition number times backward error" runs through every chapter.
- **Iteration and convergence.** Many methods improve a guess until it is good enough. The [order of convergence](/order-of-convergence/) measures how fast. [Newton's method](/newton-raphson-method/) doubles the number of correct digits at each step near a simple root, and the same idea returns in implicit ODE solvers and in iterative refinement.
- **Approximate the function, then work with the approximation.** Interpolation replaces a function by a polynomial or a spline. Quadrature integrates that polynomial instead of the function, and the methods for differential equations step forward with it. The error of each method comes from the error of the approximation, so the [interpolation error theorem](/polynomial-interpolation-error-theorem/) is behind most of the bounds in the second half of the volume.
- **Factor the matrix.** Linear systems, least squares and eigenvalue problems are solved by writing a matrix as a product of simple ones: triangular, orthogonal, diagonal. [LUP decomposition](/lup-decomposition/), the [QR decomposition](/qr-decomposition/) and the [singular value decomposition](/singular-value-decomposition/) are the workhorses of scientific computing.
- **Use structure.** A matrix that is sparse, banded, symmetric or positive definite, or a differential equation that is stiff or Hamiltonian, deserves a method made for it.

## The chapters

**Chapter 12.1, Floating point**, is about the arithmetic that all the other chapters use. It covers the formats of [IEEE 754](/ieee-754/), the [ulp](/ulp/) and [machine epsilon](/machine-epsilon/), the [rounding modes](/rounding-modes/), and the tools of error analysis: the standard model, condition numbers, forward and backward error. It then shows how to compute more accurately than plain rounding allows, with [error-free transformations](/error-free-transformations/), [Kahan summation](/kahan-summation/) and double-double arithmetic, and how to convert between binary floats and decimal strings correctly.

**Chapter 12.2, Root finding**, solves $f(x) = 0$. It starts with the [bisection method](/bisection-method/), which is slow but cannot fail on a sign change, and Newton's method, which is fast near a root but can fail far from it. [Brent's method](/brent-method/) combines the two kinds of method. The chapter goes on to polynomial roots, where the [Wilkinson polynomial](/wilkinson-polynomial/) shows how ill conditioned roots can be, and to systems of nonlinear equations.

**Chapter 12.3, Linear systems**, solves $Ax = b$ in floating point. The direct methods factor $A$: LUP decomposition with [partial pivoting](/partial-pivoting/), the [Cholesky decomposition](/cholesky-decomposition/) for positive definite matrices, and QR for least squares. For large sparse systems the chapter turns to iterative methods, from [Jacobi](/jacobi-method/) and [Gauss-Seidel](/gauss-seidel-method/) to the [conjugate gradient method](/conjugate-gradient-method/), [GMRES](/gmres/), preconditioning and multigrid, and to orderings that keep a sparse factorization sparse.

**Chapter 12.4, Eigenproblems**, computes eigenvalues, eigenvectors and singular values. [Power iteration](/power-iteration/) finds one eigenvalue, [inverse iteration](/inverse-iteration/) refines it, and the [QR algorithm](/qr-algorithm/) finds all of them in $O(n^3)$ operations. For large sparse matrices the [Lanczos](/lanczos-algorithm/) and [Arnoldi](/arnoldi-iteration/) iterations find a few. Perturbation theorems say how accurate a computed eigenvalue can be.

**Chapter 12.5, Interpolation and approximation**, replaces a function by a simpler one. It covers [polynomial interpolation](/polynomial-interpolation/) in its Lagrange, Newton and [barycentric](/barycentric-interpolation/) forms, the [Runge phenomenon](/runge-phenomenon/) and the [Chebyshev nodes](/chebyshev-nodes/) that cure it, [cubic splines](/cubic-spline-interpolation/), interpolation in several dimensions, and best approximation in the maximum norm with the [Remez algorithm](/remez-algorithm/).

**Chapter 12.6, Quadrature**, computes integrals. It goes from the [trapezoidal rule](/trapezoidal-rule/) and [Simpson's rule](/simpsons-rule/) to [Gaussian quadrature](/gaussian-quadrature/), which is exact for polynomials of degree $2n - 1$ with $n$ points, and to [adaptive quadrature](/adaptive-quadrature/), which spends its function evaluations where the integrand is hard. For integrals in many dimensions it gives [Monte Carlo integration](/monte-carlo-integration/), whose error falls like $N^{-1/2}$ whatever the dimension, and quasi-Monte Carlo methods.

**Chapter 12.7, Differential equations**, is the longest of the volume. It solves initial value problems for ordinary differential equations with [Euler's method](/euler-method/), the [Runge-Kutta methods](/runge-kutta-methods/) and the linear multistep methods, and explains order, stability and [stiffness](/stiff-ode-problem/). It then turns to boundary value problems and to partial differential equations: [finite differences](/finite-difference-method/), finite elements and finite volumes, with the stability conditions, such as the [CFL condition](/courant-friedrichs-lewy-condition/), that decide whether a scheme converges.

**Chapter 12.8, Random number generation**, makes numbers that look random from a deterministic rule. It covers the [linear congruential generator](/linear-congruential-generator/) and its theory, the [Mersenne Twister](/mersenne-twister/), the xorshift family and PCG, generators for parallel work, cryptographically secure generators, and the tests that separate good generators from bad ones.

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course, read these entries in this order. From Chapter 12.1: [floating-point arithmetic](/floating-point-arithmetic/), [machine epsilon](/machine-epsilon/), the [standard model](/floating-point-model/), [catastrophic cancellation](/catastrophic-cancellation/), the [condition number](/condition-number/) and [backward error analysis](/backward-error-analysis/). From Chapter 12.2: the [bisection method](/bisection-method/), [Newton's method](/newton-raphson-method/) and the [secant method](/secant-method/). From Chapter 12.3: [LUP decomposition](/lup-decomposition/), the [Cholesky decomposition](/cholesky-decomposition/) and the [conjugate gradient method](/conjugate-gradient-method/). From Chapter 12.4: [power iteration](/power-iteration/) and the [QR algorithm](/qr-algorithm/). From Chapter 12.5: [polynomial interpolation](/polynomial-interpolation/) and [cubic splines](/cubic-spline-interpolation/). From Chapter 12.6: the [trapezoidal rule](/trapezoidal-rule/) and [Simpson's rule](/simpsons-rule/). From Chapter 12.7: [Euler's method](/euler-method/), the [classical Runge-Kutta method](/classical-runge-kutta-4/) and [stiff equations](/stiff-ode-problem/). From Chapter 12.8: the [linear congruential generator](/linear-congruential-generator/). Do the exercises marked ◦ and • at the end of each chapter you read. That is a complete first unit on numerical computing.

If you already know the basics, start with the opening page of each chapter. It names the groups of entries in the chapter and the order to read them. The entries marked *Advanced* and *Research* in each chapter are the place to go deeper. Chapter 12.1 is the base of the others, but you do not need all of it before you move on: the standard model, the condition number and backward error are enough for the rest of the volume.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained. Entries in other volumes are cited by number too, such as [Gaussian elimination](/gaussian-elimination/) and [Horner's rule](/horner-rule/) in Volume 11.

## What you need first

You should know calculus in one and several variables, including Taylor's theorem with a remainder, and the basic linear algebra of matrices, vectors, determinants and eigenvalues. You should be able to read a short program and follow a proof by induction. From Volume 1 you need [asymptotic notation](/asymptotic-notation/), and for Chapter 12.8 and the Monte Carlo entries the basic probability of [random variables](/random-variable/), [variance](/variance/) and the [law of large numbers](/law-of-large-numbers/). From Volume 11 you need [polynomials](/polynomial/), [Horner's rule](/horner-rule/), [Gaussian elimination](/gaussian-elimination/) and the [LU decomposition](/lu-decomposition/); the exact versions of the linear algebra are there, and this volume does them again in floating point. The [discrete Fourier transform](/discrete-fourier-transform/) of Volume 11 appears in trigonometric interpolation and spectral methods. Chapter 12.8 uses [congruences](/congruence/) and [primitive roots](/primitive-root/) from Volume 10.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
