## History

Eigenvalue problems came from mechanics long before they had a name. Jacobi computed the secular perturbations of planetary orbits in 1846 by rotating a symmetric matrix toward diagonal form, by hand, and Rayleigh used the quotient that bears his name in his work on vibrations in the 1870s. The theory of the early twentieth century gave the facts the algorithms rely on: Perron (1907) and Frobenius (1908 to 1912) on nonnegative matrices, Schur (1909) on unitary triangularization, and Gerschgorin (1931) on discs that contain the eigenvalues. Von Mises and Pollaczek-Geiringer described power iteration in 1929, Hessenberg introduced his form in 1940, and Wielandt described inverse iteration in 1944.

The first computers made the old methods look slow. Jacobi's method was rediscovered around 1950, and Lanczos (1950) and Arnoldi (1951) proposed reductions to tridiagonal and Hessenberg form through Krylov vectors, which lost orthogonality in floating point. Householder's reflectors of 1958 gave a stable finite reduction instead. Rutishauser turned his quotient-difference algorithm into the LR iteration in 1958, and in 1961 John Francis in England and Vera Kublanovskaya in the Soviet Union independently replaced LR by QR. Francis's papers already contain the Hessenberg reduction, shifts, deflation and the implicit double shift, and the method has been the standard dense eigenvalue solver ever since.

Wilkinson turned these methods into a subject. His book *The Algebraic Eigenvalue Problem* (1965) gave the backward error analysis of the reductions and iterations and put eigenvalue condition numbers at the centre, and in 1968 he proved that the symmetric tridiagonal QR algorithm with his shift always converges. Golub and Kahan showed in 1965 how to reach bidiagonal form without forming $A^TA$, Golub and Reinsch gave the SVD iteration in 1970, and Moler and Stewart extended QR to matrix pencils as the QZ algorithm in 1973. The Algol procedures of the Handbook for Automatic Computation became the EISPACK library of the early 1970s and then LAPACK.

The later work went in two directions. For large sparse matrices, Paige's 1971 thesis showed that Lanczos loses orthogonality exactly as its Ritz values converge, which made it a practical iterative method, and Sorensen's implicit restart of 1992 led to the ARPACK library behind `eigs` in several systems. For dense symmetric and bidiagonal problems, attention moved to speed and relative accuracy: Cuppen's divide and conquer (1981), stabilized by Gu and Eisenstat in 1995; Demmel and Kahan's 1990 result that a bidiagonal matrix determines its singular values to high relative accuracy; the dqds algorithm of Fernando and Parlett (1994); and Dhillon and Parlett's MRRR algorithm, which computes $n$ eigenvectors in $O(n^2)$ time.

## Further reading

- Gene H. Golub and Charles F. Van Loan, *Matrix Computations*, fourth edition, Johns Hopkins University Press, 2013. Chapters 7 and 8 cover the nonsymmetric and symmetric problems with every algorithm of the dense part of the chapter.
- Lloyd N. Trefethen and David Bau III, *Numerical Linear Algebra*, SIAM, 1997. The shortest good introduction to the QR algorithm, Arnoldi and Lanczos.
- Beresford N. Parlett, *The Symmetric Eigenvalue Problem*, SIAM Classics in Applied Mathematics 20, 1998. The symmetric case in depth, including Rayleigh quotient iteration and Lanczos in floating point.
- J. H. Wilkinson, *The Algebraic Eigenvalue Problem*, Oxford University Press, 1965. The founding text of the error analysis, still worth reading for its examples.
- Yousef Saad, *Numerical Methods for Large Eigenvalue Problems*, revised edition, SIAM Classics in Applied Mathematics 66, 2011. Krylov methods, restarts and preconditioned eigensolvers.
