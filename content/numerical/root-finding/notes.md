## History

Root finding is older than algebra as a subject. The rule of false position, which solves a linear problem from two trial values, appears in the Chinese *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art* and in Fibonacci's *Liber Abaci* of 1202. Scipione del Ferro and Niccolò Tartaglia solved the cubic, and Girolamo Cardano published the solution in the *Ars Magna* of 1545, together with Lodovico Ferrari's reduction of the quartic. The search for a formula for the quintic ended with Ruffini's incomplete proof of 1799 and Abel's complete one of 1824, and Galois then explained which equations can be solved by radicals.

Newton described his method around 1669 for the cubic $x^3 - 2x - 5 = 0$, working with a sequence of polynomials rather than derivatives. Raphson published a simpler form in 1690, and Simpson gave the general form with fluxions in 1740, including systems of two equations. Halley published his third-order formulas in 1694. Daniel Bernoulli observed around 1728 that the ratios of a linear recurrence approach the largest root of its characteristic equation, and Dandelin, Lobachevsky and Gräffe found root squaring between 1826 and 1837. Laguerre gave his method for polynomials with real roots in 1880.

The modern theory came in the twentieth century. Banach proved his fixed-point theorem in 1922, Kantorovich proved convergence of Newton's method from data at the start in 1948, and Ostrowski analysed the order of the secant method and introduced the efficiency index. Traub's book of 1964 organized one-point and multipoint iterations by order. Wilkinson, testing root finders on the Pilot ACE in the 1950s, found the polynomial with roots $1, \ldots, 20$ whose roots are wildly sensitive to its coefficients. Muller published his parabola method in 1956, Broyden his quasi-Newton update in 1965, and Ehrlich (1967) and Aberth (1973) the simultaneous iteration now used for high-degree polynomials.

The safe methods for one equation came later. Dekker's 1969 algorithm combined bisection with secant steps; Brent added inverse quadratic interpolation and a forced-bisection test in 1971, published the procedure in his 1973 book, and Forsythe, Malcolm and Moler spread it as ZEROIN. Jenkins and Traub published their three-stage polynomial method in 1970, and its programs CPOLY and RPOLY entered the main numerical libraries. Today MATLAB's `roots` computes the eigenvalues of the companion matrix, and MPSolve, by Bini and Fiorentino, uses the Aberth iteration for polynomials of very high degree.

## Further reading

- J. E. Dennis Jr. and Robert B. Schnabel, *Numerical Methods for Unconstrained Optimization and Nonlinear Equations*, SIAM Classics in Applied Mathematics (1996). Newton's method in one and several variables, with line searches, secant updates and careful convergence proofs.
- Richard P. Brent, *Algorithms for Minimization without Derivatives* (Prentice-Hall, 1973; Dover reprint, 2002). The source of Brent's method, with its proof and code.
- J. F. Traub, *Iterative Methods for the Solution of Equations* (Prentice-Hall, 1964). The classical theory of the order of one-point and multipoint iterations.
- J. M. Ortega and W. C. Rheinboldt, *Iterative Solution of Nonlinear Equations in Several Variables*, SIAM Classics in Applied Mathematics 30 (2000). The reference for convergence theorems for systems, including Kantorovich's.
- Nicholas J. Higham, *Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms*, second edition (SIAM, 2002). Its chapters on polynomials and nonlinear systems explain how accurately a root can be computed at all.
