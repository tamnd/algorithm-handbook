## History

Quadrature is older than calculus. Babylonian astronomers computed the distance travelled by Jupiter as the area of a trapezoid under a time-velocity graph, as Ossendrijver showed in 2016. Kepler used the weights $1, 4, 1$ for the volume of wine barrels in 1615, and Thomas Simpson published the rule now named after him in 1743. Newton described interpolation through equally spaced points in a letter of 1676 and gave the three-eighths rule; Roger Cotes computed the weights of the closed rules up to 11 points, published after his death in *Harmonia mensurarum* (1722). Euler found the summation formula around 1732 and published it in 1738; Maclaurin published it independently in 1742.

Gauss presented his rules in 1814, derived through continued fractions, and computed them for up to seven points. Jacobi proved them by orthogonality in 1826 and identified the nodes as the zeros of the Legendre polynomials, and Christoffel extended the construction to general weight functions in 1858. Rules for other weights and with prescribed nodes followed: Lobatto described rules with both endpoints as nodes in 1852, Hermite and Laguerre studied their orthogonal polynomials in 1864 and 1879, and Radau treated rules with one fixed endpoint in 1880. Peano wrote the error of any rule as an integral against a derivative in 1913. In 1933 Pólya showed that the closed Newton-Cotes rules need not converge, and Fejér gave interpolatory rules on Chebyshev points with positive weights. Richardson's extrapolation (1911, 1927) and Filon's rule for trigonometric integrals (1928) date from the same years.

Computers changed the questions. Ulam, von Neumann and Metropolis developed the Monte Carlo method at Los Alamos in 1946 and 1947, and Metropolis and Ulam published the first account in 1949. Romberg published his extrapolation of the trapezoidal rule in 1955, Clenshaw and Curtis their Chebyshev rule in 1960, and Kuncir and McKeeman the first recursive adaptive Simpson routines in 1962. Kronrod extended the Gauss rules with reused nodes in 1964, and Golub and Welsch reduced the computation of Gauss rules to a symmetric tridiagonal eigenvalue problem in 1969. Takahasi and Mori introduced the double exponential transformation in 1974. QUADPACK, by Piessens, de Doncker-Kapenga, Überhuber and Kahaner (1983), made globally adaptive Gauss-Kronrod quadrature the integrator inside most libraries.

The theory for many dimensions grew from number theory. Weyl founded the theory of uniform distribution in 1916, Koksma proved his inequality in one dimension in 1942, and Hlawka extended it to $s$ dimensions in 1961. Van der Corput (1935), Halton (1960) and Sobol (1967) constructed low-discrepancy sequences, and Smolyak introduced sparse grids in 1963. Niederreiter's monograph of 1992 became the standard reference for quasi-Monte Carlo methods, and Paskov and Traub's 1995 report on a 360-dimensional finance problem brought them into practice. Recent work has revised old judgements: Trefethen (2008) showed that Clenshaw-Curtis is nearly as accurate as Gauss for most integrands, fast asymptotic methods since 2007 compute Gauss rules with millions of nodes, and Levin (1982) and Iserles and Nørsett (2005) built oscillatory methods that improve as the frequency grows.

## Further reading

- Philip J. Davis and Philip Rabinowitz, *Methods of Numerical Integration*, second edition (Academic Press, 1984). The classical reference for one-dimensional rules, their error terms and automatic integration.
- Walter Gautschi, *Orthogonal Polynomials: Computation and Approximation* (Oxford University Press, 2004). How to compute recurrence coefficients and Gauss-type rules for any weight, including Radau, Lobatto and Kronrod rules.
- Lloyd N. Trefethen, *Approximation Theory and Approximation Practice*, extended edition (SIAM, 2019). Explains why Clenshaw-Curtis and Gauss quadrature converge as they do.
- Lloyd N. Trefethen and J. A. C. Weideman, "The exponentially convergent trapezoidal rule", *SIAM Review* 56 (2014). The trapezoidal rule for periodic integrands and on the real line, and the transformations built on it.
- Robert Piessens, Elise de Doncker-Kapenga, Christoph W. Überhuber and David K. Kahaner, *QUADPACK: A Subroutine Package for Automatic Integration* (Springer, 1983). The design of the adaptive Gauss-Kronrod codes that most libraries still use.
- Josef Dick, Frances Y. Kuo and Ian H. Sloan, "High-dimensional integration: the quasi-Monte Carlo way", *Acta Numerica* 22 (2013). Lattice rules, digital nets and the weighted spaces in which they work.
