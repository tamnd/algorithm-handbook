A carbon fibre composite has fibres about ten micrometres across, and an aircraft panel made of it is a metre long. A finite element model that resolves every fibre needs at least $(L/\varepsilon)^3 = (10^5)^3$ cells, far beyond any computer. Yet the panel's stiffness depends on how the fibres are arranged. The same gap appears in time. An atom on a metal surface vibrates about $10^{13}$ times a second, so [molecular dynamics](/molecular-dynamics/) takes steps of a femtosecond; but with a barrier of 0.5 eV at room temperature it hops only about $4 \times 10^4$ times a second, so one hop costs about $2.5 \times 10^{10}$ steps.

Multiscale methods close these gaps by coupling a fine model, accurate and expensive, to a coarse one, cheap but missing data. Sometimes the coarse model can be derived once: periodic homogenization computes an effective coefficient from one cell, and harmonic transition state theory computes a hopping rate from a saddle point. Sometimes the coupling must stay live, with the fine model run only where a defect sits or only for short bursts. Each method in the chapter makes a specific approximation, and the entries say which error terms it has and under which assumptions the results hold.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in nine groups, read in order.

1. **Scales and coupling atoms with continua.** [Multiscale modeling](/multiscale-modeling/) sets out sequential and concurrent coupling. The [Cauchy-Born rule](/cauchy-born-rule/) derives a continuum energy from atoms; the [quasicontinuum method](/quasicontinuum-method/) uses it away from defects and shows where ghost forces come from; the [QM/MM method](/qm-mm-method/) embeds a quantum region in a force field.
2. **Homogenization.** [Periodic homogenization](/periodic-homogenization/) gives the effective coefficient from a cell problem, with an $O(\varepsilon)$ error in $L^2$. [Two-scale convergence](/two-scale-convergence/) justifies the limit, the [representative volume element](/representative-volume-element/) handles random materials, and the [FE² method](/fe2-method/) nests a cell problem in every macroscopic integration point.
3. **Multiscale finite elements.** The [multiscale finite element method](/multiscale-finite-element-method/) and [localized orthogonal decomposition](/localized-orthogonal-decomposition/) build coarse bases from fine solves; the [variational multiscale method](/variational-multiscale-method/) models the unresolved scales.
4. **Macroscale solvers with microscale data.** The [heterogeneous multiscale method](/heterogeneous-multiscale-method/) estimates missing fluxes from micro problems, and [coarse projective integration](/coarse-projective-integration/) takes large steps from short fine runs.
5. **Coarse-graining.** The [Mori-Zwanzig formalism](/mori-zwanzig-formalism/) gives the exact reduced equation, and [force matching](/force-matching-coarse-graining/), [iterative Boltzmann inversion](/iterative-boltzmann-inversion/) and [dissipative particle dynamics](/dissipative-particle-dynamics/) build practical bead models.
6. **Mesoscale continuum models.** The [phase-field model](/phase-field-model/) for moving interfaces and [peridynamics](/peridynamics/) for cracks.
7. **Saddles and rates.** The [nudged elastic band](/nudged-elastic-band/), the [string method](/string-method/) and the [dimer method](/dimer-method/) find saddles; [harmonic transition state theory](/harmonic-transition-state-theory/) turns them into rates, and [kinetic Monte Carlo](/kinetic-monte-carlo/) runs a system on those rates.
8. **Sampling rare transitions.** [Transition path sampling](/transition-path-sampling/) and [forward flux sampling](/forward-flux-sampling/) estimate rates from the dynamics when there is no single saddle.
9. **Accelerated molecular dynamics.** [Hyperdynamics](/hyperdynamics/), [parallel replica dynamics](/parallel-replica-dynamics/) and [temperature-accelerated dynamics](/temperature-accelerated-dynamics/) reach rare events sooner, each under its own assumption.

## A first reading

Start with multiscale modeling for the vocabulary. Then read periodic homogenization and the representative volume element, and work their one-dimensional examples, where the effective coefficient is a harmonic mean. Read the multiscale finite element method and the heterogeneous multiscale method next, to see two ways of using a fine solve inside a coarse one. On the atomistic side, read the Cauchy-Born rule and the quasicontinuum method, then the nudged elastic band, harmonic transition state theory and kinetic Monte Carlo, which together take a system from a potential to a long simulated trajectory. End with parallel replica dynamics as the simplest accelerated method.

That is ten entries. Two-scale convergence, the Mori-Zwanzig formalism and localized orthogonal decomposition carry the theory and can wait. The group on sampling rare transitions and the coarse-graining fits are for readers who work on soft matter or chemistry.

## Before you start

The continuum side assumes the [finite element method](/finite-element-method/) and the [Galerkin method](/galerkin-method/) of Volume 12, with the [weak formulation](/weak-formulation/) and [Sobolev spaces](/sobolev-space/) of Chapter 43.5. The atomistic side assumes [molecular dynamics](/molecular-dynamics/), [Langevin dynamics](/langevin-dynamics/), the [canonical ensemble](/canonical-ensemble/) and [interatomic potentials](/interatomic-potential/) from Chapter 43.2. The rate methods use [Markov chains](/markov-chain/) and [inverse transform sampling](/inverse-transform-sampling/) from Volume 17, and [energy landscapes](/energy-landscape/) from Volume 27. The [deformation gradient](/deformation-gradient/) of Volume 28 appears in the Cauchy-Born rule.
