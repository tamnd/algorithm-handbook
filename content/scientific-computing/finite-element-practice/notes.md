## History

The finite element method began as structural engineering. In 1956 Turner, Clough, Martin and Topp described the direct stiffness method for aircraft structures at Boeing: compute a stiffness matrix for each small piece and add them into a global matrix node by node, which is assembly as it is still done. Clough gave the method its name in 1960. Engineers soon added isoparametric elements, the patch test and frontal solvers, and Newmark's 1959 time stepper for structural dynamics became the standard for the semi-discrete equations.

The mathematics came in the 1960s and early 1970s. Lax and Milgram had proved their theorem in 1954, and Céa's thesis of 1964 gave the quasi-optimality of Galerkin approximations. Bramble and Hilbert proved their lemma on polynomial approximation in 1970, which turned Céa's bound into rates in $h$. Babuška in 1971 and Brezzi in 1974 gave the inf-sup theory of saddle point problems, which explained why some pairs of velocity and pressure spaces work and others do not. Raviart and Thomas published their $H(\mathrm{div})$ elements in 1977 and Nédélec his $H(\mathrm{curl})$ elements in 1980. Nitsche proposed weak Dirichlet conditions in 1971, and Arnold's interior penalty method of 1982 applied the same terms on every face.

Stabilization and error control followed. Hughes and Brooks developed streamline upwind Petrov-Galerkin around 1980, and Johnson, Nävert and Pitkäranta analyzed streamline diffusion in 1984. Babuška and Rheinboldt began a posteriori error estimation in 1978, and Zienkiewicz and Zhu gave their recovery estimator in 1987. A proof that the adaptive loop converges came only with Dörfler's marking rule of 1996. Binev, Dahmen and DeVore in 2004, Stevenson in 2007 and Cascon, Kreuzer, Nochetto and Siebert in 2008 then proved optimal rates. Newest vertex bisection, from Sewell's thesis of 1972 and Mitchell's work of 1989, is the refinement used in these proofs.

Degree and geometry were the last directions. Babuška and coworkers developed the p and hp versions in the 1980s; Guo and Babuška proved exponential convergence on geometric meshes in 2D in 1986. Patera introduced the spectral element method for fluid dynamics in 1984. The boundary element method grew from the integral equation work of Jaswon and Symm in 1963 and Rizzo in 1967. In structural mechanics, Simo and Taylor gave the consistent tangent of return mapping in 1985, and Riks and Crisfield made arc-length continuation practical around 1980. Belytschko and coworkers introduced the element-free Galerkin method in 1994 and the extended finite element method in 1999. Beirão da Veiga, Brezzi, Cangiani, Manzini, Marini and Russo introduced the virtual element method in 2013, and Cockburn, Gopalakrishnan and Lazarov introduced the hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin method in 2009.

## Further reading

- Susanne C. Brenner and L. Ridgway Scott, *The Mathematical Theory of Finite Element Methods* (3rd edition, Springer, 2008). The standard text for Sobolev spaces, Bramble-Hilbert, error estimates and mixed methods at a graduate level.
- Philippe G. Ciarlet, *The Finite Element Method for Elliptic Problems* (North-Holland, 1978; SIAM Classics in Applied Mathematics, 2002). The source of the definition of an element and of the interpolation theory.
- Daniele Boffi, Franco Brezzi and Michel Fortin, *Mixed Finite Element Methods and Applications* (Springer, 2013). The reference for the inf-sup condition, Taylor-Hood, Raviart-Thomas and Nédélec elements.
- Thomas J. R. Hughes, *The Finite Element Method: Linear Static and Dynamic Finite Element Analysis* (Prentice-Hall, 1987; Dover, 2000). The engineering side: isoparametric elements, the patch test, Newmark and SUPG.
- Alexandre Ern and Jean-Luc Guermond, *Theory and Practice of Finite Elements* (Springer, 2004). Theory and implementation together, with good chapters on stabilization and on discontinuous methods.
- Mark Ainsworth and J. Tinsley Oden, *A Posteriori Error Estimation in Finite Element Analysis* (Wiley, 2000). Residual, recovery and goal-oriented estimators in one place.
