## History

Molecular dynamics began with Berni Alder and Thomas Wainwright, who simulated hard spheres in 1957. Aneesur Rahman simulated liquid argon with a Lennard-Jones potential in 1964, and Loup Verlet used the integrator that bears his name, together with a neighbour list, in 1967. William Swope, Hans Andersen and coworkers gave the velocity form in 1982. Andrew McCammon, Bruce Gelin and Martin Karplus simulated a small protein for a few picoseconds in 1977. Special-purpose hardware later reached the millisecond for small proteins, and Karplus, Michael Levitt and Arieh Warshel shared the 2013 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for multiscale models of complex chemical systems.

The control of temperature and pressure came around 1980. Andersen gave stochastic collisions and a barostat in 1980, Michele Parrinello and Rahman let the box change shape in 1980 and 1981, and Herman Berendsen and coworkers gave weak coupling in 1984. Shuichi Nosé and William Hoover gave the deterministic thermostat in 1984 and 1985. Hoover showed that one harmonic oscillator is not ergodic under it, and Glenn Martyna, Michael Klein and Mark Tuckerman added chains in 1992. Giovanni Bussi, Davide Donadio and Parrinello gave stochastic velocity rescaling in 2007, and Benedict Leimkuhler and Charles Matthews gave the BAOAB splitting of Langevin dynamics in 2013. Melville Green in 1954 and Ryogo Kubo in 1957 gave the integrals that turn equilibrium correlations into transport coefficients.

The integrators and the forces grew together. Jean-Paul Ryckaert, Giovanni Ciccotti and Berendsen gave SHAKE in 1977, Andersen gave RATTLE in 1983, and Berk Hess and coworkers gave LINCS in 1997. Tuckerman, Bruce Berne and Martyna gave RESPA in 1992, and the work of Giancarlo Benettin, Antonio Giorgilli and Sebastian Reich in the 1990s explained the long-time energy behaviour of these methods through the shadow Hamiltonian. Paul Ewald's lattice sum of 1921 entered simulation through mesh methods: Tom Darden, Darrin York and Lee Pedersen gave particle mesh Ewald in 1993, and Ulrich Essmann and coworkers the smooth form in 1995. Murray Daw and Michael Baskes gave the embedded atom method in 1984. Roberto Car and Parrinello joined density functional theory to molecular dynamics in 1985, and Jörg Behler and Parrinello fitted neural network potentials to such calculations in 2007.

Free energy methods start from John Kirkwood's integration over a coupling parameter in 1935 and Robert Zwanzig's perturbation formula in 1954. Charles Bennett gave the acceptance ratio in 1976, and Glenn Torrie and John Valleau gave umbrella sampling in 1977. Shankar Kumar and coworkers gave WHAM in 1992, Alessandro Laio and Parrinello gave metadynamics in 2002, and Alessandro Barducci, Bussi and Parrinello gave its well-tempered form in 2008. Christopher Jarzynski related nonequilibrium work to free energy in 1997, and Gavin Crooks extended the relation in 1998 and 1999.

## Further reading

- Daan Frenkel and Berend Smit, Understanding Molecular Simulation: From Algorithms to Applications, 2nd edition, Academic Press, 2002. The standard text on molecular dynamics, Monte Carlo and free energy methods, with careful derivations.
- Michael P. Allen and Dominic J. Tildesley, Computer Simulation of Liquids, 2nd edition, Oxford University Press, 2017. The practical reference on neighbour lists, long-range forces, thermostats and the analysis of runs.
- Mark E. Tuckerman, Statistical Mechanics: Theory and Molecular Simulation, Oxford University Press, 2010. Ensembles, extended-system thermostats, RESPA and free energy methods, built on the theory of each.
- Benedict Leimkuhler and Charles Matthews, Molecular Dynamics: With Deterministic and Stochastic Numerical Methods, Springer, 2015. The numerical analysis of the integrators, from the shadow Hamiltonian to the splittings of Langevin dynamics.
- Dominik Marx and Jürg Hutter, Ab Initio Molecular Dynamics: Basic Theory and Advanced Methods, Cambridge University Press, 2009. Born-Oppenheimer and Car-Parrinello dynamics with the electronic structure they need.
