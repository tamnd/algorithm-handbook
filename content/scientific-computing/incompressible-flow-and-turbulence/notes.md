## History

The mathematics of the pressure is older than the computers. Helmholtz split a velocity field into a potential part and a vortex part in 1858, and Leray used the projection onto divergence-free fields in his work on the Navier-Stokes equations in the 1930s. The numerical methods began at Los Alamos, where Harlow and Welch (1965) put the pressure at cell centers and the velocities on the faces of a staggered grid. Chorin added an artificial time derivative of the pressure for steady flow in 1967 and published the projection method in 1968; Temam proved the convergence of the same fractional step idea in 1969. In finite volume codes, Patankar and Spalding (1972) introduced the pressure correction iteration SIMPLE, which Patankar's 1980 textbook made standard. Rhie and Chow (1983) made collocated grids usable, and Issa (1986) gave the non-iterative PISO scheme for time-dependent flow.

Interfaces and immersed bodies came next. Noh and Woodward (1976) used cell volume fractions with interfaces aligned to the grid, Hirt and Nichols (1981) named the volume of fluid method, and Youngs (1982) put a sloped line in each mixed cell, the start of PLIC. Brackbill, Kothe and Zemach (1992) turned surface tension into a body force. Peskin (1972) computed blood flow past heart valves with elastic boundary points on a fixed grid. Unverdi and Tryggvason (1992) used the same smoothed delta functions to move a connected front between two fluids.

Turbulence theory runs from Boussinesq's eddy viscosity (1877) and Reynolds' averaging (1895) through Richardson's cascade (1922) and Prandtl's mixing length (1925) to Kolmogorov's similarity hypotheses of 1941. Kolmogorov also proposed an equation for a turbulence frequency in 1942. The two-equation models took their modern form with Jones and Launder (1972) and Launder and Sharma (1974) for k-epsilon, and with Wilcox (1988) for k-omega. The aerodynamic models followed: Spalart and Allmaras (1992 and 1994) and Menter's SST model (1994). Smagorinsky used his subgrid viscosity in a weather model in 1963, Lilly tied its constant to the Kolmogorov spectrum, and Deardorff (1970) ran the first LES of channel flow. Germano, Piomelli, Moin and Cabot made the coefficient dynamic in 1991. Orszag and Patterson (1972) computed isotropic turbulence on a $32^3$ grid, and the channel flow DNS of Kim, Moin and Moser (1987) became a standard reference for models.

The particle and kinetic methods have other roots. Bhatnagar, Gross and Krook proposed their collision model in 1954. Lattice gas automata (Frisch, Hasslacher and Pomeau, 1986) led to the lattice Boltzmann equation of McNamara and Zanetti (1988). The BGK form on D2Q9 came from Qian, d'Humières and Lallemand and from Chen, Chen and Matthaeus in 1992, and d'Humières proposed multiple relaxation times the same year. Lucy and Gingold and Monaghan introduced SPH independently in 1977 to model stars. Cummins and Rudman (1999) brought Chorin's projection to SPH.

## Further reading

- Joel H. Ferziger, Milovan Perić and Robert L. Street, *Computational Methods for Fluid Dynamics*, 4th edition, Springer, 2020. The standard text on finite volume methods for incompressible flow, with SIMPLE, PISO, collocated grids, interfaces and turbulence.
- Stephen B. Pope, *Turbulent Flows*, Cambridge University Press, 2000. The Kolmogorov theory, the wall laws, RANS closures and LES in one consistent notation, with careful comparison against measurements.
- David C. Wilcox, *Turbulence Modeling for CFD*, 3rd edition, DCW Industries, 2006. How the RANS models are built and calibrated, constant by constant, by the author of the k-omega model.
- Ruben Scardovelli and Stéphane Zaleski, Direct numerical simulation of free-surface and interfacial flow, *Annual Review of Fluid Mechanics* 31 (1999). A survey of volume of fluid, front tracking and surface tension models.
- Timm Krüger and co-authors, *The Lattice Boltzmann Method: Principles and Practice*, Springer, 2017. A practical book on lattices, collision operators, boundary conditions and the Chapman-Enskog analysis.
- J. J. Monaghan, Smoothed particle hydrodynamics, *Reports on Progress in Physics* 68 (2005). A survey by one of the founders: the derivation from a Lagrangian, conservation, variable smoothing lengths and applications.
