## History

The scheduling results came before the machines. In 1966 Ronald Graham showed in the *Bell System Technical Journal* that any list schedule of tasks with precedence constraints on $m$ identical machines is within a factor $2 - 1/m$ of the best one. His 1969 paper in the *SIAM Journal on Applied Mathematics* developed the bound further. Richard Brent's paper on evaluating arithmetic expressions in parallel (*Journal of the ACM*, 1974) proved, as a lemma, that a computation of $q$ operations and depth $t$ runs on $p$ processors in $t + (q - t)/p$ steps. Brent did not call it a theorem; the name came later, from the textbooks. Jeffrey Ullman showed in 1975 that finding an optimal schedule is NP-hard, so the factor of two is the best that a simple rule can promise in general.

The PRAM was set down in 1978 by Steven Fortune and James Wyllie, whose P-RAM let processors read the same cell at once but not write it, and whose polynomial time turned out to equal polynomial space. The concurrent-write variants followed in the work of Leslie Goldschlager, Yossi Shiloach and Uzi Vishkin around 1980. Shiloach and Vishkin found the maximum in $O(\log \log n)$ steps. Vishkin showed in 1983 that an EREW PRAM can simulate a CRCW PRAM with a logarithmic slowdown. The lower bounds came in the 1980s. Stephen Cook, Cynthia Dwork and Rüdiger Reischuk proved in 1986 that a CREW PRAM needs $\Omega(\log n)$ steps for the OR of $n$ bits, which the common CRCW PRAM computes in one step. Paul Beame and Johan Håstad proved in 1989 that parity needs $\Omega(\log n/\log \log n)$ steps on a CRCW PRAM with polynomially many processors. Joseph JáJá's textbook of 1992 collected the PRAM algorithms of the decade and stated them in the work-time form.

Gene Amdahl's argument of 1967 was a three-page paper at the Spring Joint Computer Conference, and it contained no formula at all. It argued from the overhead of the machines of the day that parallel hardware would gain only a small factor over a fast single processor. The formula now called Amdahl's law was read into the paper later. John Gustafson's reply came in the *Communications of the ACM* in 1988, from work at Sandia National Laboratories, where programs whose size grew with the machine reached scaled speedups above 1000 on a 1024-processor hypercube.

Multithreaded scheduling grew out of functional programming. Warren Burton and Ronan Sleep described work stealing in 1981, and Robert Halstead used it in Multilisp in 1984. Robert Blumofe and Charles Leiserson proved the bounds for randomized work stealing at FOCS in 1994, with the journal version in the *Journal of the ACM* in 1999. The Cilk system (1995) put those bounds into a working language. Cilk-5 (1998) added the work-first principle and the THE protocol for the deques. In the same year Nimar Arora, Robert Blumofe and Greg Plaxton gave a non-blocking deque and a proof for programs that share the machine with others. Mingdong Feng and Leiserson's SP-bags algorithm (1997) gave the first efficient race detector for such programs. The third edition of Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest and Stein (2009) brought the work-span model into the standard algorithms course.

## Further reading

- Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest and Clifford Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, fourth edition (2022). Chapter 26 sets out work, span, greedy scheduling and races with the same conventions as most entries of this chapter.
- Joseph JáJá, *An Introduction to Parallel Algorithms* (1992). The standard account of the PRAM, its variants and the work-time framework.
- Guy E. Blelloch, *Programming Parallel Algorithms*, Communications of the ACM 39(3), 1996. A short case for analysing parallel programs by work and depth rather than by processors.
- Robert D. Blumofe and Charles E. Leiserson, *Scheduling Multithreaded Computations by Work Stealing*, Journal of the ACM 46(5), 1999. The proofs of the time, space and communication bounds of work stealing.
- Ananth Grama, Anshul Gupta, George Karypis and Vipin Kumar, *Introduction to Parallel Computing*, second edition (2003). Chapter 5 covers speedup, efficiency, cost and isoefficiency on real machines.
