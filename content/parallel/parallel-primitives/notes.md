## History

The scan is older than parallel computers, because the carries of an adder are a prefix computation. Sklansky's conditional-sum adder (1960) is the circuit now named after him, and Blelloch's notes credit Ofman (1963) with the first parallel prefix circuit, also for binary addition. Kogge and Stone (1973) solved recurrences by recursive doubling. Ladner and Fischer (1980) stated the problem for any [associative operator](/associative-operator/), called it parallel prefix computation, and gave the circuits $P_k(n)$, which reach the least depth with at most $4n$ gates. Brent and Kung (1982) designed VLSI adders around a prefix circuit of size $2n - 2 - \lg n$. Fich (1983) proved sharper size bounds at the least depth, and Snir (1986) proved $s + d \ge 2n - 2$ for every prefix circuit.

Fortune and Wyllie (1978) defined the PRAM, and Wyllie's thesis (1979) used [pointer jumping](/pointer-jumping/) to rank lists. Brent (1974) showed that every expression can be evaluated in $O(\log n)$ time, in the paper that also gives the scheduling bound now called [Brent's theorem](/brent-theorem/). Miller and Reif (1985) introduced [tree contraction](/tree-contraction/) and the random mate rule. Cole and Vishkin (1986) replaced the coins by [deterministic coin tossing](/cole-vishkin/), and Tarjan and Vishkin (1985) gave the Euler tour technique, which reduces many tree problems to [list ranking](/list-ranking/). Cole and Vishkin and, independently, Anderson and Miller (both 1988) ranked lists deterministically in $O(\log n)$ time with $n/\log n$ processors, and Anderson and Miller (1990) gave a simple randomized algorithm with the same bounds. The shunt of Abrahamson, Dadoun, Kirkpatrick and Przytycka (1989) made expression evaluation deterministic on the EREW PRAM with $O(n)$ work.

Valiant (1975) merged in $O(\log \log n)$ rounds in a comparison model, and Borodin and Hopcroft (1985) showed that this is tight there. Shiloach and Vishkin (1981) and Kruskal (1983) gave PRAM merges, and Merge Path (Odeh, Green, Mwassi, Shmueli and Birk, 2012) is the co-rank method in its modern form. For matrices, Strassen (1969) found the seven-product scheme, and Cannon (1969) gave an early mesh algorithm. Frigo, Leiserson, Prokop and Ramachandran (1999) showed that the recursive orders of the transpose and the product use the cache optimally. CLRS, 3rd edition (2009), Chapter 27, put P-MERGE and the multithreaded matrix products in textbook form.

Hillis and Steele (1986) described the data-parallel style for the Connection Machine, with the scan of $\lceil \lg n \rceil$ rounds as a basic operation. Schwartz (1980), and independently Mago (1979), first suggested segmented scans. Blelloch (1989) argued that scans should be primitive operations of a machine, and his book of 1990 built a whole programming model on them; the flattening of nested data parallelism (Blelloch and Sabot 1990) led to NESL. The words fork and join go back to Conway (1963). Cilk-5 (Frigo, Leiserson and Randall 1998) and Java's fork/join framework (Lea 2000) made the fork-join model practical, and Cilk++ added reducers (Frigo, Halpern, Leiserson and Lewin-Berlin 2009). On GPUs, Sengupta, Harris, Zhang and Owens (2007) brought the segmented scan to graphics hardware, and Merrill and Garland (2016) gave the single-pass scan with decoupled look-back.

## Further reading

- Joseph JaJa, *An Introduction to Parallel Algorithms* (1992). The PRAM textbook, with prefix sums, pointer jumping, list ranking, tree contraction and merging.
- Guy E. Blelloch, *Prefix Sums and Their Applications*, CMU-CS-90-190 (1990). A short report with the Blelloch scan, segmented scans and many applications, from radix sort to line drawing.
- Guy E. Blelloch, *Vector Models for Data-Parallel Computing* (1990). The scan vector model and the flattening of nested parallelism.
- Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest and Clifford Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, 3rd edition (2009), Chapter 27. Fork-join programs with work and span, parallel loops, P-MERGE and the matrix products.
- Michael McCool, Arch D. Robison and James Reinders, *Structured Parallel Programming* (2012). Map, reduce, scan, pack, scatter and gather as patterns, with code.
- David B. Kirk, Wen-mei W. Hwu and Izzat El Hajj, *Programming Massively Parallel Processors*, 4th edition (2022). Reduction, scan, histogram and merge on GPUs.
