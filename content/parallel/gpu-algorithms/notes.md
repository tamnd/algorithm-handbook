## History

Erik Lindholm, John Nickolls, Stuart Oberman and John Montrym described NVIDIA's Tesla architecture in *IEEE Micro* in 2008, with the SIMT instruction unit that runs threads in warps of 32. In the same year John Nickolls, Ian Buck, Michael Garland and Kevin Skadron set out the CUDA model of threads, blocks, grids and shared memory in *ACM Queue*. Later generations kept the model and changed its details. Compute capability 3.5 added several hardware queues, so that the overlap of streams depends much less on the order in which the host issues work. Volta, compute capability 7.0, gave each thread its own program counter and brought the first tensor cores.

The performance models are older than the GPUs they now describe. John Little proved his queueing formula in 1961, and latency hiding is that formula applied to a pipeline. Samuel Williams, Andrew Waterman and David Patterson introduced the roofline model in 2009 for multicore processors, and it became the usual first estimate for GPU kernels. In his GTC talk of 2010, Vasily Volkov showed with measurements that a kernel at low occupancy can beat one at high occupancy when each thread has independent work and more registers.

The basic kernels came next. In 2007 Mark Harris, Shubhabrata Sengupta and John Owens gave the scan of *GPU Gems 3*, in three passes. Shengen Yan, Guoping Long and Yunquan Zhang chained the blocks of a scan in StreamScan (2013). Duane Merrill and Michael Garland then published the decoupled look-back scan in 2016; it is a single pass, and it is the scan of the CUB library. For sparse matrices, the ELLPACK layout comes from the ITPACK guide of Grimes, Kincaid and Young (1979), written for vector machines. Nathan Bell and Michael Garland showed in 2009 that the same layout coalesces on a GPU, and they added the HYB format. For 3D stencils, Paulius Micikevicius (2009) moved a 2D tile through the third dimension. Wavefronts go back to Leslie Lamport's hyperplane method for loops (1974), and CUDASW++ (2009) by Yongchao Liu, Douglas Maskell and Bertil Schmidt ran Smith-Waterman database searches on GPUs.

The advanced techniques grew out of particular applications. Timo Aila and Samuli Laine kept a fixed grid of warps that took rays from a shared pool (2009). Kshitij Gupta, Jeff Stuart and John Owens studied this persistent style in 2012, and Ronald Graham's bounds of 1969 for list scheduling explain its load balance. Michael Bauer, Henry Cook and Brucek Khailany split the warps of a block into loaders and workers in CudaDMA (2011). Andy Adinets described warp-aggregated atomics for filtering in 2014. Mohamed Wahib and Naoya Maruyama searched for kernel fusions automatically in 2014. FlashAttention (2022) by Tri Dao and others applied fusion and tiling to attention, and FlashAttention-3 (2024) by Jay Shah and others added warp specialization on Hopper. Stefano Markidis and others (2018) and Massimiliano Fasi, Nicholas Higham, Mantas Mikaitis and Srikara Pranesh (2021) measured the precision of tensor cores.

## Further reading

- Wen-mei W. Hwu, David B. Kirk and Izzat El Hajj, *Programming Massively Parallel Processors: A Hands-on Approach*, fourth edition (2022). The standard textbook, with chapters on the memory model, tiling, stencils, scans, sparse formats and dynamic parallelism.
- NVIDIA, *CUDA Programming Guide* and *CUDA C++ Best Practices Guide*. The vendor's rules for warps, coalescing, banks, occupancy, streams and graphs, with the limits of each compute capability in tables.
- Samuel Williams, Andrew Waterman and David Patterson, *Roofline: An Insightful Visual Performance Model for Multicore Architectures*, Communications of the ACM 52(4), 2009. The short paper that introduced the roofline.
- Vasily Volkov, *Better Performance at Lower Occupancy*, GPU Technology Conference 2010. The slides of the talk. They show how latency, throughput and parallelism fit together, with measurements.
- Duane Merrill and Michael Garland, *Single-pass Parallel Prefix Scan with Decoupled Look-back*, NVIDIA Technical Report NVR-2016-002, 2016. A careful design of a single-pass GPU primitive, with its protocol and its performance.
- Nathan Bell and Michael Garland, *Implementing Sparse Matrix-Vector Multiplication on Throughput-Oriented Processors*, SC 2009. It compares the sparse formats on a GPU and explains why layout sets the speed.
