After placement every cell of a chip has a position, and each net, a set of pins that must be at the same voltage, still has to be joined by metal. Routing draws those wires. They run on a stack of metal layers, each layer with a preferred direction, and a via joins two neighbor layers. Wires of different nets must not touch, every wire must obey the spacing rules, and the result should be short, use few vias and leave no region over capacity. A design has a very large number of nets, so the work is split. Global routing divides the chip into tiles and chooses, for each net, the tiles its wire passes through. Detailed routing then fixes the exact tracks and vias inside each tile or region.

Two small cases show the two sides of the subject. Two pins on a grid with blocked cells are joined by a shortest path with [Lee's algorithm](/lee-maze-routing/), which is breadth-first search on the grid: if a path exists, it finds a shortest one. Five pins are joined by a tree, and the shortest tree in the Manhattan metric, the rectilinear Steiner minimal tree, is NP-hard to find. With many nets competing for the same tracks, almost every routing problem is hard: global routing, detailed routing, channel routing with or without doglegs, and switchbox routing are all NP-complete or NP-hard. The chapter therefore holds three kinds of results. Some subproblems are solved exactly: one shortest path, a minimum spanning tree, a channel with no vertical constraints, river routing on one layer, escape routing without a fixed order, the layers of a single net. Some are bounds: the channel density, Hwang's ratio $\frac32$ between spanning and Steiner trees, the fractional optimum of a flow relaxation. The rest are heuristics, such as rip-up and reroute, negotiated congestion and the greedy channel router, whose quality is measured on benchmarks and not proved. Each entry says which kind of result it gives.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in seven groups, read in order.

1. **Maze routing.** Lee's algorithm and its faster forms: [Hadlock's algorithm](/hadlock-algorithm/), which searches by detour number and is still shortest, [Soukup's algorithm](/soukup-algorithm/), which is complete but not shortest, and the [line-probe routers](/line-probe-routing/).
2. **Rectilinear trees for one net.** The [rectilinear minimum spanning tree](/rectilinear-minimum-spanning-tree/), [Hanan's theorem](/hanan-theorem/) on where Steiner points can lie, [Hwang's ratio](/hwang-rectilinear-steiner-ratio/), and the heuristics [iterated 1-Steiner](/iterated-one-steiner/) and [FLUTE](/flute/).
3. **Trees with short source paths.** The [rectilinear Steiner arborescence](/rectilinear-steiner-arborescence/), the [bounded-radius bounded-cost tree](/bounded-radius-bounded-cost-tree/) and the [Prim-Dijkstra tradeoff](/prim-dijkstra-tradeoff/), which trade total length against the length of the paths from the source.
4. **Global routing.** The [problem](/global-routing/) with its measures and lower bounds, [pattern routing](/pattern-routing/), [rip-up and reroute](/rip-up-and-reroute/), [negotiated congestion](/negotiated-congestion-routing/), the [multicommodity flow](/multicommodity-flow-global-routing/) approach with a proved fractional bound, and [layer assignment](/layer-assignment/).
5. **Detailed routing and the channel model.** [Detailed routing](/detailed-routing/) in general, then the channel: its [density](/channel-density/), the [channel routing problem](/channel-routing-problem/), the [vertical constraint graph](/vertical-constraint-graph/) and the [NP-completeness](/channel-routing-np-completeness/) of the minimum track count.
6. **Channel routers.** The [left-edge algorithm](/left-edge-algorithm/), [dogleg routing](/dogleg-channel-routing/), the [greedy channel router](/greedy-channel-router/) and the [Yoshimura-Kuh algorithm](/yoshimura-kuh-algorithm/).
7. **Other routing regions.** The [switchbox](/switchbox-routing/), [river routing](/river-routing/) on one layer and [escape routing](/escape-routing/) out of a dense pin array.

## A first reading

Take these fifteen entries in order. For the search on a grid: Lee's algorithm and Hadlock's algorithm. For trees: the rectilinear minimum spanning tree, Hanan's theorem and Hwang's ratio. For global routing: the problem, pattern routing, rip-up and reroute and negotiated congestion. For detailed routing: detailed routing, channel density, the channel routing problem, the vertical constraint graph, the left-edge algorithm and the greedy channel router. They give the exact search for one net, the bounds for trees, the measured methods for many nets and the lower bounds of a channel.

The arborescence group, FLUTE, multicommodity flow, the NP-completeness proofs, the Yoshimura-Kuh algorithm, switchboxes, river routing and escape routing can wait for a second pass.
