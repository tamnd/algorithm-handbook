1. The intervals are $[2,5]$, $[1,3]$, $[4,8]$, $[6,10]$ and $[7,9]$ for nets 1 to 5. The local densities are $1, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 2, 1$, so $d = 3$. The edges are $1 \to 2$ (column 2), $1 \to 3$ (column 5) and $4 \to 3$ (column 8). Left-edge puts nets 1 and 4 on track 1, nets 2 and 3 on track 2 and net 5 on track 3. Three tracks equal the density, so the result is optimal.

2. The only shortest path goes right to column 3, up to row 2, left to column 0, up to row 0, right along row 0 to column 5 and down to $t$: length $3 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 5 + 4 = 19$. The Manhattan distance is 5, so the detour number is $(19 - 5)/2 = 7$. On the other grid the path has length $\mathrm{md}(s,t) + 2D = 9 + 6 = 15$.

4. The Hanan grid has the columns 0, 2, 6 and the rows 0, 3, 5, so 9 points, of which $(0,3)$, $(2,0)$, $(2,5)$ and $(6,5)$ are not pins. The spanning tree has length 16, for example $5 + 4 + 4 + 3$. Hwang's theorem gives $\mathrm{RSMT} \ge \frac23 \cdot 16 = \frac{32}{3}$, so at least 11, since a tree on the Hanan grid has integer length; the half-perimeter is $6 + 5 = 11$. Both bounds are 3 below the optimum.

6. The half-perimeters are $3, 1, 4, 4, 3, 4$, so the wirelength is at least 19. All six nets cross the cut, which has 4 edges, so the total overflow is at least 2, some edge carries 2 nets in every integral routing, and $\lambda^* \ge 6/4 = 3/2$.

7. pres_fac is $0, \frac12, \frac34, \frac98$. The history grows by $1$, $0$ and $2$, so $h = 3$. With 2 other nets the present factor is $1 + \frac98 \cdot 1 = \frac{17}{8}$ and the cost is $(1 + 3) \cdot \frac{17}{8} = \frac{17}{2}$. With 1 other net one more still fits, the factor is 1 and the cost is 4.

10. $|E| = 2 \cdot 100 \cdot 99 = 19800$ and $\ln|E| \approx 9.893$, so $T \ge 100 \cdot 9.893 / 0.01 \approx 98934.4$: 98,935 rounds, after which $\lambda_{\mathrm{frac}} \le 1.2$.

12. The intervals are $[1,2]$, $[1,3]$, $[2,4]$, $[3,4]$ and $d = 3$. The graph has the edges $1 \to 2$, $1 \to 3$, $2 \to 4$ and $3 \to 4$, and its longest paths have 3 nets. Net 1 lies above nets 2 and 3, net 4 below both, and nets 2 and 3 overlap in columns 2 and 3, so they need two different tracks between those of nets 1 and 4: 4 tracks in all. Every net has pins in two columns only, so it is one piece and doglegs at pin columns change nothing.

14. For $h = 1$ the pairs of wires 1 and 2 ask $0 \le s \le 1$, and wires 3 and 4 ask $s \ge 2$: no shift works. For $h = 2$ the conditions are $s \ge -1$ and $s \le 4$. For $h = 3$ only $a_4 + s - b_1 \ge 3$ and $b_4 - a_1 - s \ge 3$ remain, so $-1 \le s \le 5$.

17. For $c = 0$ the tree is the path $s, p_1, p_4, p_2, p_3$ with edges $2, 4, 3, 7$: cost 16 and radius 16. For $c = 1/2$, $p_3$ is attached to $p_1$ with key $\frac12 \cdot 2 + 8 = 9$, less than $10$ from $s$ and $\frac12 \cdot 9 + 7$ from $p_2$. The cost is 17 and the radius is $10 = R$, half the bound $R/c = 20$.

18. A single gcell or three gcells give $1 - 2 = -1$, two gcells on a side give $2 - 2 = 0$, two opposite corners give $0 - 4 = -4$. The best cut bound is 0. Each route of A shares exactly one edge with each route of B, so every routing has overflow at least 1. Cuts count how many nets must pass, not that their routes must cross.

21. For $h \le n - 1$, take $i = 1$: $a_{1+h} - b_1 = h - s < h$. For $h = n$ the condition is empty. Wire $i$ runs right by $s \ge 1$, so its horizontal piece passes the column $i + 1$ of the next top terminal, and wire $i + 1$ must make its turn on a higher track: $n$ wires, $n$ tracks.

23. In iteration 1 every edge costs 1, A sees 2 against 3, B sees 2 against 4, both take the short route, and $e_1$ gets $h = 1$. In iteration 2 (pres_fac $\frac12$), A sees $e_1$ at $(1 + 1)(1 + \frac12) = 3$, so its short route costs 4 against 3 and it moves. Then B sees $e_1$ at $2$, short route 3 against 4, and stays. Iteration 2 ends without overflow. A moves because it is rerouted first and its detour is cheaper; rerouting B first gives B a tie, 4 against 4, so the outcome depends on the order and on how ties are broken.

24. Suppose the congestion is 1. Each of the cuts between rows 0 and 1 and between rows 1 and 2 has 3 edges and is crossed by all 3 nets, so each net uses exactly one edge of each and its route moves up through the rows once. In row 0 the three nets go up at three different columns. If the net from $(1,0)$ moves to column 0, it uses the row edge at $(0,0)$ and the up edge of column 0, so the net from $(0,0)$ can neither move nor go up; column 2 is the same. So it goes up at column 1, and then the corner nets cannot pass column 1 and go up in their own columns. In row 2 the same argument gives the reversed order, so the net from $(0,0)$ enters row 1 at column 0 and leaves at column 2, and so does the net from $(2,0)$ in the other direction: both use both edges of row 1. A routing of congestion 2 exists, so the optimum is 2.

25. Root the tree at a pin. For a segment $e$ into a node $v$ and a layer $\ell$ allowed for $e$, let $F(e, \ell)$ be the fewest vias at $v$ and below. For each interval $[lo, hi]$ that contains $\ell$ (and 1 if $v$ is a pin), the cost is $(hi - lo) + \sum_j \min\{F(e_j, \ell') : \ell' \in [lo, hi]\}$ over the child segments $e_j$, and $F(e, \ell)$ is the least such cost; an interval wider than the layers used only costs more, so the minimum is exact. There are $O(L^2)$ intervals and each child minimum takes $O(L)$, so the time is $O(nL^3)$ for $n$ segments.

26. Let $u_i(r)$ be the column where wire $i$ goes down from track $r$ to track $r - 1$, with $u_i(h) = a_i$ and $u_i(0) = b_i$; on track $r$ the wire runs between $u_i(r)$ and $u_i(r-1)$. Disjointness asks $u_{i+1}(r') \ge u_i(r) + 1$ for $|r' - r| \le 1$. This is a system of difference constraints on a graph in which every edge raises $i$ by 1 and has weight 1, so it is feasible exactly when every path between fixed values is satisfied: from $(i, 0)$ to $(j, h)$, which needs $j - i \ge h$ steps, this is $a_j - b_i \ge j - i$, implied by $a_{i+h} - b_i \ge h$; the other direction gives $b_{i+h} - a_i \ge h$. Take $u_i(r)$ as the longest path from the fixed values.

27. The two short sides and one bridge between them give $2\min(a,b) + \max(a,b) = a + b + \min(a,b)$. For the lower bound, the length is at least the integral over $t$ of the number of points where the line $y = t$ meets the tree, plus the same for vertical lines, and every such line meets the tree. If every horizontal line meets it twice, the length is at least $2b + a$; if every vertical line does, $2a + b$. Otherwise some horizontal and some vertical line each meet it in one point; both points must be the same point $p$, and $p$ separates the four corners, so the length is at least the sum of the four distances from $p$, which is $2(a + b)$.

28. At a cell of distance $k$, the neighbors have distance $k - 1$ or $k + 1$, since the grid is bipartite, so the backtrace can tell them apart exactly when $f(k-1) \ne f(k+1)$; an open grid has cells with neighbors of both kinds. The rule $f(k+1) = 1 - f(k-1)$ fixes $f$ from $f(0)$ and $f(1)$: the four shifts of $0, 0, 1, 1$. In $0, 1, 0, 1$ the values at $k - 1$ and $k + 1$ are equal. With the states empty and blocked, a cell has four states, two bits.
