## History

Routing by computer began with printed circuit boards. E. F. Moore described the breadth-first labeling of a maze in 1959, and C. Y. Lee of Bell Telephone Laboratories published it in 1961 as a method for path connections on a wiring grid. S. B. Akers cut the labels to two bits in 1967. Mikami and Tabuchi in 1968 and Hightower in 1969 replaced the cells by line probes to save memory, and Hadlock in 1977 and Soukup in 1978 aimed the search at the target, Hadlock with a guarantee of a shortest path and Soukup without one.

The theory of rectilinear trees grew alongside. M. Hanan proved in 1966 that a rectilinear Steiner minimal tree can be found on the grid of lines through the pins, F. K. Hwang proved in 1976 that the minimum spanning tree is at most $\frac32$ times as long, and Garey and Johnson showed in 1977 that the problem is NP-complete. For routing this meant heuristics with a good bound in reach: Kahng and Robins introduced iterated 1-Steiner at ICCAD 1990, and Chris Chu presented FLUTE at ICCAD 2004, with optimal lookup tables for small nets. Around 1992 delay entered the tree problem: Cong, Kahng, Robins, Sarrafzadeh and Wong gave trees with bounded radius and bounded cost, Rao, Sadayappan, Hwang and Shor the 2-approximation for the rectilinear Steiner arborescence, and Alpert, Hu, Huang, Kahng and Karger the Prim-Dijkstra tradeoff in 1993.

The channel model came from the wiring between rows of cells. Hashimoto and Stevens gave the left-edge algorithm in 1971, Deutsch added doglegs at pin columns in 1976, and 1982 brought both the greedy router of Rivest and Fiduccia and the merging algorithm of Yoshimura and Kuh. The hardness followed: LaPaugh showed in 1980 that the minimum track count without doglegs is NP-complete, and Szymanski in 1985 that it stays so with doglegs. River routing on one layer, by contrast, got exact linear-time answers from Tompa and from Leiserson and Pinter in the early 1980s.

Global routing took the form used today in the 1980s. Dees and Karger described rip-up and reroute at DAC 1982, Raghavan and Thompson cast global wiring as a 0-1 multicommodity flow and rounded its relaxation in 1987, and Nair rerouted every net in turn in the same year. McMurchie and Ebeling added a history cost in PathFinder, an FPGA router presented at FPGA '95, and that cost scheme returned to chip routing with NTHU-Route 2.0 in 2008. The ISPD contests of 2007 and 2008 gave the field common benchmarks, on which global routers are still compared.

## Further reading

- Andrew B. Kahng, Jens Lienig, Igor L. Markov and Jin Hu, *VLSI Physical Design: From Graph Partitioning to Timing Closure*, 2nd edition, Springer, 2022. A textbook whose chapters on global and detailed routing cover most of this chapter, with worked examples.
- Naveed A. Sherwani, *Algorithms for VLSI Physical Design Automation*, 3rd edition, Kluwer Academic Publishers, 1999. The fullest textbook account of channel, switchbox and river routing and of the maze routers.
- M. Hanan, "On Steiner's problem with rectilinear distance", *SIAM Journal on Applied Mathematics* 14 (1966). The grid theorem and exact trees for small nets, the starting point of rectilinear Steiner trees.
- Ronald L. Rivest and Charles M. Fiduccia, "A greedy channel router", *Proceedings of the 19th Design Automation Conference*, 1982. A short paper that shows how a column scan with local rules always completes a channel.
- Larry McMurchie and Carl Ebeling, "PathFinder: a negotiation-based performance-driven router for FPGAs", *Proceedings of the ACM Symposium on Field-Programmable Gate Arrays*, 1995. The present and history costs of negotiated congestion, in their original setting.
