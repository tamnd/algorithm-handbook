## History

Logic simulation grew with the first large computers built from gates. Ulrich described time-ordered simulation with selective trace in 1965 and the event-driven method that evaluates only active gates in 1969. Eichelberger used three values in 1965 to find hazards and races in asynchronous circuits, and Brzozowski and Seger later proved what ternary simulation can and cannot detect. Hardware for simulation followed in the early 1980s with the Yorktown Simulation Engine, described by Pfister in 1982, and Barzilai, Carter, Rosen and Rutledge gave compiled-code simulation with bit-parallel words in 1987. The value systems and delta cycles of VHDL and Verilog fixed the semantics that event-driven simulators now implement, with the nine values of IEEE 1164 in 1993.

Bryant brought logic simulation down to the transistor with MOSSIM in 1981. Nodes held charge of a given size, transistors were switches with strengths, and the unit of work was the channel-connected component. The same model later carried symbolic values in COSMOS and symbolic trajectory evaluation.

Circuit analysis rests on Kirchhoff's laws of 1845 and 1847 and on Tellegen's theorem of 1952. The computer methods came in a few years around 1970. Shichman and Hodges gave their square-law MOSFET model in 1968. Director and Rohrer found the adjoint network for sensitivities in 1969, and Hachtel, Brayton and Gustavson wrote the sparse tableau in 1971. Ho, Ruehli and Brennan published modified nodal analysis in 1975, the year of Nagel's SPICE2 with its junction limiting, AC and noise analyses. Rohrer, Nagel, Meyer and Weber had given noise analysis by the adjoint in 1971. Sparse pivoting goes back to Markowitz in 1957. Gilbert and Peierls gave LU in time proportional to arithmetic in 1988, and Davis and Palamadai Natarajan published KLU in 2010. Bank and coauthors introduced TR-BDF2 in 1985 for device and circuit simulation, and Kelley and Keyes analyzed pseudo-transient continuation in 1998.

Periodic and radio-frequency analyses came next. Aprille and Trick gave the shooting-Newton method in 1972. Nakhla and Vlach used harmonic balance in 1976, and Kundert and Sangiovanni-Vincentelli made it practical for large circuits in 1986. Feldmann and Freund computed the Padé approximant of asymptotic waveform evaluation stably by the Lanczos process in 1995, and Demir, Mehrotra and Roychowdhury gave the nonlinear theory of oscillator phase noise in 2000. On the side of speed, MOTIS of Chawla, Gummel and Kozak in 1975 introduced timing simulation, with one relaxation sweep per time step. Newton's SPLICE of 1978 joined circuit and logic simulation, and Saleh, Kleckner and Newton iterated the sweeps to convergence in 1983.

## Further reading

- Farid N. Najm, *Circuit Simulation*, Wiley-IEEE Press, 2010. A textbook on MNA, Newton's method, sparse solvers, transient analysis and convergence aids, with proofs.
- Jiri Vlach and Kishore Singhal, *Computer Methods for Circuit Analysis and Design*, second edition, Van Nostrand Reinhold, 1994. The classic reference for equation formulation, sensitivity, noise and frequency-domain methods.
- Kenneth S. Kundert, Jacob K. White and Alberto Sangiovanni-Vincentelli, *Steady-State Methods for Simulating Analog and Microwave Circuits*, Kluwer, 1990. Harmonic balance and shooting methods in one book.
- Miron Abramovici, Melvin A. Breuer and Arthur D. Friedman, *Digital Systems Testing and Testable Design*, IEEE Press, 1994. Its chapters on logic simulation cover value systems, delay models and event-driven and compiled methods.
- Janusz A. Brzozowski and Carl-Johan H. Seger, *Asynchronous Circuits*, Springer, 1995. The theory of ternary simulation, hazards and races.
- Timothy A. Davis, *Direct Methods for Sparse Linear Systems*, SIAM, 2006. Sparse LU, the Gilbert-Peierls method and fill-reducing orders, with code.
