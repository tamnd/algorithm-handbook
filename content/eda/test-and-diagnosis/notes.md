## History

The early history of test generation, fault simulation, scan and self-test is in the notes of chapter 32.7, from Eldred's stuck lines of 1959 and Roth's D-algorithm of 1966 to PODEM in 1981 and SAT-based generation around 1990. This chapter starts from the questions that work left open. Mei studied bridging faults in 1974, Hayes and Friedman placed test points the same year, and Schnurmann, Lindbloom and Carpenter built a weighted random pattern generator in 1975. Parker and McCluskey defined signal probabilities in 1975, and Brglez added observability and the COP estimate in 1984. Williams and Brown published the defect level formula in 1981, which tied fault coverage to the quality of shipped parts.

Generation and simulation became faster in the 1980s. Goel and Rosales described dynamic compaction in 1979, and Krishnamurthy and Akers proved in 1984 that finding the size of a minimum test set is NP-hard. Fujiwara and Shimono published FAN in 1983, Schulz, Trischler and Sarfert added static learning in SOCRATES in 1988, and Kunz and Pradhan gave recursive learning in 1992. Abramovici, Menon and Miller presented critical path tracing in 1983, and Waicukauski and his colleagues at IBM described parallel-pattern single-fault propagation in 1985 and applied it to transition faults in 1986. Delay testing took its two main models from Smith's path delay faults of 1985 and from that transition fault work; Lin and Reddy studied robust tests in 1987, and Savir introduced skewed-load, or launch-on-shift, tests in 1992 and, with Patil, broad-side tests in 1994.

Memory test and diagnosis grew alongside. Nair, Thatte and Abraham gave the linear test MATS in 1978, Suk and Reddy a march test for coupling faults in 1981, and Marinescu the march notation and March C in 1982; van de Goor's book of 1991 fixed the fault models. Abramovici and Breuer proposed effect-cause diagnosis in 1980, as an alternative to the fault dictionary. On the self-test side, Smith counted aliasing streams in 1980, and Williams, Daehn, Gruetzner and Starke treated independent errors with a Markov chain in 1987 and 1988. Boundary scan became IEEE 1149.1 in 1990, from the work of the Joint Test Action Group.

From 1990 the cost of tester data drove the work. Koenemann proposed LFSR reseeding in 1991, and Hellebrand and her colleagues extended it to several polynomials in 1995. Ma, Franco and McCluskey compared many test sets on the defective chips of a Stanford test chip in 1995, the usual first citation for N-detect tests. Broadcast scan came from Lee, Chen and Huang and from Hamzaoglu and Patel at the end of the decade, X-Compact from Mitra and Kim in 2002, and embedded deterministic test from Rajski, Tyszer, Kassab and Mukherjee in 2004. Iyengar, Chakrabarty and Marinissen optimized core wrappers and test access together in 2002, and IEEE 1500 standardized the wrapper in 2005. Hapke and his colleagues brought cell-aware test to industrial libraries in 2009.

## Further reading

- Michael L. Bushnell and Vishwani D. Agrawal, *Essentials of Electronic Testing for Digital, Memory and Mixed-Signal VLSI Circuits* (Kluwer, 2000). The textbook behind most entries here, with chapters on delay test, memory test, compaction and diagnosis.
- Miron Abramovici, Melvin A. Breuer and Arthur D. Friedman, *Digital Systems Testing and Testable Design* (IEEE Press, 1994). The classic treatment of fault models, fault simulation, sequential test generation and diagnosis.
- Laung-Terng Wang, Cheng-Wen Wu and Xiaoqing Wen (editors), *VLSI Test Principles and Architectures* (Morgan Kaufmann, 2006). Covers scan, test compression, logic and memory BIST and boundary scan as industry uses them.
- Paul H. Bardell, William H. McAnney and Jacob Savir, *Built-In Test for VLSI: Pseudorandom Techniques* (Wiley-Interscience, 1987). Pattern generators, signature analysis, aliasing and weighted random patterns, with the algebra over GF(2).
- A. J. van de Goor, *Testing Semiconductor Memories: Theory and Practice* (Wiley, 1991). The functional fault models of memory and the march tests that detect them.
