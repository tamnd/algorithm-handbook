## History

Exact methods came first. Dantzig, Fulkerson and Johnson (1954) solved a 49-city instance with the subtour elimination constraints, the LP that still gives the best lower bounds. Heuristics followed: Croes (1958) improved tours by reversing parts of them, the move now called 2-opt, and Lin (1965) studied 3-opt. Held and Karp (1970, 1971) showed that the value of the subtour LP can be reached with 1-trees and degree penalties, and Lin and Kernighan (1973) introduced the variable-depth search that is still the base of the best heuristics. Or (1976) proposed moving short segments of a tour in his thesis on routing for regional blood banking.

The theory of approximation for the problem took shape in the 1970s. Sahni and Gonzalez (1976) showed that with arbitrary costs no ratio is possible unless P = NP. Rosenkrantz, Stearns and Lewis (1977) analyzed the construction rules: ratio 2 for nearest and cheapest insertion, and bounds of order $\log n$ for nearest neighbour and for insertion in any order. Christofides (1976), in a technical report, and Serdyukov (1978), in a Russian journal, independently replaced the second copy of the tree by a matching and reached $3/2$. Papadimitriou (1977) proved that the problem stays NP-hard for points in the plane.

For points in the plane, attention turned to random inputs and to speed. Beardwood, Halton and Hammersley (1959) had shown that an optimal tour through $n$ random points in the unit square has length close to $\beta\sqrt{n}$, and Karp (1977) used this law to show that his partitioning scheme is within $1 + \varepsilon$ of OPT almost surely. Bartholdi and Platzman (1982) sorted points along a space-filling curve to plan meal deliveries in Atlanta, and Platzman and Bartholdi (1989) proved the $O(\log n)$ ratio. Arora and Mitchell then found, independently and in the mid-1990s, the first approximation schemes for Euclidean inputs, and shared the Gödel Prize in 2010. On the practical side, Johnson and McGeoch (1997) measured the heuristics on large instances against the Held-Karp bound, and Helsgaun (2000) built LKH on candidate edges chosen from 1-trees.

The variants had their own lines. Frieze, Galbiati and Maffioli (1982) gave the $\log_2 n$ ratio for the asymmetric problem with repeated cycle covers. Parker and Rardin (1984) gave the ratio 2 for the bottleneck problem using Fleischner's theorem (1974) on squares of biconnected graphs. Asadpour, Goemans, Mądry, Oveis Gharan and Saberi (2010) broke the $\log n$ barrier with random thin trees, and Svensson, Tarnawski and Végh (2018) found the first constant ratio by another route, later lowered to $22 + \varepsilon$ by Traub and Vygen.

For the symmetric metric problem, the bound $3/2$ stood for more than 40 years. Oveis Gharan, Saberi and Singh (2011) beat it for graph metrics. Karlin, Klein and Oveis Gharan posted the first algorithm below $3/2$ for general metric costs in 2020, with a ratio below $3/2 - 10^{-36}$ in expectation; the paper received the best paper award at STOC 2021. On the side of hardness, Karpinski, Lampis and Schmied (2015) showed that no ratio below $123/122$ is possible unless P = NP.

## Further reading

- David L. Applegate, Robert E. Bixby, Vašek Chvátal and William J. Cook, *The Traveling Salesman Problem: A Computational Study* (2006). The subtour LP, cutting planes and the exact solution of very large instances, with a chapter on tour heuristics.
- David S. Johnson and Lyle A. McGeoch, "The traveling salesman problem: a case study in local optimization", in Aarts and Lenstra (eds.), *Local Search in Combinatorial Optimization* (1997). The measurements of construction rules, 2-opt, 3-opt and Lin-Kernighan quoted throughout the chapter.
- David P. Williamson and David B. Shmoys, *The Design of Approximation Algorithms* (2011). The double tree, Christofides' algorithm, the insertion rules and Arora's scheme, with full proofs.
- Bernhard Korte and Jens Vygen, *Combinatorial Optimization* (sixth edition). A chapter on the TSP covering the approximation algorithms, Arora's scheme, Lin-Kernighan and the Held-Karp bound.
- J. Michael Steele, *Probability Theory and Combinatorial Optimization* (1997). The theorem of Beardwood, Halton and Hammersley and its consequences for random points.
