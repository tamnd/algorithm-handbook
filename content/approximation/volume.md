Many of the problems that matter in practice are NP-hard. A shipping firm packs parcels into trucks, a chip maker drills holes in a board, a cloud provider assigns jobs to machines. For none of these is there a known algorithm that finds the best answer fast on every input, and most likely there is none. Yet the answers are needed today. An approximation algorithm runs in polynomial time and comes with a proof that its answer is within a fixed factor of the best one on every input. That factor is its [approximation ratio](/approximation-ratio/): an algorithm for a minimization problem has ratio $\rho \ge 1$ when its cost is at most $\rho \cdot \text{OPT}$, and an algorithm for a maximization problem has ratio $\alpha \le 1$ when its value is at least $\alpha \cdot \text{OPT}$.

This volume collects what is known about such algorithms. It starts with covering and packing, where most of the methods were first found. Then come scheduling, the traveling salesman problem, the general method of rounding a linear program, the approximation schemes that come as close to OPT as you like, and the theory that proves some ratios cannot be improved.

## Why approximation gets a volume of its own

An approximation algorithm is judged by a proof, not only by a run on test data, and the proofs share a small set of ideas. In this volume you will meet:

- **Lower bounds on OPT.** Every proof compares the answer with something that can be computed and is never more than OPT. In [bin packing](/bin-packing/) it is the total size of the items; in [vertex cover](/vertex-cover/) it is the size of a matching; in scheduling it is the average load and the longest job; in the traveling salesman problem it is the [minimum spanning tree](/mst-lower-bound-for-tsp/). To read a ratio proof, first find its lower bound.
- **Greedy algorithms.** [Greedy set cover](/greedy-set-cover/) is within $H_d \le \ln d + 1$ of OPT, and [greedy maximum coverage](/greedy-max-coverage/) reaches $1 - 1/e$ of OPT. Both ratios are the best possible in polynomial time unless P = NP. [List scheduling](/list-scheduling/) is greedy too, with Graham's ratio $2 - 1/m$.
- **Linear programs and duality.** The [LP relaxation](/lp-relaxation/) of an integer program is a lower bound that can be computed, and by [weak duality](/weak-duality/) so is the value of any feasible solution of its [dual](/dual-linear-program/). [LP rounding](/linear-programming-rounding/) turns a fractional optimum into an integral answer; the [primal-dual schema](/primal-dual-schema/) builds a solution and a dual together and never solves the LP.
- **Randomized rounding.** A fractional solution can be read as a set of probabilities. Rounding at random and bounding the losses with the [Chernoff bound](/chernoff-bound/) gives the ratios for set cover, MAX-SAT and the general covering and packing integer programs.
- **Schemes.** Some problems allow a ratio $1 + \varepsilon$ for every $\varepsilon > 0$. Rounding the numbers of the input lets a dynamic program run in polynomial time, as in the [knapsack FPTAS](/knapsack-fptas/); cutting the plane or a planar graph into small pieces gives the schemes for the Euclidean TSP and for planar graphs.
- **Hardness.** Proofs that no algorithm can do better close the gap from the other side. A [gap problem](/gap-problem/) is the tool: if telling instances with OPT at most $c$ from those with OPT above $\rho c$ is NP-hard, no ratio below $\rho$ is possible unless P = NP. The PCP theorem supplies such gaps for most of the problems of the volume.

## The chapters

**Chapter 18.1, Covering and packing**, is where most readers should start. It treats bin packing and its simple rules, [next fit](/next-fit-bin-packing/), [first fit](/first-fit-bin-packing/) and [first fit decreasing](/first-fit-decreasing/), and the relatives of bin packing. Then come [set cover](/set-cover/) and [maximum coverage](/max-coverage/) with their greedy algorithms; vertex cover, with and without [weights](/weighted-vertex-cover/); the [primal-dual method](/primal-dual-approximation/) and the [local ratio technique](/local-ratio-technique/); set packing, independent sets and [maximum cut](/maximum-cut/); the [k-center problem](/k-center-problem/) with [farthest-first traversal](/gonzalez-k-center/); and the general covering and packing integer programs.

**Chapter 18.2, Scheduling approximation**, assigns jobs to machines. It sets out the [three-field notation](/scheduling-notation/) and the [lower bounds on the makespan](/makespan-lower-bounds/), then proves [Graham's bound](/list-scheduling-bound/) $2 - 1/m$ for list scheduling and the bound $\frac43 - \frac{1}{3m}$ for the [longest processing time rule](/longest-processing-time-first/). It covers exact rules for one machine, such as [earliest due date](/earliest-due-date-first/), precedence constraints, the shop problems and, for [unrelated machines](/unrelated-machine-scheduling/), the [Lenstra-Shmoys-Tardos rounding](/lenstra-shmoys-tardos/) with ratio 2 against a lower bound of $3/2$.

**Chapter 18.3, TSP approximation**, treats the [metric TSP](/metric-tsp/), where distances obey the triangle inequality. The [double-tree algorithm](/double-tree-algorithm/) has ratio 2 and [Christofides' algorithm](/christofides-algorithm/) has ratio $3/2$; the algorithm of Karlin, Klein and Oveis Gharan beats $3/2$ by a tiny constant. The chapter also covers the [subtour LP](/subtour-elimination-lp/) and the [Held-Karp bound](/held-karp-bound/), the [asymmetric](/asymmetric-tsp/) and [bottleneck](/bottleneck-tsp/) problems, [Arora's scheme](/arora-euclidean-tsp-ptas/) for points in the plane, and the heuristics used in practice, from nearest neighbour to [2-opt](/two-opt/) and [Lin-Kernighan](/lin-kernighan/).

**Chapter 18.4, LP rounding**, turns the methods of Chapter 18.1 into a general toolkit: [threshold rounding](/threshold-rounding/), [randomized rounding](/randomized-rounding/), [half-integrality](/half-integrality/), dependent and pipage rounding, [iterative rounding](/iterative-rounding/), the primal-dual schema for network design with the [Goemans-Williamson Steiner forest](/goemans-williamson-steiner-forest/) algorithm, and [dual fitting](/dual-fitting/). Its applications include MAX-SAT, facility location, the [generalized assignment problem](/generalized-assignment-problem/), [survivable network design](/jain-iterative-rounding-network-design/) with Jain's ratio 2, and the [configuration LP](/configuration-lp/) for bin packing.

**Chapter 18.5, Approximation schemes**, studies the problems that can be approximated as closely as you like. It also holds the definitions the whole volume uses: the approximation ratio, the [PTAS](/ptas/), the [FPTAS](/fptas/) and [APX](/apx/). Then come [rounding and scaling](/rounding-and-scaling/) and [trimming](/trimming-technique/), with the schemes for knapsack and subset sum; the proof that a strongly NP-hard problem has [no FPTAS](/no-fptas-for-strongly-np-hard-problems/); [dual approximation](/dual-approximation/) for makespan and the asymptotic schemes for bin packing; and the geometric methods, [Baker's technique](/baker-technique/) for planar graphs, the [shifting technique](/shifting-technique/) and the portal and guillotine schemes for Euclidean problems.

**Chapter 18.6, Hardness of approximation**, proves the lower bounds. It starts with [gap problems](/gap-problem/), the [inapproximability of the general TSP](/general-tsp-inapproximability/), and the reductions that preserve gaps or ratios, such as the [L-reduction](/l-reduction/). It defines [APX-completeness](/apx-completeness/), then builds the tools of the PCP theorem: [Label Cover](/label-cover/), the [long code](/long-code/), [Fourier analysis](/fourier-analysis-of-boolean-functions/) and [Håstad's three-bit PCP](/hastad-3bit-pcp/). It states the [Unique Games Conjecture](/unique-games-conjecture/) and what follows from it, and ends with the thresholds for set cover, vertex cover, clique, satisfiability, maximum cut, the metric TSP and the other problems of the volume.

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course, read these entries in this order: [the approximation ratio](/approximation-ratio/), [bin packing](/bin-packing/), [next fit](/next-fit-bin-packing/), [set cover](/set-cover/), [greedy set cover](/greedy-set-cover/), [the maximal matching algorithm](/vertex-cover-2-approximation/), [primal-dual weighted vertex cover](/primal-dual-weighted-vertex-cover/) and [farthest-first traversal](/gonzalez-k-center/) in Chapter 18.1; [list scheduling](/list-scheduling/) and [Graham's bound](/list-scheduling-bound/) in Chapter 18.2; the [double-tree algorithm](/double-tree-algorithm/) and [Christofides' algorithm](/christofides-algorithm/) in Chapter 18.3; [LP rounding for vertex cover](/lp-rounding-vertex-cover/) and [randomized rounding](/randomized-rounding/) in Chapter 18.4; the [PTAS](/ptas/) and the [knapsack FPTAS](/knapsack-fptas/) in Chapter 18.5; and the [gap problem](/gap-problem/) and the [inapproximability of the general TSP](/general-tsp-inapproximability/) in Chapter 18.6. Do the exercises marked ◦ and • at the end of these chapters. That is a complete first unit on approximation.

If you already know the basic algorithms, start with the opening page of each chapter, which names its groups and the order to read them. The entries marked *Advanced* and *Research* are the place to go deeper; most of Chapter 18.6 is at that level.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained. Entries in other volumes are cited by number too, as the [LP relaxation](/lp-relaxation/) in Volume 15 and the [harmonic series](/harmonic-series/) in Volume 1 are; the number is printed with the link. Some prerequisites, such as NP-hardness, polynomial-time reductions and the PCP theorem, belong to Volume 38, which is not yet written; their definitions are in any textbook on computational complexity.

## What you need first

You should be able to follow a proof by induction. From Volume 15 you need the [linear programming problem](/linear-programming-problem/), the LP relaxation, the dual and weak duality, [complementary slackness](/complementary-slackness/) and [basic feasible solutions](/basic-feasible-solution/). From Volume 7 you need [independent sets](/independent-set/), [maximal matchings](/maximal-matching/), the [minimum spanning tree](/minimum-spanning-tree/) and the [Eulerian circuit](/eulerian-circuit/). From Volume 1 you need [linearity of expectation](/linearity-of-expectation/), the harmonic series and the Chernoff bound. From Volume 2 you need the [greedy algorithm](/greedy-algorithm/) and dynamic programming. The $k$-center and TSP chapters use the [metric space](/metric-space/) and the [triangle inequality](/triangle-inequality/) of Volume 5. You should also know what it means for a problem to be NP-hard.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
