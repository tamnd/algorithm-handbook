## History

The first approximation schemes were for knapsack problems. Sartaj Sahni gave the scheme by partial enumeration in 1975, and in the same year Oscar Ibarra and Chul Kim gave the first fully polynomial scheme, by scaling the profits, for the knapsack and subset sum problems. Eugene Lawler made the knapsack scheme faster in 1979. Michael Garey and David Johnson explained in 1978 why the method stops at problems with large numbers: a strongly NP-hard problem with polynomially bounded integer values has no FPTAS unless P = NP. For scheduling, Sahni gave schemes for a fixed number of machines in 1976, and Sahni and Teofilo Gonzalez showed in the same year that the general TSP has no constant ratio unless P = NP.

The early 1980s brought bin packing and the plane. Wenceslas Fernandez de la Vega and George Lueker showed in 1981 that bin packing can be solved within $1 + \varepsilon$ in linear time, up to an additive constant, by grouping the large items and solving a linear program over configurations. Narendra Karmarkar and Richard Karp made the time polynomial in $1/\varepsilon$ in 1982. Dorit Hochbaum and Wolfgang Maass introduced the shifting technique in 1985 for covering and packing problems in image processing and VLSI. Brenda Baker presented her scheme for planar graphs in 1983, and the journal version appeared in 1994. Hochbaum and David Shmoys gave dual approximation and the PTAS for makespan on identical machines in 1987.

The Euclidean TSP resisted for twenty years. In 1996 Sanjeev Arora, with portals on a randomly shifted quadtree, and Joseph Mitchell, with m-guillotine subdivisions, found schemes independently. They shared the Gödel Prize in 2010 for this work. Arora, Prabhakar Raghavan and Satish Rao extended the method to Euclidean $k$-median in 1998. In a different direction, Arora, David Karger and Marek Karpinski showed in 1995 that dense instances of max cut and many other problems have schemes by sampling, and Fernandez de la Vega gave a scheme for dense max cut at about the same time.

The finer classes came last. Christos Papadimitriou and Mihalis Yannakakis defined the class MAX SNP, and the PCP theorem of Arora, Carsten Lund, Rajeev Motwani, Madhu Sudan and Mario Szegedy (1992) showed that its hard problems have no PTAS unless P = NP. Marco Cesati and Luca Trevisan linked efficient schemes to fixed-parameter tractability in 1997, and Dániel Marx used the link in 2005 to show that independent set in unit disk graphs has no EPTAS unless FPT = W[1]. Noga Alon, Yossi Azar, Gerhard Woeginger and Tal Yadid gave an EPTAS for makespan in 1998. Chandra Chekuri and Sanjeev Khanna gave the PTAS for multiple knapsack in 2000, and Klaus Jansen improved it to an EPTAS in 2010. Leah Epstein and Asaf Levin gave a bin packing scheme that moves few items as new ones arrive in 2006, and Glencora Borradaile, Philip Klein and Claire Mathieu gave the $O(n \log n)$ scheme for planar Steiner tree in 2007.

## Further reading

- David P. Williamson and David B. Shmoys, *The Design of Approximation Algorithms* (2011). Chapter 3 treats the knapsack FPTAS, makespan and bin packing by rounding data and dynamic programming, and later chapters return to the Euclidean TSP.
- Vijay V. Vazirani, *Approximation Algorithms* (2001). Short chapters on the knapsack FPTAS, the bin packing APTAS, makespan scheduling and the Euclidean TSP, each with exercises.
- Giorgio Ausiello, Pierluigi Crescenzi, Giorgio Gambosi, Viggo Kann, Alberto Marchetti-Spaccamela and Marco Protasi, *Complexity and Approximation: Combinatorial Optimization Problems and Their Approximability Properties* (1999). The classes NPO, APX, PTAS and FPTAS, with a large compendium of problems.
- Sanjeev Arora, *Approximation schemes for NP-hard geometric optimization problems: a survey*, Mathematical Programming 97(1–2), 2003. The portal method and its extensions, from the author.
- Hans Kellerer, Ulrich Pferschy and David Pisinger, *Knapsack Problems* (2004). The schemes for knapsack, subset sum and multiple knapsack in full detail.
