## History

Scheduling theory began in operations research in the 1950s, with exact rules for small shops. S. M. Johnson solved the two-machine flow shop in 1954. Jackson proved in a 1955 report that the earliest due date order minimizes the largest lateness, and Wayne E. Smith gave the ratio rule for the weighted completion time in 1956. McNaughton gave the wrap-around rule for preemptive makespan in 1959, and T. C. Hu the level rule for in-trees of unit jobs in 1961, in a study of assembly lines. J. Michael Moore published the algorithm for the number of late jobs in 1968, in a shorter form due to Hodgson, and Eugene Lawler the backward rule for precedence constraints in 1973.

Ronald Graham's paper of 1966 in the Bell System Technical Journal proved that every list schedule is within a factor $2 - 1/m$ of the optimum, and showed the anomalies of list scheduling. His 1969 paper in the SIAM Journal on Applied Mathematics proved $\frac43 - \frac{1}{3m}$ for the list sorted longest first. These are now read as the first worst-case analyses of approximation algorithms. Coffman and Graham solved two processors with unit jobs and precedence in 1972, and Lam and Sethi proved in 1977 that the same rule is within $2 - 2/m$ on $m$ processors. Coffman, Garey and Johnson turned bin packing into a scheduling method with MULTIFIT in 1978; Friesen improved its bound from $1.22$ to $1.2$ in 1984, and Yue proved $\frac{13}{11}$ in 1990.

NP-completeness explained why the exact rules stopped at special cases. Garey, Johnson and Sethi proved the three-machine flow shop strongly NP-hard in 1976, and Gonzalez and Sahni showed in the same year that the two-machine open shop is easy and the three-machine one NP-hard. Ullman proved the hardness of unit jobs with precedence in 1975, and Lenstra, Rinnooy Kan and Brucker the strong NP-hardness of many one-machine problems with release dates in 1977. In 1979 Graham, Lawler, Lenstra and Rinnooy Kan surveyed the whole field, introduced the three-field notation, and classified the problems by their complexity.

The approximation schemes and the LP methods came next. Hochbaum and Shmoys introduced dual approximation in 1987 and used it for a PTAS on identical machines, and in 1988 on uniform machines. Lenstra, Shmoys and Tardos presented the LP rounding for unrelated machines at FOCS in 1987, with the ratio 2 and the lower bound $3/2$; the journal version appeared in 1990. The gap between the two has stayed open, and Shchepin and Vakhania improved the ratio only to $2 - 1/m$ in 2005. For restricted assignment, Svensson showed in 2012 that the configuration LP estimates the optimum within $33/17$. For completion times, Sidney's decomposition of 1975 later gave a 2-approximation under precedence, by the work of Chekuri and Motwani in 1999 and of Margot, Queyranne and Wang in 2003.

## Further reading

- R. L. Graham, E. L. Lawler, J. K. Lenstra and A. H. G. Rinnooy Kan, *Optimization and approximation in deterministic sequencing and scheduling: a survey*, Annals of Discrete Mathematics 5 (1979). The survey that set the three-field notation and mapped the complexity of the classic problems.
- E. L. Lawler, J. K. Lenstra, A. H. G. Rinnooy Kan and D. B. Shmoys, *Sequencing and scheduling: algorithms and complexity*, in Handbooks in Operations Research and Management Science, Volume 4 (1993). A longer survey that adds the approximation results of the 1980s.
- Michael L. Pinedo, *Scheduling: Theory, Algorithms, and Systems*, fifth edition. A textbook that covers the one-machine rules, parallel machines and shops, with an eye on practice.
- Peter Brucker, *Scheduling Algorithms*, fifth edition. A compact reference for the exact algorithms and the complexity of each problem class.
- David P. Williamson and David B. Shmoys, *The Design of Approximation Algorithms* (2011). Its chapters prove the list scheduling, LPT, Hochbaum-Shmoys and Lenstra-Shmoys-Tardos results in the style of this chapter.
- Dorit S. Hochbaum, editor, *Approximation Algorithms for NP-Hard Problems* (1997). Its first chapter, by Leslie A. Hall, surveys approximation algorithms for scheduling.
