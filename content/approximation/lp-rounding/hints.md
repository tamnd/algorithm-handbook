2. Markov's inequality gives $\Pr[\text{cost} > 3\,\text{OPT}] \le 2/3$, so one run succeeds with probability at least $1/3$. With $k$ runs all fail with probability at most $(2/3)^k$, which is at most $1/100$ from $k = 12$ on.

The triangle alone needs LP value $\frac32$, reached only at $\frac12$ on each of its vertices, and the star of $c$ and its leaves needs value 1 more, reached only at $x_c = 1$ with the leaves at 0: lowering $x_c$ by $\delta$ forces $\delta$ on each of the three leaves. So the unique optimum is $x_c = 1$, $x_{\ell_i} = 0$, $x_{t_i} = \frac12$, with $\text{LP}^* = \frac52$. Persistency puts $c$ in the cover and the leaves out; the triangle needs 2 more vertices, so OPT $= 3$.

7. Edge $ab$ subtracts 2, leaving $a$ at 0 and $b$ at 1; edge $bc$ subtracts 1, leaving $b$ at 0 and $c$ at 2; edge $cd$ subtracts 2, leaving both at 0. The cover is all four vertices, of weight 10, and the amounts sum to 5. OPT is 5, by $\{a, c\}$ or $\{b, d\}$, so the ratio 2 is reached.

10. Every set has at most 3 points, so $f = 3$, and $\text{LP}^* = 1 + 1 + \frac32 + \frac12 = 4$. Each set contains a point at $\frac12$, so the best threshold is $\frac12$. The rounding takes $a, b, d, e$ at cost $8 = 2\,\text{LP}^*$, against $f \cdot \text{LP}^* = 12$; OPT is 5, by $\{a, d\}$. The dual $y = 1$ on the sets $\{a, b, c\}$, $\{b, d\}$, $\{c, d, e\}$, $\{a, c, d\}$ and $y = 0$ on $\{a, e\}$ is feasible with value 4.

Add the bounds $z_1 \le \frac23 \cdot 1 + \frac13 (1 - y_2 + y_3)$, $z_2 \le \frac23 \cdot 1 + \frac13 (1 - y_1 + y_3)$, $z_3 \le y_1$, $z_4 \le y_2$ and $z_5 \le \frac13 \cdot 1 + \frac23 (3 - y_1 - y_2 - y_3)$: the $y$ terms cancel and $\sum z_j \le \frac{13}{3}$, which $y = \frac23$ reaches. LP rounding gives $\frac79 + \frac79 + \frac23 + \frac23 + \frac{19}{27} = \frac{97}{27} \approx 3.59$, the random assignment $\frac{27}{8} = 3.375$, and OPT $= 4$; both exceed $\frac34 \cdot \frac{13}{3} = 3.25$.

14. Greedy takes $C$ (price $\frac92$ for each of 1, 2, 4, 5), then $A$ and $B$ (price 15 for 3 and for 6), for cost 48. The set $A$ collects $\frac92 + \frac92 + 15 = 24 = \frac85 \cdot 15$, and so does $B$, while $C$ collects exactly 18, so $\gamma = \frac85 < H_4 = \frac{25}{12}$. The fitted dual has value $48 / \frac85 = 30$, so $\text{LP}^* \ge 30$, and $A$ and $B$ cost 30, so $\text{LP}^* = \text{OPT} = 30$.

15. $B$ is paid $\frac32 + \frac12 = 2$ at time $\frac52$ by the clients at 5 and 2, and opens; they freeze. $A$ is paid 3 at time 3 and opens, and the clients at 0 and 1 freeze, so $\alpha = (3, 3, \frac52, \frac52)$ with sum 11. The client at 2 pays both, so phase 2 keeps only $B$, at cost $F + C = 2 + 10 = 12$. OPT opens $A$ alone for 11, and $C + 3F = 16 \le 33$.

17. The configurations are $\{0.6\}$, $\{0.4\}$, $\{0.6, 0.4\}$ and $\{0.4, 0.4\}$. Two bins $\{0.6, 0.4\}$ and half a bin $\{0.4, 0.4\}$ give $\text{LP}^* = \frac52$; the dual $\frac12$ for each size is feasible, since every configuration has at most two items, and its value is $2 \cdot \frac12 + 3 \cdot \frac12 = \frac52$. The total size is $2.4$, and OPT $= 3$, since no bin holds three items.

21. All $k$ moats grow at rate 1, and at time 1 every cycle edge carries load 2 while each hub edge carries only 1. The algorithm adds $k - 1$ cycle edges at time 1, and the one component is then inactive; reverse delete keeps the path. The cost is $2(k - 1)$ and the dual is $k$. Putting $x_e = \frac12$ on the cycle edges is feasible, since every cut that separates terminals crosses the cycle twice, and it costs $k$, so $\text{LP}^* = k$. A tree through the hub has $k$ edges of cost at least $1 + \varepsilon$, so OPT $= k(1 + \varepsilon)$, and the ratio is $\frac{2(k - 1)}{k(1 + \varepsilon)}$.

22. Minimize $1/\alpha + 3/(1 - \alpha)$: the derivative vanishes when $(1 - \alpha)/\alpha = \sqrt 3$, so $\alpha = 1/(1 + \sqrt 3) \approx 0.366$. The bound is $(4 + 2\sqrt 3) F_{\text{LP}}$ against $\text{LP}^* = 2 F_{\text{LP}}$, a ratio of $2 + \sqrt 3 \approx 3.73$, against 4 at $\alpha = \frac14$.

23. The upper bound is $4^{y - 1} + 4^{-y} \ge 2\sqrt{4^{-1}} = 1$, by the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means. A positive literal fails with probability $4^{-y_i}$ and a negative one with probability $g(y_i) \le 4^{-(1 - y_i)}$, so a clause fails with probability at most $4^{-z_j}$. Since $1 - 4^{-z}$ is concave and equals $\frac34 z$ at $z = 0$ and $z = 1$, the clause is satisfied with probability at least $\frac34 z_j$, and the ratio is $\frac34$.

24. Each $u'$ is joined to $v''$ for all $v \ne u$. A cover that leaves out two primed vertices must hold the whole double-primed side, of weight 10. One that leaves out only $u'$ holds three primed and three double-primed vertices, of weight at least $20 - 2w_u \ge 12$. So $\tau_w(H) = 10$, reached only by $\{1', 2', 3', 4'\}$ and $\{1'', 2'', 3'', 4''\}$; both give $x_v = \frac12$ for every $v$, and $\text{LP}^* = 5$ as in exercise 1.

25. Putting $x_v = \frac12$ on every vertex covers each edge to $\min(1, 1) = 1$, so the LP optimum is $\binom{n}{2}$. The best $k$ vertices miss the $\binom{n/2}{2}$ edges among the others, so OPT $= \binom{n}{2} - \binom{n/2}{2}$, and the ratio is $1 - \frac{n - 2}{4(n - 1)} \to \frac34$. So no rounding of this LP beats $\frac34$ when $q = 2$, and the pipage bound is tight.
