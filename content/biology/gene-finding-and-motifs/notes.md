## History

Coding statistics came first. In 1982 Fickett proposed a test of coding from the base composition at the three codon positions, and Staden and McLachlan scored reading frames by codon preference. Sharp and Li defined the codon adaptation index in 1987. Borodovsky and McIninch built GeneMark in 1993 on three-periodic Markov chains of coding DNA, and Salzberg, Delcher, Kasif and White introduced the interpolated Markov models of Glimmer in 1998. In the same years Bird (1986) and Gardiner-Garden and Frommer (1987) described CpG islands, and the two-state HMM for them became the teaching example of the book by Durbin, Eddy, Krogh and Mitchison (1998).

Gene structure followed. Gelfand, Mironov and Pevzner stated the spliced alignment problem in 1996, the same year that Kulp, Haussler, Reese and Eeckman used a generalized HMM in the gene finder Genie. Burge and Karlin's GENSCAN (1997) made the generalized HMM the standard method for vertebrate genes and introduced maximal dependence decomposition for splice signals. Comparative gene finders added a second genome: TWINSCAN of Korf, Flicek, Duan and Brent (2001) and SLAM of Alexandersson, Cawley and Pachter (2003). Benson's tandem repeat finder (1999) and RepeatMasker of Smit, Hubley and Green became the standard tools for the repeats that have to be masked first.

The study of motifs began with weight matrices. Schneider, Stormo, Gold and Ehrenfeucht (1986) measured the information content of binding sites, and Schneider and Stephens (1990) drew it as the sequence logo. Hertz, Hartzell and Stormo (1990) gave a greedy method for aligning sites, and Lawrence and Reilly (1990) fitted motifs by expectation maximization. Lawrence and colleagues published the Gibbs site sampler in 1993, and Bailey and Elkan published MEME in 1994. Staden (1989) computed the score distribution of a matrix, and Touzet and Varré (2007) made the exact p-value fast with rounded scores.

The hard cases came later. Pevzner and Sze (2000) posed the planted $(15, 4)$ motif as a challenge, showed that the profile methods of the time failed on it, and gave WINNOWER. Buhler and Tompa (2002) answered with random projection. Li, Ma and Wang (2002) proved that the consensus pattern problem behind motif finding is NP-hard and gave a polynomial-time approximation scheme. Tagle and colleagues (1988) named phylogenetic footprinting, and Blanchette and Tompa (2002) turned it into an exact parsimony algorithm. Convolutional networks for binding and chromatin data arrived in 2015 with DeepBind of Alipanahi, Delong, Weirauch and Frey and DeepSEA of Zhou and Troyanskaya.

## Further reading

- Richard Durbin, Sean R. Eddy, Anders Krogh and Graeme Mitchison, *Biological Sequence Analysis: Probabilistic Models of Proteins and Nucleic Acids*, Cambridge University Press, 1998. Markov chains and HMMs for sequence, with CpG islands as the running example.
- Neil C. Jones and Pavel A. Pevzner, *An Introduction to Bioinformatics Algorithms*, MIT Press, 2004. Exon chaining, spliced alignment and the exact motif searches, with full proofs.
- Phillip Compeau and Pavel Pevzner, *Bioinformatics Algorithms: An Active Learning Approach*, 2nd edition, Active Learning Publishers, 2015. Greedy, randomized and Gibbs motif search, built up step by step with exercises.
- Christopher Burge and Samuel Karlin, Finding the genes in genomic DNA, *Current Opinion in Structural Biology* 8, 1998. A short review of gene finding at the time of GENSCAN.
- Mark Yandell and Daniel Ence, A beginner's guide to eukaryotic genome annotation, *Nature Reviews Genetics* 13, 2012. The annotation pipeline as it is run in practice.
