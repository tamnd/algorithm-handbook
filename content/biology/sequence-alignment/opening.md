To align two sequences is to write one above the other, with gaps, so that letters with a common origin stand in the same column. Two genes that descend from one ancestor still look alike, but evolution has changed some letters and inserted or deleted others; the alignment puts the history back on the page. Under the toy scheme of $+2$ for a match, $-1$ for a mismatch and a penalty of 2 for each gap symbol, the best global alignment of ACGTC and AGTAC is

```
A C G T - C
A - G T A C
```

with score 4. A biologist with a new protein searches it against a database of hundreds of millions of sequences and reads the function of the best hits. A sequencing machine produces hundreds of millions of reads, and each one must be placed on a reference genome of three billion bases, allowing for errors and for the two strands. A family of proteins is aligned in one table before a tree is built from it or a structure predicted.

Every result in the chapter is stated under a scoring scheme: a [substitution matrix](/substitution-matrix/), a [gap penalty](/gap-penalty/), and the choice of a global, local or semi-global form. Under the scheme, the dynamic programs of the chapter are exact: [Needleman-Wunsch](/needleman-wunsch/), [Smith-Waterman](/smith-waterman/) and [Gotoh's algorithm](/gotoh-algorithm/) return an optimal alignment in $\Theta(mn)$ time. Whether that alignment is the true history is a different question, which the scheme only models. The tools that search databases and map reads, [BLAST](/blast/), [BWA](/bwa/) and [minimap2](/minimap2/) among them, are heuristics: they can miss the optimal alignment, and their sensitivity is measured on test data, not proved. Multiple alignment under the sum-of-pairs score is [NP-hard](/msa-np-hardness/), so its exact algorithm is exponential in the number of sequences, one method has a proved ratio of $2 - 2/k$ for metric costs, and the programs in daily use are heuristics judged on benchmarks. The pages say which kind of guarantee each method has.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in ten groups, read in order.

1. **Scoring an alignment.** The [alignment score](/alignment-score/), [linear](/linear-gap-penalty/) and [affine](/affine-gap-penalty/) gap penalties, and the [PAM](/pam-matrix/) and [BLOSUM](/blosum-matrix/) substitution matrices.
2. **Global alignment and the table.** The [pairwise problem](/pairwise-sequence-alignment/), the [alignment graph](/alignment-graph/), the [dot matrix plot](/dot-matrix-plot/), Needleman-Wunsch, and [Hirschberg's algorithm](/hirschberg-algorithm/), which finds the alignment in linear space.
3. **Local, semi-global and affine alignment.** [Local alignment](/local-alignment/) by Smith-Waterman, [semi-global alignment](/semi-global-alignment/) for overlaps and fits, affine gaps by Gotoh's three tables, and [banded alignment](/banded-alignment/).
4. **When is a local score significant.** [High-scoring segment pairs](/high-scoring-segment-pair/), the [E-value](/e-value/), the [bit score](/bit-score/) and the [Karlin-Altschul statistics](/karlin-altschul-statistics/) behind them.
5. **Searching a database.** [Seed and extend](/seed-and-extend/), BLAST, [FASTA](/fasta-algorithm/), [X-drop extension](/x-drop-extension/) and [spaced seeds](/spaced-seed/).
6. **Indexes and read mapping.** The [k-mer index](/kmer-index/), [minimizers](/minimizer/), [maximal exact matches](/maximal-exact-match/), [read mapping](/read-mapping/) with [BLAT](/blat/), [Bowtie](/bowtie/) and BWA, and [alignment-free comparison](/alignment-free-comparison/).
7. **Chaining, genomes and graphs.** [Colinear chaining](/colinear-chaining/), [seed chaining](/seed-chaining/), [whole-genome alignment](/whole-genome-alignment/) with [MUMmer](/mummer/), minimap2, and [sequence-to-graph alignment](/sequence-to-graph-alignment/).
8. **Multiple alignment.** The [sum-of-pairs score](/sum-of-pairs-score/), the [problem](/multiple-sequence-alignment/) and its exact program, the [center-star method](/center-star-method/) and its [bound](/center-star-approximation-bound/), NP-hardness, and the [consensus sequence](/consensus-sequence/) and [sequence logo](/sequence-logo/).
9. **Profiles and hidden Markov models.** The [position-specific scoring matrix](/position-specific-scoring-matrix/), [profile alignment](/sequence-profile-alignment/), the [profile HMM](/profile-hmm/) and [HMMER](/hmmer/), and the [pair HMM](/pair-hmm/).
10. **Multiple alignment in practice.** [Progressive alignment](/progressive-alignment/) and [Clustal](/clustal/), [iterative refinement](/iterative-refinement-alignment/) in [MUSCLE](/muscle/) and [MAFFT](/mafft/), the consistency methods [T-Coffee](/t-coffee/) and [ProbCons](/probcons/), and [partial order alignment](/partial-order-alignment/).

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these fourteen entries in order: the alignment score, the gap penalty, the substitution matrix, BLOSUM, pairwise sequence alignment, the alignment graph, Needleman-Wunsch, Smith-Waterman, Gotoh's algorithm, the E-value, seed and extend, BLAST, multiple sequence alignment and progressive alignment. They give the score, the exact programs for two sequences, the meaning of a local score, the heuristic that makes search fast, and the two facts about many sequences: the exact program is exponential, and the practical one follows a tree.

Skip at first the theorems on the Karlin-Altschul limit, the center-star bound and NP-hardness; come back to them once the algorithms are familiar. Skip also the named mappers and multiple aligners after Clustal: they combine the parts of groups 5 to 10, and each is easier to read once the parts are known.

## Before you start

The scores are the similarity form of the [edit distance](/edit-distance/) of Volume 8, with its [edit operations](/edit-operations/) and [weighted edit distance](/weighted-edit-distance/); [approximate string matching](/approximate-string-matching/) there is the core of read mapping. The tables are [dynamic programming](/tabulation/) from Volume 2, with the [traceback](/traceback/) and the [rolling-array space reduction](/rolling-array-space-reduction/), and Hirschberg's algorithm is [divide and conquer](/divide-and-conquer-paradigm/). The alignment graph is a [longest path in a DAG](/dag-longest-path/) from Volume 7. The indexes are the [suffix array](/suffix-array/), [suffix tree](/suffix-tree/) and [FM-index](/fm-index/) of Volume 8, and the [hash table](/hash-table/) of Volume 3; the [k-mer](/kmer/) is defined in Chapter 27.2. Chaining uses the [longest increasing subsequence](/longest-increasing-subsequence/) of Volume 6 and a [Fenwick tree](/fenwick-tree/). The statistics need the [Poisson distribution](/poisson-distribution/) of Volume 1, the PAM matrices a [Markov chain](/markov-chain/), and the profile and pair HMMs the [hidden Markov model](/hidden-markov-model/) with the [Viterbi](/viterbi-algorithm/) and [forward](/forward-algorithm/) algorithms of Volume 25. The hardness results use [NP-completeness](/np-complete/) from Volume 38 and the [shortest common supersequence](/shortest-common-supersequence/) of Volume 6.
