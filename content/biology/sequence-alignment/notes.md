## History

Needleman and Wunsch published a dynamic program in 1970 to compare the amino acid sequences of two proteins. They allowed a gap penalty of any shape, so each cell looked at every gap length and the method took cubic time. Sankoff (1972) and Sellers (1974) gave the $O(mn)$ form, and Wagner and Fischer found the same table for edit distance in 1974. Hirschberg (1975) found a longest common subsequence in linear space by divide and conquer. Smith and Waterman (1981) added the floor of zero that gives local alignment, and Gotoh (1982) brought affine gaps down to $O(mn)$ with three tables; Altschul and Erickson corrected its traceback in 1986, and Myers and Miller (1988) gave affine alignment in linear space.

The scores came from protein data. Dayhoff, Schwartz and Orcutt published the PAM model of accepted point mutations in the *Atlas of Protein Sequence and Structure* in 1978, and Henikoff and Henikoff counted the BLOSUM matrices from blocks of aligned proteins in 1992. Lipman and Pearson's FASTP (1985) and Pearson and Lipman's FASTA (1988) made database search fast by looking first for short exact matches. In 1990 Karlin and Altschul proved the Poisson law for ungapped local scores, and the same year Altschul, Gish, Miller, Myers and Lipman published BLAST, which used it to set its cutoff. Gapped BLAST and PSI-BLAST followed in 1997, with $\lambda$ and $K$ for gapped scores fitted by simulation. Ma, Tromp and Li showed with PatternHunter (2002) that a spaced seed finds more alignments than a contiguous one of the same weight.

Multiple alignment took a different road. Feng and Doolittle (1987) aligned along a tree and kept every gap once made, and Higgins and Sharp wrote the first CLUSTAL in 1988; ClustalW (1994) added sequence weights and position-specific gap penalties. Carrillo and Lipman (1988) pruned the exact sum-of-pairs program. Gusfield gave the center-star method with its ratio $2 - 2/k$ in a 1991 technical report and in 1993, and Wang and Jiang proved in 1994 that the sum-of-pairs problem is NP-hard; Elias closed the question for every metric cost in 2006. Krogh, Brown, Mian, Sjölander and Haussler introduced profile HMMs in 1994, and Eddy's HMMER followed, with HMMER3 (2011) making profile search about as fast as BLAST. T-Coffee (2000), MAFFT (2002), partial order alignment (2002), MUSCLE (2004) and ProbCons (2005) are the main aligners of the next decade.

Genome sequencing changed the scale. Delcher and colleagues aligned whole bacterial genomes with MUMmer in 1999, and Kent wrote BLAT (2002) to align mRNA and proteins to the draft human genome. Roberts, Hayes, Hunt, Mount and Yorke proposed minimizers in 2004, independently of the winnowing of Schleimer, Wilkerson and Aiken (2003). Short reads brought mappers on the FM-index: Bowtie (Langmead, Trapnell, Pop and Salzberg) and BWA (Li and Durbin) appeared in 2009, and BWA-MEM in 2013. Li's minimap2 (2018) chains minimizer hits and serves reads of any length.

## Further reading

- Richard Durbin, Sean R. Eddy, Anders Krogh and Graeme Mitchison, *Biological Sequence Analysis*, Cambridge University Press, 1998. Pairwise alignment, its statistics, pair HMMs and profile HMMs, all under one probabilistic model.
- Dan Gusfield, *Algorithms on Strings, Trees, and Sequences*, Cambridge University Press, 1997. The algorithms: alignment in linear space, suffix trees, and multiple alignment with the center-star bound.
- Neil C. Jones and Pavel A. Pevzner, *An Introduction to Bioinformatics Algorithms*, MIT Press, 2004. A first course that presents alignment as paths in the alignment graph.
- João Setubal and João Meidanis, *Introduction to Computational Molecular Biology*, PWS Publishing, 1997. A short text on alignment, database search and multiple alignment.
- Veli Mäkinen, Djamal Belazzougui, Fabio Cunial and Alexandru I. Tomescu, *Genome-Scale Algorithm Design*, Cambridge University Press, 2015. Indexes, read mapping and chaining at the scale of whole genomes.
