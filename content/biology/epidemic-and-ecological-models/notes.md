## History

Population models begin with Verhulst, who proposed the logistic equation in 1838 as growth with a ceiling. Lotka in 1925 and Volterra in 1926 wrote the predator-prey equations and found their neutral cycles; Volterra was explaining the fish catches of the Adriatic. Gause tested competition between species of *Paramecium* in 1934, and Hardin named the competitive exclusion principle in 1960. Leslie set out the matrix model with age classes in 1945, Ricker gave his stock-recruitment curve for fisheries in 1954, and Holling classified the responses of predators to prey density in 1959.

Epidemic theory has two roots. Ross gave coupled equations for humans and mosquitoes in 1911, and Macdonald added the latent period in the mosquito and the analysis of $R_0$ in the 1950s, which shaped the campaign to eradicate malaria. Kermack and McKendrick published their threshold theorem and the final size relation in 1927. Reed and Frost taught their chain binomial model at Johns Hopkins in the 1920s, and Abbey published it in 1952. Bailey studied the stochastic epidemic in the early 1950s, and Whittle gave the probability of a minor outbreak in 1955. The extinction problem itself is older: Galton and Watson asked in 1875 when a family name dies out, and Bienaymé had stated the criterion earlier.

Space and chaos came next. Fisher, and Kolmogorov, Petrovsky and Piskunov, found the traveling front and its minimal speed $2\sqrt{rD}$ in 1937, and Turing showed in 1952 that diffusion can destabilize a stable reaction and make patterns. Levins introduced the metapopulation model in 1969. Li and Yorke in 1975 and May in 1976 showed that the simplest discrete population model, the logistic map, is chaotic, and Feigenbaum found the universal ratio of its period doublings.

Diversity measures came from information theory and from sampling. Shannon defined entropy in 1948 and Simpson his index in 1949. Petersen used marked plaice in 1896 and Lincoln used bird bands in 1930 to estimate population sizes; Chapman gave the corrected form in 1951. Sanders proposed rarefaction in 1968, Hurlbert corrected it in 1971, and Chao published her lower bound on richness in 1984.

Modern epidemic theory made $R_0$ general and fitted models to data. Diekmann, Heesterbeek and Metz defined $R_0$ as the spectral radius of the next-generation operator in 1990. Pastor-Satorras and Vespignani showed in 2001 that the threshold vanishes on networks with heavy-tailed degrees. Gibson and Renshaw in 1998 and O'Neill and Roberts in 1999 used data augmentation MCMC for missing infection times, Wallinga and Lipsitch linked growth rates to $R$ in 2007, and Cori and coworkers gave the method for $R_t$ in 2013, now a standard tool in outbreak response.

## Further reading

- Matt J. Keeling and Pejman Rohani, *Modeling Infectious Diseases in Humans and Animals*, Princeton University Press (2008). Compartmental, stochastic and spatial epidemic models, with code.
- Roy M. Anderson and Robert M. May, *Infectious Diseases of Humans, Dynamics and Control*, Oxford University Press (1991). $R_0$, vaccination and data from many infections.
- Odo Diekmann, Hans Heesterbeek and Tom Britton, *Mathematical Tools for Understanding Infectious Disease Dynamics*, Princeton University Press (2013). The next-generation matrix, final size and stochastic models, with exercises.
- Hakan Andersson and Tom Britton, *Stochastic Epidemic Models and Their Statistical Analysis*, Springer (2000). Branching approximations, the Reed-Frost model and inference.
- James D. Murray, *Mathematical Biology I and II*, 3rd edition, Springer (2002, 2003). Population models, traveling waves and Turing patterns.
- Anne E. Magurran, *Measuring Biological Diversity*, Blackwell (2004). Diversity indices, rarefaction and richness estimators.
