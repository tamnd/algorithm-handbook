A cell keeps its instructions in long strings over small alphabets. DNA is a string over A, C, G and T, three billion letters long in a human genome; a protein is a string over twenty amino acids that folds into a shape in space. Biology now reads these strings by the billion. A sequencing machine returns hundreds of millions of short reads, each a few hundred letters with some errors, and the work starts there: put the reads together into a genome, compare a new gene with every known one, find which species are related, and predict the shape a protein or an RNA will fold into.

This volume is about the algorithms for that work. Its four chapters hold 254 entries: sequence alignment, genome assembly, phylogenetics and structure prediction.

## Why biology gets a volume of its own

The algorithms of this volume come from other volumes: dynamic programming, string indexes, graph paths, Markov chains and optimization. What they share is a way of stating results. A few ideas come back in every chapter:

- **Every result names its model.** The best alignment is best under a scoring scheme, a [substitution matrix](/substitution-matrix/) and a [gap penalty](/gap-penalty/). An assembly is right under a model of reads and errors, such as the random reads of the [Lander-Waterman model](/lander-waterman-model/). A tree is estimated under an evolutionary model such as [Jukes-Cantor](/jukes-cantor-model/), and a structure is the minimum of an energy model. The model is not the truth, and an optimum under it can be the wrong answer about the cell.
- **Each guarantee has a kind.** Some algorithms are exact under their model: [Needleman-Wunsch](/needleman-wunsch/) for alignment, [Felsenstein's pruning](/felsenstein-pruning/) for the likelihood of a tree, the [Zuker algorithm](/zuker-algorithm/) for RNA folding under a loop energy model. Some have a proved ratio, like the [greedy superstring](/greedy-shortest-common-superstring/) or the [center-star](/center-star-method/) alignment. Some estimators are statistically consistent: they return the true tree as the data grow, if the model is right. And some tools are only measured: the sensitivity of [BLAST](/blast/), an assembler on real reads, the accuracy of [AlphaFold](/alphafold/). The entries say which.
- **Many central problems are NP-hard.** Multiple alignment under the sum-of-pairs score, the [shortest common superstring](/shortest-common-superstring-np-hard/), scaffolding, [maximum parsimony](/maximum-parsimony-np-hard/), the [maximum likelihood tree](/ml-tree-np-hard/), RNA folding with [pseudoknots](/pseudoknot-np-hardness/) and protein folding in the [HP model](/hp-folding-np-completeness/) are all NP-hard. The practical methods are heuristics, exact programs on restricted forms, or exponential searches with good pruning.
- **The data are huge.** A quadratic table for two genomes would have $10^{19}$ cells. The tools index the data once, look for short exact matches and do the costly work only near them, as in [seed and extend](/seed-and-extend/). An index of the genome, such as the [FM-index](/fm-index/) of Volume 8, is worth building because the genome is searched again and again.
- **Repeats cause ambiguity.** A repeat longer than a read cannot be placed by assembly, a read from a repeat maps to several places, and similar genes confuse the guide tree of an alignment. Good algorithms report what the data cannot decide, as [unitigs](/unitig/) do, instead of guessing.

## The chapters

**Chapter 27.1, Sequence alignment**, lines up sequences so that letters with a common origin share a column. It covers the [alignment score](/alignment-score/) with the [PAM](/pam-matrix/) and [BLOSUM](/blosum-matrix/) matrices and [affine gaps](/affine-gap-penalty/); global alignment by Needleman-Wunsch, local alignment by [Smith-Waterman](/smith-waterman/) and affine gaps by [Gotoh's algorithm](/gotoh-algorithm/), all exact in $\Theta(mn)$ time, and [Hirschberg's algorithm](/hirschberg-algorithm/) in linear space; the [Karlin-Altschul statistics](/karlin-altschul-statistics/) behind the [E-value](/e-value/); database search with BLAST and [FASTA](/fasta-algorithm/), and [read mapping](/read-mapping/) with [BWA](/bwa/) and [minimap2](/minimap2/), which are heuristics; and [multiple sequence alignment](/multiple-sequence-alignment/), NP-hard under the sum-of-pairs score, with the center-star ratio $2 - 2/k$, [progressive alignment](/progressive-alignment/), [profile HMMs](/profile-hmm/) and the aligners in daily use.

**Chapter 27.2, Genome assembly**, rebuilds a genome from its reads. It covers [shotgun sequencing](/shotgun-sequencing/), [coverage](/sequencing-coverage/) $c = NL/G$ and the Lander-Waterman count of islands; the [genome assembly problem](/genome-assembly-problem/) and the NP-hard shortest common superstring, with the greedy algorithm, at most 3.5 times the optimum; the [overlap graph](/overlap-graph/), the [string graph](/string-graph/) and [overlap-layout-consensus](/overlap-layout-consensus/); the [k-mer](/kmer/) and [de Bruijn assembly](/de-bruijn-assembly/), where a repeat of length at least $k - 1$ ends a contig; unitigs as safe contigs, the [Eulerian superpath problem](/eulerian-superpath-problem/), graph cleaning by [tip removal](/tip-removal/) and [bubble popping](/bubble-popping/); [scaffolding](/scaffolding/) with [paired-end reads](/paired-end-reads/), which is NP-hard; and the measured quality of an assembly, from [N50](/n50/) to [misassemblies and completeness](/assembly-quality-assessment/).

**Chapter 27.3, Phylogenetics**, builds the tree of descent of a set of species or genes. It covers [phylogenetic trees](/phylogenetic-tree/), rooted and unrooted, and the [substitution models](/substitution-model/) from Jukes-Cantor to [GTR](/gtr-model/); [small parsimony](/small-parsimony/) by the [Fitch](/fitch-algorithm/) and [Sankoff](/sankoff-algorithm/) algorithms, and [long-branch attraction](/long-branch-attraction/), under which parsimony is inconsistent on some trees; [maximum likelihood](/maximum-likelihood-phylogeny/), consistent under its model but NP-hard to optimize, and [Bayesian inference](/bayesian-phylogenetic-inference/); the distance methods [UPGMA](/upgma/), exact on ultrametric data, and [neighbor joining](/neighbor-joining/), exact on tree metrics in $O(n^3)$ time, with the [four-point condition](/four-point-condition/); [tree search](/subtree-prune-and-regraft/), the [bootstrap](/phylogenetic-bootstrap/), the [Robinson-Foulds distance](/robinson-foulds-distance/) and [consensus trees](/consensus-tree/); the [coalescent](/kingman-coalescent/) and species trees with [ASTRAL](/astral/); and [perfect phylogeny](/perfect-phylogeny/), [phylogenetic networks](/phylogenetic-network/) and [reconciliation](/tree-reconciliation/).

**Chapter 27.4, Structure prediction**, turns a sequence into a shape. For RNA it covers [secondary structure](/rna-secondary-structure/), the [Nussinov algorithm](/nussinov-algorithm/) in $O(n^3)$ time, minimum free energy folding by the Zuker algorithm under the [nearest neighbor model](/nearest-neighbor-model/), the [McCaskill partition function](/mccaskill-algorithm/), and pseudoknots, which make the problem NP-hard. For proteins it covers [RMSD](/rmsd/) with the [Kabsch algorithm](/kabsch-algorithm/), [TM-score](/tm-score/) and [GDT-TS](/gdt-ts/); [secondary structure prediction](/protein-secondary-structure-prediction/) from [Chou-Fasman](/chou-fasman-method/) to [PSIPRED](/psipred/), measured by accuracy per residue; the NP-complete HP model; [side-chain packing](/side-chain-packing/) with [dead-end elimination](/dead-end-elimination/) and [threading](/protein-threading/), both NP-hard in general; [comparative modeling](/comparative-modeling/), [fragment assembly](/fragment-assembly/) in [Rosetta](/rosetta/) and [molecular dynamics](/molecular-dynamics/); and AlphaFold, whose accuracy is measured on blind tests, not proved.

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course, read these entries in this order: the alignment score, Needleman-Wunsch, Smith-Waterman, the E-value, BLAST, multiple sequence alignment, progressive alignment, shotgun sequencing, sequencing coverage, the overlap graph, de Bruijn assembly, N50, the phylogenetic tree, the Jukes-Cantor model, the Fitch algorithm, Felsenstein's pruning, neighbor joining, RNA secondary structure, the Nussinov algorithm and RMSD. Do the exercises marked ◦ at the end of each chapter you touch.

If you already know the basic algorithms, start with the opening page of each chapter. It names the groups in the chapter and the order to read them. Chapter 27.1 comes first, because the other three use alignment: assembly finds overlaps by alignment, phylogenetics starts from an aligned set of sequences, and structure prediction starts from the alignment of a family. After it, the chapters can be read in any order.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained. Entries in other volumes are cited by number too.

## What you need first

You should be able to read a short program and follow a proof by induction. No biology is assumed; the entries define the terms they use. Most of the volume is [dynamic programming](/tabulation/) from Volume 2, including the [interval form](/interval-dp/) of RNA folding. From Volume 8 you need the [edit distance](/edit-distance/), the [suffix array](/suffix-array/), the FM-index and the [de Bruijn graph](/de-bruijn-graph/); from Volume 6, the [longest common subsequence](/longest-common-subsequence/). Assembly uses the [Eulerian path](/eulerian-path/) and the [Hamiltonian path](/hamiltonian-path/) of Volume 7, and phylogenetics uses its [tree traversals](/postorder-traversal/). The statistics need the [Poisson distribution](/poisson-distribution/) of Volume 1, the [Markov chain](/markov-chain/) and [Metropolis-Hastings](/metropolis-hastings/) of Volume 17, and the [hidden Markov model](/hidden-markov-model/) and [maximum likelihood estimation](/maximum-likelihood-estimation/) of Volume 25. Superposition uses the [singular value decomposition](/singular-value-decomposition/) of Volume 12, and the hardness results use [NP-hardness](/np-hard/) from Volume 31.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
