A cell keeps its instructions in long strings over small alphabets. DNA is a string over A, C, G and T, three billion letters long in a human genome; a protein is a string over twenty amino acids that folds into a shape in space. Biology now reads these strings by the billion. A sequencing machine returns hundreds of millions of short reads, each a few hundred letters with some errors, and the work starts there: put the reads together into a genome, compare a new gene with every known one, find which species are related, call the variants of a person, find the genes and their controls, and measure which genes are switched on.

This volume is about the algorithms for that work and for the models built on its results. Its ten chapters hold 468 entries.

## Why biology gets a volume of its own

The algorithms of this volume come from other volumes: dynamic programming, string indexes, graph paths, Markov chains, statistics and differential equations. What they share is a way of stating results. A few ideas come back in every chapter:

- **Every result names its model.** The best alignment is best under a [substitution matrix](/substitution-matrix/) and a [gap penalty](/gap-penalty/). A tree is estimated under a model such as [Jukes-Cantor](/jukes-cantor-model/), a genetic diversity is predicted by the [Wright-Fisher model](/wright-fisher-model/), and an epidemic curve by the [SIR model](/kermack-mckendrick-sir-model/). The model is not the truth, and an optimum under it can be the wrong answer about the cell.
- **Each guarantee has a kind.** Some algorithms are exact under their model: [Needleman-Wunsch](/needleman-wunsch/) for alignment, [Felsenstein's pruning](/felsenstein-pruning/) for the likelihood of a tree, the [Gillespie algorithm](/gillespie-algorithm/) for a sample path of a reaction network. Some have a proved ratio, like the [greedy superstring](/greedy-shortest-common-superstring/). Some estimators are statistically consistent if the model is right. And some tools are only measured: the sensitivity of [BLAST](/blast/), a variant caller on a truth set, the accuracy of [AlphaFold](/alphafold/). The entries say which.
- **Many central problems are NP-hard.** Multiple alignment under the sum-of-pairs score, the [shortest common superstring](/shortest-common-superstring-np-hard/), [maximum parsimony](/maximum-parsimony-np-hard/), protein folding in the [HP model](/hp-folding-np-completeness/), [haplotype assembly](/minimum-error-correction-problem/) and [motif finding](/motif-finding-problem/) are all NP-hard. The practical methods are heuristics, exact programs on restricted forms, or exponential searches with good pruning.
- **The data are huge.** A quadratic table for two genomes would have $10^{19}$ cells. The tools index the data once, look for short exact matches and do the costly work only near them, as in [seed and extend](/seed-and-extend/), or skip alignment altogether, as in [pseudoalignment](/pseudoalignment/).
- **Repeats cause ambiguity.** A repeat longer than a read cannot be placed by assembly, and a read from a repeat maps to several places. Good algorithms report what the data cannot decide, as [unitigs](/unitig/) and [mapping qualities](/mapping-quality/) do, instead of guessing.

## The chapters

**Chapter 27.1, Sequence alignment**, lines up sequences so that letters with a common origin share a column. It covers the [alignment score](/alignment-score/), global and local alignment by Needleman-Wunsch and [Smith-Waterman](/smith-waterman/) in $\Theta(mn)$ time, the [E-value](/e-value/) of a hit, database search with BLAST, [read mapping](/read-mapping/) with [BWA](/bwa/) and [minimap2](/minimap2/), and [multiple sequence alignment](/multiple-sequence-alignment/) with [progressive alignment](/progressive-alignment/) and [profile HMMs](/profile-hmm/).

**Chapter 27.2, Genome assembly**, rebuilds a genome from its reads. It covers [coverage](/sequencing-coverage/) and the [Lander-Waterman model](/lander-waterman-model/), the shortest common superstring, the [overlap graph](/overlap-graph/) and the [string graph](/string-graph/), [de Bruijn assembly](/de-bruijn-assembly/), where a repeat of length at least $k - 1$ ends a contig, graph cleaning, [scaffolding](/scaffolding/), and the measured quality of an assembly, from [N50](/n50/) on.

**Chapter 27.3, Phylogenetics**, builds the tree of descent of a set of species or genes. It covers [substitution models](/substitution-model/), [small parsimony](/small-parsimony/) and [long-branch attraction](/long-branch-attraction/), [maximum likelihood](/maximum-likelihood-phylogeny/) and [Bayesian inference](/bayesian-phylogenetic-inference/), the distance methods [UPGMA](/upgma/) and [neighbor joining](/neighbor-joining/), tree search and the [bootstrap](/phylogenetic-bootstrap/), the [coalescent](/kingman-coalescent/) and species trees, and [phylogenetic networks](/phylogenetic-network/).

**Chapter 27.4, Structure prediction**, turns a sequence into a shape. For RNA it covers [secondary structure](/rna-secondary-structure/), the [Nussinov algorithm](/nussinov-algorithm/), minimum free energy folding by the [Zuker algorithm](/zuker-algorithm/) and the [McCaskill partition function](/mccaskill-algorithm/). For proteins it covers [RMSD](/rmsd/) with the [Kabsch algorithm](/kabsch-algorithm/), secondary structure prediction, [side-chain packing](/side-chain-packing/), [threading](/protein-threading/), [fragment assembly](/fragment-assembly/) and AlphaFold. The simulation of motion over time, [molecular dynamics](/molecular-dynamics/), is in Volume 43.

**Chapter 27.5, Read mapping and variant calling**, goes from mapped reads to the differences of one genome from the reference. It covers base and [mapping qualities](/mapping-quality/), the [genotype likelihood](/genotype-likelihood/) and Bayesian [variant calling](/variant-calling-problem/), the NP-hard [haplotype assembly](/haplotype-assembly/), [structural variants](/structural-variant-detection/) from read pairs and split reads, and [copy number segmentation](/copy-number-segmentation/).

**Chapter 27.6, Population genetics**, turns the variants of many people into statements about populations. It covers the Wright-Fisher model and drift, the [Hardy-Weinberg principle](/hardy-weinberg-principle/), estimators of diversity under the infinite sites model, selection and Kimura's fixation probability, linkage disequilibrium, phasing and imputation, population structure and admixture, [genome-wide association](/genome-wide-association-study/) and the history of population size from one genome by [PSMC](/psmc/).

**Chapter 27.7, Gene finding and motifs**, finds the genes in a genome and the short sites that control them. It covers [open reading frames](/open-reading-frame/), coding statistics and [CpG islands](/cpg-island-detection/), [exon chaining](/exon-chaining/) and the [generalized HMM gene finder](/generalized-hmm-gene-finder/), repeat masking and annotation, and motif finding, NP-hard in general, with exact search, the [Gibbs motif sampler](/gibbs-motif-sampler/), [MEME](/meme-algorithm/) and the methods for subtle planted motifs.

**Chapter 27.8, Gene expression and transcriptomics**, measures which genes are switched on. It covers [RNA sequencing](/rna-sequencing/) and its units, [transcripts per million](/transcripts-per-million/) among them, normalization, the [negative binomial model](/negative-binomial-count-model/) for [differential expression](/differential-expression-analysis/), isoform abundances by [EM](/em-isoform-quantification/), transcript assembly on the [splice graph](/splice-graph/), and [single-cell](/single-cell-rna-sequencing/) methods up to [trajectory inference](/trajectory-inference/).

**Chapter 27.9, Systems biology**, models the networks of a cell. It covers reaction networks under [mass action](/mass-action-kinetics/), the [Hill equation](/hill-equation/) and circuits such as the [repressilator](/repressilator/), the [chemical master equation](/chemical-master-equation/) and its exact simulation by the Gillespie algorithm, [flux balance analysis](/flux-balance-analysis/) as a linear program over the steady states of metabolism, and the [inference of regulatory networks](/gene-regulatory-network-inference/) from data.

**Chapter 27.10, Epidemic and ecological models**, models populations of hosts, pathogens and species. It covers compartmental models from the SIR model to the [stochastic SIR model](/stochastic-sir-model/), the [basic reproduction number](/basic-reproduction-number/) and the [final size relation](/final-size-relation/), branching processes and the [extinction probability](/branching-process-extinction-theorem/) of an outbreak, predator and prey in the [Lotka-Volterra model](/lotka-volterra-predator-prey/), the [Leslie matrix](/leslie-matrix/), and estimators of diversity and of the reproduction number from data.

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course, read these entries in this order: the alignment score, Needleman-Wunsch, Smith-Waterman, BLAST, sequencing coverage, de Bruijn assembly, the [phylogenetic tree](/phylogenetic-tree/), neighbor joining, the Nussinov algorithm, the genotype likelihood, the Wright-Fisher model, the Hardy-Weinberg principle, CpG island detection, the Gibbs motif sampler, transcripts per million, the negative binomial model, the Gillespie algorithm, flux balance analysis, the SIR model and the basic reproduction number. Do the exercises marked ◦ at the end of each chapter you touch.

If you already know the basic algorithms, start with the opening page of each chapter. It names the groups in the chapter and the order to read them. Chapter 27.1 comes first, because most of the others use alignment: assembly finds overlaps, phylogenetics starts from aligned sequences, variant calling and expression start from mapped reads, and gene finding uses weight matrices. Population genetics uses the coalescent of Chapter 27.3. Chapters 27.9 and 27.10 need little from the rest of the volume and can be read on their own.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained. Entries in other volumes are cited by number too.

## What you need first

You should be able to read a short program and follow a proof by induction. No biology is assumed; the entries define the terms they use. Most of the sequence chapters are [dynamic programming](/tabulation/) from Volume 2. From Volume 8 you need the [edit distance](/edit-distance/), the [suffix array](/suffix-array/), the [FM-index](/fm-index/) and the [de Bruijn graph](/de-bruijn-graph/). The statistics need the [Poisson](/poisson-distribution/) and [binomial](/binomial-distribution/) distributions of Volume 1, the [Markov chain](/markov-chain/) and [Gibbs sampling](/gibbs-sampling/) of Volume 17, the [hidden Markov model](/hidden-markov-model/), [expectation maximization](/expectation-maximization/) and the [generalized linear model](/generalized-linear-model/) of Volume 25, and the [linear mixed model](/linear-mixed-model/) and [Benjamini-Hochberg procedure](/benjamini-hochberg-procedure/) of Volume 33. The dynamic models need the [initial value problems](/ode-initial-value-problem/) and [eigenvalues](/eigenvalue/) of Volume 12, and flux balance needs the [simplex method](/simplex-method/) of Volume 15. The hardness results use [NP-hardness](/np-hard/) from Volume 39.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
