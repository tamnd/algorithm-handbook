A sequencer cannot read a chromosome from end to end. It reads millions of short pieces taken at random places, and the genome has to be put back together from them. Take the 15-base genome CCGTAAGTATTGTAG, in which the word GTA occurs three times, and cut it into its twelve 4-mers. Each 4-mer occurs once, yet two different strings use exactly this set of 4-mers: CCGTAAGTATTGTAG and CCGTATTGTAAGTAG. Both are Eulerian paths through the same graph, and no assembler can tell them apart from 4-mers alone. A careful one outputs only what both strings contain, the four pieces CCGTA, GTAAGTA, GTAG and GTATTGTA. With 5-mers the two strings differ, and the reconstruction is unique. A read of length 5 spans the repeat GTA with one letter on each side, and that is what makes the difference.

That example holds the chapter in small form. Repeats longer than the reads make the genome ambiguous, so the useful output is a set of pieces that every consistent genome contains, called [contigs](/contig/). Coverage decides how many gaps are left, and the read length against the repeat length decides how much can be resolved.

Every result in the chapter is stated under a model, and the pages say which kind of guarantee each one has. Some results are exact under the model. The [de Bruijn assembler](/de-bruijn-assembly/) outputs exactly the [unitigs](/unitig/) of its graph, and the [string graph](/string-graph/) transitive reduction is exact. Some hold only under conditions: greedy merging returns the genome when adjacent reads overlap by more than the longest repeat, and the de Bruijn assembler returns one contig when no $(k-1)$-mer repeats. Some problems are NP-hard, among them the [shortest common superstring](/shortest-common-superstring-np-hard/), [scaffolding](/scaffolding/) and the [Eulerian superpath problem](/eulerian-superpath-problem/) with multiplicities. The last kind is only measured: k-mer counters, overlap finders, graph cleaning and hybrid tools are tested on data, not proved. The [Lander-Waterman model](/lander-waterman-model/) and its relatives give expectations under random read starts, which are neither worst-case bounds nor measurements.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these twelve entries in order: shotgun sequencing, the k-mer, coverage, repeats, the assembly problem, the contig, the unitig, N50, the overlap graph, the string graph, the de Bruijn assembler and scaffolding. They give the read model, the reason the shortest answer is the wrong target, the safe output, the one number everyone reports and its limits, and the two graphs that assemblers build. Scaffolding then shows what paired reads add.

Skip at first the NP-hardness proofs, the Lander-Waterman formulas beyond the gap count, and the Eulerian superpath problem. Come back to them once the two graphs are familiar. The cleaning steps of group 5 and the tools of group 7 are easier to read after the basic assemblers.
