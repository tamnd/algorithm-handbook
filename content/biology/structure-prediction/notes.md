## History

The algorithms for RNA came first. Tinoco and his colleagues showed in the 1970s that the stability of an RNA helix can be estimated from its neighboring base pairs, the start of the nearest-neighbor model. In 1978 Nussinov, Pieczenik, Griggs and Kleitman gave the dynamic program that maximizes the number of nested pairs, and Nussinov and Jacobson refined it in 1980. Zuker and Stiegler (1981) put loop energies into the same scheme, the core of mfold. Sankoff (1985) showed how to fold and align several sequences at once, and McCaskill (1990) turned the minimum into a sum, to get the partition function and the pair probabilities. The Vienna RNA package (Hofacker and others, 1994) and the Turner parameters, collected by Mathews and others in 1999 and 2004, made them routine.

Grammars and pseudoknots followed. Eddy and Durbin (1994) described RNA families by covariance models, the basis of Infernal and the Rfam database. Rivas and Eddy (1999) gave an $O(n^6)$ dynamic program for a broad class of pseudoknots, and Lyngsø and Pedersen (2000) proved that pseudoknot prediction in the nearest-neighbor model is NP-complete.

For proteins, the physical picture came before the algorithms. Pauling, Corey and Branson described the α-helix in 1951, Ramachandran, Ramakrishnan and Sasisekharan mapped the allowed backbone angles in 1963, and Anfinsen's experiments on ribonuclease, from 1961, led to the thermodynamic hypothesis. Levinthal (1969) noted that a random search would take far too long to find the native state. Chou and Fasman (1974) and Garnier, Osguthorpe and Robson (1978) predicted secondary structure from statistics, Kabsch (1976) solved the superposition problem, and Kabsch and Sander (1983) defined secondary structure from hydrogen bonds in DSSP. Dill's HP model (1985) made folding a combinatorial problem; Hart and Istrail (1996) gave approximation algorithms for it, and in 1998 Crescenzi and colleagues and Berger and Leighton proved it NP-complete in two and three dimensions.

Template methods and search took over in the 1990s. Bowie, Lüthy and Eisenberg (1991) and Jones, Taylor and Thornton (1992) fitted sequences to known folds, and Lathrop (1994) proved threading with pair terms NP-complete. Sali and Blundell (1993) built MODELLER on spatial restraints, and Holm and Sander (1993) and Shindyalov and Bourne (1998) gave the structure alignment programs DALI and CE. Desmet and colleagues introduced dead-end elimination in 1992. Simons, Kooperberg, Huang and Baker (1997) assembled structures from fragments, the method of Rosetta, which later designed the new fold Top7 (Kuhlman and others, 2003). CASP, a blind test held every two years since 1994, became the measure of progress, with GDT, as computed by Zemla's LGA (2003), and TM-score (Zhang and Skolnick, 2004) among its scores.

The last step came from sequence statistics. Direct coupling analysis (Weigt and others, 2009; the mean-field form by Morcos and others, 2011) separated direct from indirect correlations in alignments, and Marks and colleagues (2011) folded proteins from the predicted contacts. AlphaFold (Senior and others, 2020) predicted distances with a deep network; AlphaFold 2 led CASP14 in 2020 and was described by Jumper and others in 2021. Demis Hassabis and John Jumper shared half of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it.

## Further reading

- Richard Durbin, Sean R. Eddy, Anders Krogh and Graeme Mitchison, *Biological Sequence Analysis: Probabilistic Models of Proteins and Nucleic Acids*, Cambridge University Press, 1998. Its RNA chapters derive the Nussinov algorithm, stochastic context-free grammars and covariance models from one point of view.
- Ronny Lorenz and others, ViennaRNA Package 2.0, *Algorithms for Molecular Biology* 6, 2011. The minimum free energy, partition function and design methods as implemented in practice, with the Turner 2004 parameters.
- Carl Branden and John Tooze, *Introduction to Protein Structure*, second edition, Garland, 1999. The structural biology behind the protein entries: backbone angles, secondary structure, folds.
- Ken A. Dill and Justin L. MacCallum, The protein-folding problem, 50 years on, *Science* 338, 2012. A short account of the physics and the computation of folding before the trained predictors.
- Simona Cocco, Christoph Feinauer, Matteo Figliuzzi, Rémi Monasson and Martin Weigt, Inverse statistical physics of protein sequences: a key issues review, *Reports on Progress in Physics* 81, 2018. Potts models, direct coupling analysis and their use for contacts.
