## History

The field began with Mendel's laws applied to populations. G. H. Hardy and Wilhelm Weinberg each showed in 1908 that random mating gives the proportions $p^2$, $2pq$, $q^2$ in one generation. R. A. Fisher's book of 1930 and Sewall Wright's paper of 1931 founded the theory of drift on the model that carries both their names, and Haldane (1927) found that a new advantageous mutant survives with probability about $2s$. Wright also defined the fixation index, in its standard form in 1951. Motoo Kimura made the theory a theory of diffusions from 1955 and gave the fixation probability of a selected allele in 1962. P. A. P. Moran introduced his overlapping-generation model in 1958.

Mutation models came next. Kimura and James Crow defined the infinite alleles model in 1964 and Kimura the infinite sites model in 1969. Warren Ewens found the sampling formula of the infinite alleles model in 1972, and G. A. Watterson the estimator of $\theta$ from segregating sites in 1975. Kingman's coalescent of 1982 changed the point of view: Richard Hudson simulated samples backward in time in 1983, Masatoshi Nei and Wen-Hsiung Li defined nucleotide diversity in 1979, and Fumio Tajima built his test in 1989 on two estimators that agree under neutrality. Lewontin (1964) and Hill and Robertson (1968) gave the measures $D'$ and $r^2$ of linkage disequilibrium.

Molecular data brought tests of selection. Nei and Gojobori (1986) counted synonymous and nonsynonymous sites, McDonald and Kreitman (1991) compared polymorphism with divergence, and Goldman and Yang and Muse and Gaut (both 1994) wrote codon substitution models. Pedigree analysis had its own line: Elston and Stewart (1971) peeled families one at a time, and Lander and Green (1987) moved along the markers with a hidden Markov model.

Large genotype data sets shaped the rest. Clark (1990) resolved haplotypes by parsimony, Excoffier and Slatkin (1995) estimated their frequencies by EM, and Na Li and Matthew Stephens (2003) gave the copying model behind most later phasing and imputation methods. Pritchard, Stephens and Donnelly (2000) wrote the admixture model of STRUCTURE, Alexander, Novembre and Lange (2009) fitted it by maximum likelihood, and Patterson, Price and Reich (2006) put principal components on a statistical footing. Reich and others (2009) introduced f-statistics, and Green and others (2010) used the ABBA-BABA test to show gene flow from Neanderthals. In association studies, Devlin and Roeder (1999) proposed genomic control, Kang, Zaitlen and Eskin (2008) mixed models, Yang and others (2010) SNP heritability, and Bulik-Sullivan and others (2015) LD score regression. McVean and Cardin (2005) approximated the coalescent with recombination by a Markov chain along the genome, and Heng Li and Richard Durbin (2011) used it to read population history from one genome. Durbin's PBWT (2014) made haplotype matching linear in the panel.

## Further reading

- Warren J. Ewens, *Mathematical Population Genetics I: Theoretical Introduction*, 2nd edition, Springer, 2004. The mathematical theory of drift, diffusion and the infinite alleles model, with proofs.
- John Wakeley, *Coalescent Theory: An Introduction*, Roberts and Company, 2009. The coalescent from first principles, with mutation, recombination and population structure.
- John H. Gillespie, *Population Genetics: A Concise Guide*, 2nd edition, Johns Hopkins University Press, 2004. A short first book that gets to the main results quickly.
- Rasmus Nielsen and Montgomery Slatkin, *An Introduction to Population Genetics: Theory and Applications*, Sinauer, 2013. Theory and data analysis side by side, at an undergraduate level.
- Kenneth Lange, *Mathematical and Statistical Methods for Genetic Analysis*, 2nd edition, Springer, 2002. Pedigree likelihoods, kinship and the algorithms of linkage analysis.
