## History

Numerical methods for trees began in the 1950s and 1960s. Robert Sokal and Charles Michener introduced pair-group methods in 1958, the common origin of UPGMA and WPGMA. Joseph Camin and Sokal gave an irreversible form of parsimony in 1965. In 1967 Luca Cavalli-Sforza and Anthony Edwards fitted trees to distances by least squares, and Walter Fitch and Emanuel Margoliash built a tree of cytochrome c sequences by a weighted fit. Thomas Jukes and Charles Cantor gave the simplest substitution model in 1969. Fitch solved the small parsimony problem on binary trees in 1971, John Hartigan extended it to any degree in 1973, and David Sankoff gave the version for any cost table in 1975.

The theory came quickly. Peter Buneman showed in 1971 that a tree is determined by its splits and that the four-point condition characterizes tree metrics, a condition K. A. Zaretskii had found in 1965. In 1978 Joseph Felsenstein showed that parsimony can converge to the wrong tree when two long branches are separated by a short one. In 1981 he made maximum likelihood practical with the pruning algorithm, and in 1985 he brought the bootstrap to phylogenetics. Leslie Foulds and Ronald Graham proved in 1982 that maximum parsimony is NP-complete. For likelihood the corresponding result came much later, from Benny Chor and Tamir Tuller in 2005, and Sébastien Roch gave a short proof in 2006. Substitution models grew from Motoo Kimura's two-parameter model of 1980 through the model of Masami Hasegawa, Hirohisa Kishino and Taka-aki Yano of 1985 to the general time-reversible model, described by Simon Tavaré in 1986.

Naruya Saitou and Masatoshi Nei introduced neighbor-joining in 1987, and James Studier and Karl Keppler gave its usual form and its $O(n^3)$ running time in 1988. Kevin Atteson proved its error radius in 1999, and Olivier Gascuel and Mike Steel showed in 2006 that it is a greedy method for balanced minimum evolution. For comparing trees, David Robinson and Leslie Foulds defined their distance in 1981, and William Day gave linear-time algorithms in 1985. Edward Adams proposed a consensus method in 1972, and T. Margush and F. R. McMorris defined the majority-rule tree in 1981. Bayesian inference of trees by MCMC came in the late 1990s, with Bruce Rannala and Ziheng Yang and with Bob Mau, Michael Newton and Bret Larget, and it spread through the program MrBayes of John Huelsenbeck and Fredrik Ronquist.

Gene trees and species trees met through the coalescent of John Kingman (1982). Richard Hudson added recombination in 1983, and Pekka Pamilo and Nei described in 1988 how gene trees can disagree with species trees. Wayne Maddison framed the species tree problem in 1997, James Degnan and Noah Rosenberg found the anomaly zone in 2006, and Siavash Mirarab and coauthors introduced ASTRAL in 2014. Reconciliation of gene trees with species trees goes back to Morris Goodman and coauthors in 1979 and to Roderic Page in 1994. For data that no tree fits, Hans-Jürgen Bandelt and Andreas Dress gave split decomposition in 1992, and David Bryant and Vincent Moulton introduced NeighborNet in 2004.

## Further reading

- Joseph Felsenstein, *Inferring Phylogenies* (2004). The standard reference for the whole field, from parsimony and distances to likelihood, the bootstrap and comparative methods, with much history.
- Charles Semple and Mike Steel, *Phylogenetics* (2003). The mathematics of trees, splits, triplets, quartets and tree metrics, with full proofs.
- Tandy Warnow, *Computational Phylogenetics: An Introduction to Designing Methods for Phylogeny Estimation* (2017). An algorithmic view of the field: statistical consistency, the number of sites a method needs, and the design of methods for large data sets.
- Ziheng Yang, *Molecular Evolution: A Statistical Approach* (2014). Substitution models, likelihood, Bayesian inference and divergence times, treated with care for the statistics.
- John Wakeley, *Coalescent Theory: An Introduction* (2009). The coalescent and its use for samples of genes, the basis of the group on gene trees and species trees.
- Daniel H. Huson, Regula Rupp and Celine Scornavacca, *Phylogenetic Networks: Concepts, Algorithms and Applications* (2010). Split networks, rooted networks and the algorithms that build them.
