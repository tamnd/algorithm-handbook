A phylogeny is a tree of descent. Its leaves are living taxa, such as species, genes or virus samples. Its inner vertices are their extinct common ancestors. Nobody sees the tree; it has to be inferred from data at the leaves, usually aligned DNA or protein sequences, and sometimes a matrix of distances or a table of characters. The result is used for many jobs. A tree of virus samples traces an outbreak back to its source. A tree of genes tells which genes in two genomes are copies of one ancestral gene. A tree of species is the frame for every comparative study in biology.

The algorithmic core is a search over a huge space. There are $(2n-5)!!$ unrooted binary trees on $n$ taxa, about $2 \times 10^{20}$ for $n = 20$. The methods differ in what they ask of a tree. A distance method fits the path lengths of the tree to a matrix. Parsimony counts the fewest changes the tree needs. Likelihood asks how probable the data are under a substitution model. Bayesian inference turns that likelihood into a posterior distribution over trees. Each criterion splits into two problems. Scoring one fixed tree is easy and exact. Finding the best tree over all trees is NP-hard for parsimony and for likelihood, so programs search the space by local moves. The model matters for every guarantee in the chapter: it says when a method finds the true tree as the data grow, and when it can be led astray.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in ten groups, read in order.

1. **Trees, splits and the space of trees.** [Phylogenetic trees](/phylogenetic-tree/), [rooted and unrooted](/rooted-and-unrooted-trees/), their number, and how a root is placed. A tree is the same thing as its set of [splits](/splits-of-a-tree/), and also as its set of [quartets](/quartet/).
2. **Trees from distances.** [Tree metrics](/tree-metric/) and the [four-point condition](/four-point-condition/). [UPGMA](/upgma/) for clock-like data, and [neighbor-joining](/neighbor-joining/), which is exact on every tree metric and tolerates small errors. Least squares and [minimum evolution](/minimum-evolution/) on noisy data.
3. **Parsimony and perfect phylogeny.** The [small parsimony problem](/small-parsimony/) on a fixed tree, solved exactly by [Fitch's](/fitch-algorithm/) and [Sankoff's](/sankoff-algorithm/) algorithms. [Maximum parsimony](/maximum-parsimony/) over all trees is NP-hard. [Perfect phylogeny](/perfect-phylogeny/) asks whether every character can change only once.
4. **Searching the space of trees.** [Stepwise addition](/stepwise-addition/) and the moves [NNI](/nearest-neighbor-interchange/), [SPR](/subtree-prune-and-regraft/) and [TBR](/tree-bisection-and-reconnection/). The [bootstrap](/phylogenetic-bootstrap/) measures how well the data support each split.
5. **Substitution models and likelihood.** From the [Jukes-Cantor model](/jukes-cantor-model/) to [GTR](/gtr-model/). The [pruning algorithm](/felsenstein-pruning/) computes the likelihood of one tree exactly; the [maximum likelihood tree](/maximum-likelihood-phylogeny/) is NP-hard. [Long-branch attraction](/long-branch-attraction/) shows how parsimony fails. [Bayesian inference](/bayesian-phylogenetic-inference/) samples trees by MCMC.
6. **Time, speciation and traits.** Clocks and [divergence times](/divergence-time-estimation/), the [Yule model](/yule-model/) of random trees, [phylogenetic diversity](/phylogenetic-diversity/) and [independent contrasts](/phylogenetic-independent-contrasts/).
7. **Comparing trees.** The [Robinson-Foulds distance](/robinson-foulds-distance/) in linear time, and distances by moves, which are NP-hard.
8. **Combining trees.** [Consensus trees](/consensus-tree/) of trees on one taxon set, and [supertrees](/supertree/) of trees on overlapping sets.
9. **Gene trees and species trees.** The [coalescent](/coalescent-theory/), [incomplete lineage sorting](/incomplete-lineage-sorting/), [ASTRAL](/astral/), and [reconciliation](/tree-reconciliation/) with duplications, losses and transfers.
10. **Networks.** [Phylogenetic networks](/phylogenetic-network/) for hybridization and recombination, and split networks that display conflict in the data.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these fifteen entries in order: [phylogenetic tree](/phylogenetic-tree/), [rooted and unrooted trees](/rooted-and-unrooted-trees/), [phylogenetic tree reconstruction](/phylogenetic-tree-reconstruction/), [splits of a tree](/splits-of-a-tree/), [tree metric](/tree-metric/), [UPGMA](/upgma/), [neighbor-joining](/neighbor-joining/), [small parsimony](/small-parsimony/), [Fitch's algorithm](/fitch-algorithm/), [maximum parsimony](/maximum-parsimony/), [nearest neighbor interchange](/nearest-neighbor-interchange/), [the Jukes-Cantor model](/jukes-cantor-model/), [Felsenstein's pruning algorithm](/felsenstein-pruning/), [the phylogenetic bootstrap](/phylogenetic-bootstrap/) and [the Robinson-Foulds distance](/robinson-foulds-distance/). They give the whole pipeline: a tree, three ways to score it, a way to search, a measure of support and a way to compare results. Skip at first the NP-hardness proofs, the parsimony variants, the move distances, triplet and quartet consistency, and the networks group. Read [incomplete lineage sorting](/incomplete-lineage-sorting/) next if you work with many genes.

## Before you start

You need trees from Volume 7: [trees](/tree/), [rooted trees](/rooted-tree/), [postorder traversal](/postorder-traversal/), [lowest common ancestors](/lowest-common-ancestor/) and [minimum spanning trees](/minimum-spanning-tree/). The scoring algorithms are [dynamic programming on trees](/tree-dynamic-programming/) from Volume 2. The substitution models are [Markov chains](/markov-chain/), and Bayesian inference uses [Metropolis-Hastings](/metropolis-hastings/) sampling, both from Volume 17. From Volume 25 you need [maximum likelihood estimation](/maximum-likelihood-estimation/), and for the distance methods [agglomerative clustering](/agglomerative-clustering/) and [ultrametrics](/ultrametric/). The hardness results use [NP-hardness](/np-hard/) from Volume 31. Many inputs are a [multiple sequence alignment](/multiple-sequence-alignment/), built as in the chapter on sequence alignment. No biology is assumed beyond the idea that DNA is a string over four letters that mutates as it is copied.
