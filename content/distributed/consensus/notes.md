## History

The first agreement problems came from databases and networks. Akkoyunlu, Ekanadham and Huber described the coordination problem over a lossy channel in 1975, and Jim Gray gave it the name of the two generals in his 1978 notes on database operating systems. In the same notes Gray described two-phase commit, which Lampson and Sturgis had also worked out at Xerox PARC around 1976. Dale Skeen added a third phase in 1981 to remove blocking, under timing assumptions that later turned out to matter. Fred Schneider's 1990 tutorial in *ACM Computing Surveys* set out the state machine approach, the idea that deterministic replicas fed the same commands stay in step.

The theory took shape in the early 1980s. Fischer and Lynch proved in 1982 that $f + 1$ synchronous rounds are needed for interactive consistency with $f$ faulty processes, and Dolev and Strong showed in 1983 that the bound holds also when messages are signed. Fischer, Lynch and Paterson presented their impossibility theorem at PODS in 1983, with the journal version in the *Journal of the ACM* in 1985. In 2001 it received the first PODC Influential Paper Award, now the Dijkstra Prize. Michael Ben-Or showed in 1983 that coin flips get around it, and Michael Rabin's paper of the same year used a common coin. Dwork, Lynch and Stockmeyer defined partial synchrony in 1988, and Chandra and Toueg's 1996 paper on unreliable failure detectors stated the timing needs of consensus as properties of an oracle. In the same year Chandra, Hadzilacos and Toueg proved that $\Omega$ is the weakest oracle that suffices.

Practical protocols followed a slower path. Oki and Liskov published Viewstamped Replication at PODC in 1988. Leslie Lamport submitted "The Part-Time Parliament" in 1990, but it appeared only in 1998 in *ACM Transactions on Computer Systems*, and his short "Paxos Made Simple" of 2001 is what most readers met first. A family of variants came after it: Cheap Paxos with Massa in 2004, Fast Paxos in 2006, Paxos Commit with Gray in 2006 and Vertical Paxos with Malkhi and Zhou in 2009. Mencius (2008) and EPaxos (2013) spread the leader's work, and Flexible Paxos (2016) showed that the two phases need different quorums only to meet each other.

Industry adopted these ideas through lock and coordination services. ZooKeeper's Zab was described by Reed and Junqueira in 2008 and analysed in 2011. Diego Ongaro and John Ousterhout designed Raft for understandability and presented it at USENIX ATC in 2014. Many systems built since then use it. A separate line began in 2008 with Satoshi Nakamoto's white paper, whose network started in January 2009. Garay, Kiayias and Leonardos gave the first formal analysis of the longest-chain rule in 2015.

## Further reading

- Nancy A. Lynch, *Distributed Algorithms* (1996). Gives the two generals problem, FloodSet, the round lower bound, FLP and the commit protocols with complete proofs in one precise model.
- Hagit Attiya and Jennifer Welch, *Distributed Computing: Fundamentals, Simulations, and Advanced Topics*, second edition (2004). A careful treatment of the models, the lower bounds and randomized consensus.
- Christian Cachin, Rachid Guerraoui and Luís Rodrigues, *Introduction to Reliable and Secure Distributed Programming*, second edition (2011). Broadcast, consensus and failure detectors as layered modules with pseudocode.
- Leslie Lamport, *Paxos Made Simple*, ACM SIGACT News 32(4), 2001. The shortest correct account of Paxos, from the invariant up.
- Diego Ongaro and John Ousterhout, *In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm*, USENIX ATC 2014. The Raft paper, with the full rules on one page.
- Robbert van Renesse and Deniz Altinbuken, *Paxos Made Moderately Complex*, ACM Computing Surveys 47(3), 2015. Multi-Paxos as working pseudocode, with the details that the short papers leave out.
