## History

The problem came from building a reliable flight-control computer. The SIFT project at SRI replicated its computations and voted on the results, and its designers had to decide what a faulty processor could do. Marshall Pease, Robert Shostak and Leslie Lamport answered in "Reaching agreement in the presence of faults" (Journal of the ACM, 1980), which defined interactive consistency and proved that it needs $n > 3t$ when messages are not signed. Lamport, Shostak and Pease gave the problem its lasting name in "The Byzantine generals problem" (ACM Transactions on Programming Languages and Systems, 1982), with the oral messages algorithm and the signed messages algorithm.

The bounds followed quickly. Fischer and Lynch proved in 1982 that $t + 1$ rounds are needed, and Dolev and Strong (1983) showed that the bound holds with signatures and gave an authenticated algorithm with polynomially many messages. Dolev (1982) found the connectivity condition $2t + 1$ for general networks, and Dolev and Reischuk (1985) bounded the number of messages and signatures. Fischer, Lynch and Merritt (1986) gave short proofs of the resilience bounds by building a ring of processes, the hexagon argument. Lamport (1983) defined the weak Byzantine generals problem, and the bound $n > 3t$ holds for it too. Berman, Garay and Perry (1989) gave the phase king algorithm, and Berman and Garay the phase queen in the same year, so deterministic agreement with optimal resilience no longer needed exponential messages.

Randomization opened a second line. Ben-Or (1983) gave asynchronous protocols with local coins, and Rabin (1983) used a coin dealt in advance to reach a constant expected number of rounds. Bracha and Toueg (1985) proved that asynchronous Byzantine agreement needs $n > 3t$, and Bracha (1987) reached it with his reliable broadcast. Feldman and Micali presented a synchronous protocol with optimal resilience and constant expected rounds, with no dealer, at STOC in 1988.

Practical systems came a decade later. Malkhi and Reiter (1998) defined Byzantine quorum systems, and Miguel Castro and Barbara Liskov presented PBFT at OSDI in 1999, with a replicated file system that showed the cost could be small. Kotla, Alvisi, Dahlin, Clement and Wong presented Zyzzyva at SOSP in 2007; a 2017 paper by Abraham, Gueta, Malkhi, Alvisi, Kotla and Martin showed that its view change is unsafe. Blockchains with known validator sets revived the field: Jae Kwon described Tendermint in 2014, Miller, Xia, Croman, Shi and Song presented HoneyBadgerBFT at CCS in 2016, and Yin, Malkhi, Reiter, Golan Gueta and Abraham published HotStuff at PODC in 2019.

## Further reading

- Leslie Lamport, Robert Shostak and Marshall Pease, *The Byzantine Generals Problem*, ACM Transactions on Programming Languages and Systems 4(3), 1982. The founding paper, short and readable, with both the oral and the signed algorithm.
- Danny Dolev and H. Raymond Strong, *Authenticated Algorithms for Byzantine Agreement*, SIAM Journal on Computing 12(4), 1983. The polynomial authenticated algorithm and the proof that signatures do not save a round.
- Nancy A. Lynch, *Distributed Algorithms* (1996). Gives exponential information gathering, the lower bounds and the connectivity condition with complete proofs.
- Hagit Attiya and Jennifer Welch, *Distributed Computing: Fundamentals, Simulations, and Advanced Topics*, second edition (2004). A clear account of the phase king, the lower bounds and randomized agreement.
- Christian Cachin, Rachid Guerraoui and Luís Rodrigues, *Introduction to Reliable and Secure Distributed Programming*, second edition (2011). Byzantine broadcast, consistent and reliable broadcast, and Byzantine consensus as layered modules with pseudocode.
- Miguel Castro and Barbara Liskov, *Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance and Proactive Recovery*, ACM Transactions on Computer Systems 20(4), 2002. The full PBFT protocol, its view change and the engineering that made it fast.
