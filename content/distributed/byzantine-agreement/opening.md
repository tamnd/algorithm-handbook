Four flight computers read the same altitude sensor and must agree on one reading before they move the controls. One of them has a fault that does not stop it. It tells the second computer that the plane is at 3,000 metres and the third that it is at 300. A crash would be easy to handle, since a silent computer is simply outvoted. A computer that keeps talking, and tells each listener a different story, is harder: an honest computer cannot tell whether the liar is the one that sent the reading or the one that passed it on. The designers of SIFT, a fault-tolerant aircraft computer built at SRI in the late 1970s, met this problem, and their solution started the study of [Byzantine faults](/byzantine-failure-model/).

The answers come as sharp thresholds. Without signatures, agreement among $n$ processes with $t$ liars is possible exactly when $n > 3t$, the [resilience bound](/byzantine-resilience-bound/). With signatures that no one can forge, broadcast works for any $t < n$, though consensus still needs $n > 2t$, the [authenticated bound](/authenticated-resilience-bound/). A deterministic algorithm needs $t + 1$ rounds by the [round lower bound](/byzantine-round-lower-bound/) and $\Omega(n + t^2)$ messages by the [Dolev-Reischuk bound](/dolev-reischuk-lower-bound/). Coins buy a constant expected number of rounds, and in a fully asynchronous network a deterministic algorithm cannot agree at all. The same counting carries over to [practice](/byzantine-fault-tolerance/): replicated services and blockchains that tolerate $f$ malicious replicas run on $3f + 1$ of them and collect $2f + 1$ votes per decision.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups, read in order.

1. **Faults that lie.** The [Byzantine failure model](/byzantine-failure-model/), the [oral messages model](/oral-messages-model/) and the [signed messages model](/signed-messages-model/). [Byzantine fault tolerance](/byzantine-fault-tolerance/) explains the $3f + 1$ of replicated services.
2. **What agreement means.** [Byzantine broadcast](/byzantine-broadcast/), [strong validity](/strong-validity/) and [weak validity](/weak-validity/), [interactive consistency](/interactive-consistency/), and [Byzantine reliable broadcast](/byzantine-reliable-broadcast/) for networks with no clock.
3. **Agreement by word of mouth.** The [oral messages algorithm](/oral-messages-algorithm/), the [resilience bound](/byzantine-resilience-bound/) $n > 3t$, and the [phase king](/phase-king-algorithm/) and [phase queen](/phase-queen-algorithm/) algorithms.
4. **Agreement with signatures.** The [signature chain](/signature-chain/), the [signed messages algorithm](/signed-messages-algorithm/), the [Dolev-Strong algorithm](/dolev-strong-algorithm/) and the [authenticated resilience bound](/authenticated-resilience-bound/).
5. **The cost of determinism.** The [round lower bound](/byzantine-round-lower-bound/) and the [Dolev-Reischuk bound](/dolev-reischuk-lower-bound/) on messages.
6. **Asynchrony and coins.** [Bracha's reliable broadcast](/bracha-reliable-broadcast/), [Bracha's agreement](/bracha-asynchronous-agreement/), [Rabin's algorithm](/rabin-randomized-byzantine-agreement/) with a dealt coin, and the [Feldman-Micali algorithm](/feldman-micali-byzantine-agreement/), which makes its own coin.
7. **Quorums and certificates.** The [Byzantine quorum system](/byzantine-quorum-system/) and the [quorum certificate](/quorum-certificate/).
8. **Replicated services.** [Byzantine state machine replication](/byzantine-state-machine-replication/), [PBFT](/pbft/), [Zyzzyva](/zyzzyva/), [Tendermint](/tendermint/), [HotStuff](/hotstuff/) and [HoneyBadgerBFT](/honeybadger-bft/).

## A first reading

A first pass can take ten entries. Start with the [Byzantine failure model](/byzantine-failure-model/) and [Byzantine broadcast](/byzantine-broadcast/). Run the [oral messages algorithm](/oral-messages-algorithm/) by hand for $n = 4$, then read the [resilience bound](/byzantine-resilience-bound/) to see why three processes cannot do it. Read the [signature chain](/signature-chain/) and the [Dolev-Strong algorithm](/dolev-strong-algorithm/) to see what signatures change. The [phase king algorithm](/phase-king-algorithm/) shows a deterministic algorithm with small messages, and [Bracha's reliable broadcast](/bracha-reliable-broadcast/) shows the echo pattern that most asynchronous protocols use. Finish with the [quorum certificate](/quorum-certificate/) and [PBFT](/pbft/), the protocol that most later systems start from.

Skip on a first pass the variants of validity, the lower bounds beyond their statements, the randomized algorithms of Rabin and of Feldman and Micali, and the research protocols Zyzzyva and HoneyBadgerBFT. Zyzzyva is worth reading later as a lesson: its published view change is unsafe.

## Before you start

The chapter builds on Chapter 21.1 and assumes its vocabulary: the [synchronous model](/synchronous-model/), the [asynchronous model](/asynchronous-model/), [partial synchrony](/partial-synchrony/), [crash failures](/crash-failure/), the [consensus problem](/consensus-problem/) with its [correctness conditions](/consensus-properties/), and the [quorum](/quorum/). The [round lower bound for crashes](/consensus-round-lower-bound/) and [Ben-Or's algorithm](/ben-or-consensus/) are the crash versions of two entries here, and the [view change](/view-change/) of Paxos-style protocols returns in PBFT. From Chapter 21.4 you need [state machine replication](/state-machine-replication/) and [quorum intersection](/quorum-intersection/).

Signatures are used as a black box: a correct process's signature cannot be forged, and anyone can check it. Threshold signatures, threshold encryption and verifiable secret sharing appear in the later protocols, and their constructions belong to the cryptography volume. The randomized entries need expected values and the idea of a [Las Vegas algorithm](/las-vegas-algorithm/). Tendermint spreads votes by [gossip](/gossip-protocol/), and the [message complexity](/message-complexity/) of Chapter 21.5 is the measure used for the Dolev-Reischuk bound.
