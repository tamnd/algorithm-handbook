## History

Election began as a practical question about token rings. In 1977 Gérard Le Lann proposed that, when the token is lost, every process sends its identifier all the way round the ring, so that all of them see every identifier and agree on the largest; this costs $n^2$ messages. In 1979 Ernest Chang and Rosemary Roberts let a message die as soon as it meets a larger identifier. The worst case stayed quadratic, but the average dropped to $n H_n$, and the paper turned election into a problem of counting messages.

The race to $O(n \log n)$ followed quickly. Daniel Hirschberg and J. B. Sinclair reached it in 1980 on bidirectional rings with probes over doubling distances. In 1982 Randolph Franklin gave the simpler local-maxima rule, and Gary Peterson and, independently, Danny Dolev, Maria Klawe and Michael Rodeh showed that the same bound holds on a one-way ring. James Burns proved in 1980 that $\Omega(n \log n)$ messages are needed on asynchronous rings when the algorithm does not know $n$. Greg Frederickson and Nancy Lynch proved in 1987 that synchronous rounds do not help an algorithm that only compares identifiers, which explained why algorithms such as TimeSlice must use identifiers as numbers to do better.

In 1980 Dana Angluin showed that identical processes cannot break symmetry deterministically, which made the identifiers of the ring algorithms a necessity rather than a convenience. Alon Itai and Michael Rodeh answered at FOCS 1981 with coin flips: random identifiers elect a leader with probability 1 when $n$ is known, and no algorithm can be both always correct and always terminating when it is not. The journal version appeared in 1990. Masafumi Yamashita and Tsunehiko Kameda later described exactly which anonymous networks allow election, using the views of the processes.

Other topologies came next. Robert Gallager, Pierre Humblet and Philip Spira published their minimum spanning tree algorithm in 1983, and it elects a leader with $O(m + n \log n)$ messages on any network; Baruch Awerbuch brought its time down to $O(n)$ in 1987. For complete networks, Ephraim Korach, Shay Kutten and Shlomo Moran gave the capture method at PODC 1985, and Yehuda Afek and Eli Gafni studied the trade-off between time and messages, with the journal paper in 1991. Hector Garcia-Molina's paper of 1982 took the problem into systems with crashes and partitions with the bully and invitation algorithms, which remain the textbook answers for practitioners.

## Further reading

- Nancy A. Lynch, *Distributed Algorithms* (1996). Chapter 3 gives LCR, Hirschberg-Sinclair, TimeSlice and the Frederickson-Lynch bound, Chapter 4 gives FloodMax, and Chapter 15 returns to rings in the asynchronous model with Peterson's algorithm and Burns's bound.
- Hagit Attiya and Jennifer Welch, *Distributed Computing: Fundamentals, Simulations, and Advanced Topics*, second edition (2004). Chapter 3 treats ring election, Angluin's impossibility and the lower bounds in one careful model.
- Gerard Tel, *Introduction to Distributed Algorithms*, second edition (2000). Its election chapter covers rings, trees and general networks, and presents Dolev-Klawe-Rodeh as a simulation of Franklin.
- Nicola Santoro, *Design and Analysis of Distributed Algorithms* (2007). The fullest book account of election, with saturation on trees and exact message counts for many ring algorithms.
- Alon Itai and Michael Rodeh, "Symmetry breaking in distributed networks", *Information and Computation* 88(1), 1990. The randomized algorithms for anonymous rings and the proofs of what is impossible without knowing $n$.
- Greg N. Frederickson and Nancy A. Lynch, "Electing a leader in a synchronous ring", *Journal of the ACM* 34(1), 1987. The lower bound for comparison-based algorithms and the algorithms that beat it with numbers.
