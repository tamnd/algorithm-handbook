One server in a cluster of 1024 learns a new configuration. In every round it calls one other server chosen at random and passes the news on, and so does every server that already knows it. In one seeded run the number of informed servers goes 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 31, 60, 115, 215, 370, 577, 754, 889, 973, 1006, 1015, 1021, 1024. The count almost doubles in each of the first ten rounds, while the news is rare. At the end most calls reach servers that already know, and the last 51 servers take four more rounds. The run ends after 17 rounds and 7057 messages, close to the prediction $\log_2 n + \ln n \approx 16.9$. Nobody built a tree, nobody kept a list of the members, and after the first few rounds no single crash could have stopped the spread. When the callee is also allowed to answer with the news, the same cluster is informed in 9 rounds.

This is a [gossip protocol](/gossip-protocol/): each process talks, round after round, with a few peers chosen at random. Gossip sends more messages than a broadcast along a spanning tree, which needs only $n - 1$, and in exchange it needs no structure, spreads the load evenly and survives crashes and lost messages. Large stores, cluster managers and peer-to-peer systems use it to spread updates, to detect failures, to keep membership lists and to compute averages and counts.

The chapter has a theory half and a systems half. The theory gives exact round counts on the complete graph, a bound on the number of messages that every algorithm must send, and the speed on any graph in terms of its conductance. The systems half shows how real protocols get the random peer that the theory assumes, and how they turn gossip into broadcast, failure detection and aggregation.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these twelve entries in order: [the gossip protocol](/gossip-protocol/), [the random phone call model](/random-phone-call-model/), [push gossip](/push-gossip/), [pull gossip](/pull-gossip/), [push-pull gossip](/push-pull-gossip/), [the fanout](/gossip-fanout/), [rumour mongering](/rumor-mongering/), [probabilistic broadcast](/probabilistic-broadcast/), [SWIM](/swim/), [the peer sampling service](/peer-sampling-service/), [Cyclon](/cyclon/) and [gossip aggregation](/gossip-aggregation/). Before you read push-pull, simulate push and pull by hand on ten nodes. The two shapes of the curve, fast start and slow end against slow start and fast end, are the whole idea of push-pull.

On a first pass, skip the three theorem entries of the second and third groups. The [push spreading time](/push-rumor-spreading-time/) proof, the [message lower bound](/rumor-spreading-message-lower-bound/) and the [conductance bound](/conductance-rumor-spreading-bound/) are the hardest proofs in the chapter, and their statements are enough to read the rest.
