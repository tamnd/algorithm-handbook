## History

Leslie Lamport's paper of 1978, "Time, clocks, and the ordering of events in a distributed system", started the subject. It defined happened-before, gave the two rules of the logical clock, built the total order with ties broken by process number, used it for mutual exclusion, and then added physical clocks with a bound on their drift. Colin Fidge (1988) and Friedemann Mattern (1989) published vector time independently and showed that it captures happened-before exactly. Bernadette Charron-Bost proved in 1991 that no vector with fewer than $n$ entries can do so. Version vectors were older than vector clocks: D. Stott Parker and his colleagues used them in 1983 to detect conflicting copies of files. Gene Wuu and Arthur Bernstein used a matrix of counters per site in 1984 to discard log entries that every site had seen.

Clock synchronization grew in parallel. Keith Marzullo's 1984 Stanford thesis, after a 1983 paper with Susan Owicki, gave the interval intersection that NTP still uses. Jennifer Lundelius and Nancy Lynch proved the bound of $(1 - 1/n)u$ with the shifting argument in 1984, and Dolev, Halpern and Strong (1986) and Srikanth and Toueg (1987) studied the cost of faults and drift. David Mills specified NTP in RFC 958 in 1985 and led it up to version 4 in RFC 5905 (2010). Flaviu Cristian published probabilistic clock synchronization in 1989, and in the same year Riccardo Gusella and Stefano Zatti described TEMPO, the time service of Berkeley UNIX 4.3BSD. IEEE 1588, the Precision Time Protocol, first appeared in 2002, from work led by John Eidson at Agilent.

K. Mani Chandy and Leslie Lamport published the snapshot algorithm with markers in 1985 with the reachability theorem. Ten H. Lai and Tao H. Yang removed the FIFO assumption in 1987 by colouring the messages, and Mattern (1993) replaced their histories with counters and used the same idea for global virtual time. Robert Cooper and Keith Marzullo defined possibly and definitely in 1991, and Vijay Garg and Brian Waldecker gave the fast checker for conjunctive predicates in 1994. On the ordering of messages, Kenneth Birman, André Schiper and Pat Stephenson gave the vector-timestamp rule for causal multicast in 1991, and Vassos Hadzilacos and Sam Toueg set out FIFO, causal and total order as layers over reliable broadcast in 1994.

Simulation took its own path to the same ideas. Randal Bryant described a conservative method in an MIT report in 1977, and Chandy and Jayadev Misra published it with null messages and a proof in 1979. David Jefferson published virtual time and Time Warp in 1985, with rollback, antimessages and global virtual time. Later work fitted clocks to new systems. Francisco Torres-Rojas and Mustaque Ahamad defined plausible clocks in 1996, Paulo Sérgio Almeida, Carlos Baquero and Victor Fonte gave interval tree clocks in 2008, and the dotted version vectors of Nuno Preguiça and his colleagues (2010) went into Riak. Google's Spanner (Corbett and others, 2012) made clock uncertainty part of its interface with TrueTime, and Sandeep Kulkarni and his colleagues presented hybrid logical clocks in 2014.

## Further reading

- Leslie Lamport, *Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System*, Communications of the ACM 21(7) (1978). Short, and still the clearest account of happened-before and logical clocks.
- Ajay D. Kshemkalyani and Mukesh Singhal, *Distributed Computing: Principles, Algorithms, and Systems*, Cambridge University Press (2008). Covers logical time, snapshots, message ordering and predicate detection with pseudocode.
- Hagit Attiya and Jennifer Welch, *Distributed Computing: Fundamentals, Simulations and Advanced Topics*, second edition, Wiley (2004). Its chapter on causality and time gives logical and vector clocks and the shifting proof of the clock synchronization lower bound.
- Reinhard Schwarz and Friedemann Mattern, *Detecting Causal Relationships in Distributed Computations: In Search of the Holy Grail*, Distributed Computing 7(3) (1994). A survey of causal history, vector time and the cost of capturing causality exactly.
- Richard M. Fujimoto, *Parallel and Distributed Simulation Systems*, Wiley (2000). The standard book on conservative and optimistic simulation, lookahead and global virtual time.
- Martin Kleppmann, *Designing Data-Intensive Applications*, O'Reilly (2017). Shows how unreliable clocks, Lamport timestamps and version vectors play out in real databases.
