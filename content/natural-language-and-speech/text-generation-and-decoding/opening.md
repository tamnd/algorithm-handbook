A language model gives a probability to every next token, but it does not say which text to write. Ask a translation model for the German of "Thank you" and it may give "Danke" probability 0.4, "Vielen Dank" 0.35 and the empty output 0.05. Taking the most probable token at each step is fast, but it can miss the most probable sentence. Searching for the most probable sentence is better for translation, yet for many models the most probable output is short or bland, and for stories it loops on the same phrase. Sampling gives variety, and cutting off the unlikely tail of the distribution keeps it from drifting into nonsense. The decoder is the algorithm that makes these choices, and the same model can write well or badly depending on it.

Decoding is also where most of the time of a large model is spent, since each token needs one pass of the network. Caching the attention keys and values, and letting a small model propose tokens that the large model checks, make generation faster; the second method still samples exactly from the large model. The chapter ends with older ways to generate text: summarizers that pick the important sentences of a document, the measures that score a summary against one written by a person, and the pipeline that turns data into sentences.

Most methods here are heuristics with measured quality. A few results are theorems: the exact mode is hard to find in general, speculative decoding and stochastic beam search sample exactly from the model, and extractive summarization without redundancy is a knapsack problem solved by dynamic programming.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in nine groups, read in order.

1. **The decoding problem.** [Autoregressive decoding](/autoregressive-decoding/) as search for the mode or as sampling, [greedy decoding](/greedy-decoding/), and [length normalization](/length-normalization/), which stops the score from favoring short outputs.
2. **Sampling the next token.** [Temperature sampling](/temperature-sampling/), [top-k sampling](/top-k-sampling/), [nucleus sampling](/nucleus-sampling/) and [typical sampling](/typical-sampling/), and two fixes for repetition, the [repetition penalty](/repetition-penalty/) and [contrastive search](/contrastive-search/).
3. **Beam search variants and constraints.** [Diverse beam search](/diverse-beam-search/), [grid beam search](/grid-beam-search/) for required words, [stochastic beam search](/stochastic-beam-search/), which samples without replacement, and [grammar-constrained decoding](/grammar-constrained-decoding/).
4. **The mode and the risk.** The [inadequacy of the mode](/inadequacy-of-the-mode/), [minimum Bayes risk decoding](/minimum-bayes-risk-decoding/) and [self-consistency](/self-consistency-decoding/), a vote over sampled answers.
5. **Faster decoding.** The [key-value cache](/key-value-cache/) and [speculative decoding](/speculative-decoding/).
6. **Training and test mismatch.** [Exposure bias](/exposure-bias/) and [scheduled sampling](/scheduled-sampling/).
7. **Summarization.** [Automatic summarization](/automatic-summarization/) as selection under a budget, [Luhn's method](/luhn-summarization/), the graph methods [LexRank](/lexrank/) and [TextRank](/textrank/), and the [pointer-generator network](/pointer-generator-network/), which copies words from the source.
8. **Scoring generated text.** [ROUGE](/rouge/) and [BERTScore](/bertscore/).
9. **Generation from data.** The [natural language generation pipeline](/natural-language-generation-pipeline/) and the [incremental algorithm](/incremental-algorithm-referring-expressions/) for referring expressions.
