A phone in a kitchen hears "set a timer for ten minutes and add eggs to the list". A program finds where speech starts in the audio and turns each hundredth of a second into a short vector of features. A recognizer searches for the word sequence that best explains those vectors, weighing what was heard against what people usually say. The text is then split into tokens, "ten minutes" is read as a duration and "eggs" as an item, and the sentence is parsed into two commands. The reply, "Timer set for ten minutes", is generated as text and spoken by a synthesizer. Each step is a problem with its own algorithms, and most of them must cope with ambiguity: the same sounds fit several word sequences, and the same words fit several structures.

This volume collects those algorithms. Seven chapters treat text, from tokens to word vectors, language models, decoding, tagging, parsing and translation; the last two treat speech. It has nine chapters and 299 entries.

## Why natural language and speech gets a volume of its own

A few ideas run through the whole volume:

- **Choose the most probable reading.** Most methods define a probability or a score over the possible outputs and search for the best one. When the model factors along the input, the search is an exact dynamic program: the [Viterbi algorithm](/viterbi-algorithm/) for hidden Markov models and linear-chain CRFs, and the [Eisner algorithm](/eisner-algorithm/), in $\Theta(n^3)$ time, for arc-factored projective dependency trees. For neural models that condition on the whole prefix, the search is greedy or a beam and need not find the best output.
- **Counts need smoothing.** A model estimated from counts gives probability zero to everything it has not seen. [Good-Turing estimation](/good-turing-estimation/), [Kneser-Ney smoothing](/kneser-ney-smoothing/) and their relatives move some mass to unseen events and still give a proper distribution. They are estimators, not theorems about text, and they are compared by [perplexity](/perplexity/) on held-out data.
- **Models learn from raw text.** [Expectation-maximization](/expectation-maximization/) fits word alignments, hidden Markov models and subword vocabularies with no labels; it reaches the global optimum for [IBM model 1](/ibm-model-1/), whose likelihood is concave, and in general only a local one.
- **Words become vectors.** By the [distributional hypothesis](/distributional-hypothesis/), a word is known by its contexts. Count matrices, word2vec and [transformers](/transformer/) turn words into vectors whose geometry reflects use; analogy and similarity scores of such vectors are measurements.
- **Finite-state machines do much of the work.** Tokenizers, morphological analyzers and speech decoding graphs are [finite-state transducers](/finite-state-transducer/), and composing, determinizing and minimizing them are central operations.
- **Quality is measured.** Few results in the volume are theorems about correctness. Methods are compared on held-out data by perplexity, [word error rate](/word-error-rate/), [BLEU](/bleu/) or listener ratings, and a method is better only on the data and measure tested. [Scaling laws](/neural-scaling-law/) of large models are power laws fitted over a measured range.

## The chapters

**Chapter 41.1, Tokenization and text normalization** (22 entries), covers [language identification](/language-identification/), [tokenization](/text-tokenization/) and the Penn Treebank rules, [sentence boundary detection](/sentence-boundary-detection/) with [Punkt](/punkt-sentence-tokenizer/), truecasing, [word segmentation](/word-segmentation/), [morphological analysis](/morphological-analysis/) and Morfessor, and the subword vocabularies: [WordPiece](/wordpiece/), [byte-level BPE](/byte-level-bpe/), the [unigram language model](/unigram-language-model-tokenization/), and the [NP-hardness of optimal tokenization](/optimal-tokenization-hardness/).

**Chapter 41.2, Word and sentence embeddings** (32 entries), starts from [pointwise mutual information](/pointwise-mutual-information/) and the [word-context matrix](/word-context-matrix/). It covers the [skip-gram model](/skip-gram-model/) with [negative sampling](/negative-sampling/) and hierarchical softmax, [GloVe](/glove/), [Brown clustering](/brown-clustering/) and subword vectors, and the theorem that skip-gram with negative sampling factorizes a shifted PMI matrix when every dot product is free. It treats word sense disambiguation with the [Lesk](/lesk-algorithm/) and Yarowsky algorithms, cross-lingual mappings, bias measured by [WEAT](/word-embedding-association-test/) and reduced by [hard debiasing](/hard-debiasing/), and sentence vectors up to [Sentence-BERT](/sentence-bert/).

**Chapter 41.3, Language modeling** (41 entries), is the hub of the volume. It defines the [language model](/language-model/) and perplexity, builds the [n-gram model](/n-gram-language-model/) with smoothing from Laplace to [Katz backoff](/katz-backoff/) and Kneser-Ney, and then the neural models: recurrent models, [scaled dot-product attention](/scaled-dot-product-attention/), positional encodings and the transformer. It goes on to [BERT](/bert/), [GPT](/generative-pretrained-transformer/), [FlashAttention](/flashattention/) (exact attention with fewer memory transfers), scaling laws, and tuning to instructions and preferences by [RLHF](/reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedback/), [DPO](/direct-preference-optimization/) and [LoRA](/low-rank-adaptation/).

**Chapter 41.4, Text generation and decoding** (29 entries), turns a language model into a generator. It covers [autoregressive decoding](/autoregressive-decoding/), greedy and beam decoding with length normalization, [minimum Bayes risk decoding](/minimum-bayes-risk-decoding/), and the [inadequacy of the mode](/inadequacy-of-the-mode/), the measured fact that the most probable output of a translation model is often empty or too short. Sampling runs from temperature to [top-k](/top-k-sampling/) and [nucleus](/nucleus-sampling/) sampling; [speculative decoding](/speculative-decoding/) samples exactly from the large model with fewer sequential calls of it, and the [key-value cache](/key-value-cache/) makes the attention cost of each step linear in the length. It ends with summarization by [TextRank](/textrank/) and pointer-generator networks, scored by [ROUGE](/rouge/).

**Chapter 41.5, Tagging and information extraction** (26 entries), treats [sequence labeling](/sequence-labeling/): [part-of-speech tagging](/part-of-speech-tagging/) with the [HMM tagger](/hmm-part-of-speech-tagger/), the Brill tagger, maximum entropy Markov models and the [label bias problem](/label-bias-problem/), the [structured perceptron](/structured-perceptron/) and the BiLSTM-CRF. It covers [named entity recognition](/named-entity-recognition/), relation extraction with distant supervision, [coreference resolution](/coreference-resolution/) from the Hobbs algorithm to span ranking, entity linking and sentiment analysis.

**Chapter 41.6, Syntactic and semantic parsing** (33 entries), covers [constituency parsing](/constituency-parsing/) with treebanks, PARSEVAL, lexicalized and latent-variable grammars and [A* parsing](/a-star-parsing/), and [dependency parsing](/dependency-parsing/) by transitions ([arc-standard](/arc-standard-parsing/), arc-eager, swap, dynamic oracles) and by graphs (the Eisner algorithm and [maximum spanning trees](/mst-dependency-parsing/)). Then come [CCG](/combinatory-categorial-grammar/) and TAG, [semantic parsing](/semantic-parsing/), semantic role labeling, AMR and discourse.

**Chapter 41.7, Machine translation and alignment** (36 entries), starts from the [parallel corpus](/parallel-corpus/), [Gale-Church](/gale-church-alignment/) sentence alignment and the [noisy channel](/noisy-channel-translation-model/). It covers [word alignment](/word-alignment/) with the IBM and HMM models, [phrase-based translation](/phrase-based-translation-model/) with stack decoding, hierarchical translation with [cube pruning](/cube-pruning/), the [encoder-decoder model](/encoder-decoder-model/) with attention, and the metrics BLEU, chrF, TER and [COMET](/comet-metric/).

**Chapter 41.8, Speech analysis and synthesis** (36 entries), begins with the [source-filter model](/source-filter-model/). It builds the features of recognition, the [mel filter bank](/mel-filter-bank/) and [MFCCs](/mel-frequency-cepstral-coefficients/), then voice activity detection, pitch tracking from cepstral methods to pYIN, formants, coding by [CELP](/code-excited-linear-prediction/) and modification by PSOLA. It covers [text-to-speech](/text-to-speech/) from grapheme-to-phoneme conversion and [unit selection](/unit-selection-synthesis/) to HMM synthesis, [Tacotron](/tacotron/), [WaveNet](/wavenet/) and HiFi-GAN, judged by the [mean opinion score](/mean-opinion-score/).

**Chapter 41.9, Speech recognition** (44 entries), covers the [source-channel model](/source-channel-speech-recognition/), [dynamic time warping](/dynamic-time-warping/), and the [GMM-HMM acoustic model](/gmm-hmm-acoustic-model/) with triphones, adaptation and discriminative training. Decoding uses time-synchronous [beam search](/time-synchronous-beam-search/), [word lattices](/word-lattice/) and the [HCLG graph](/hclg-decoding-graph/) built by weighted composition and determinization. End-to-end models follow: [CTC](/connectionist-temporal-classification/), the RNN transducer, attention models and wav2vec 2.0. It ends with keyword spotting, [speaker verification](/speaker-verification/) from GMM-UBM to x-vectors, and diarization.

## How to read this volume

Chapter 41.1 comes first and assumes little. Chapter 41.3 is the hub: its n-gram models are used by truecasing, Brown clustering, translation and recognition, and its transformer by most neural methods in the volume. Read its first groups early. Chapter 41.2 can come next; its contextual vectors need the neural models of 41.3. Chapter 41.4 needs the transformer of 41.3 and the encoder-decoder and attention of 41.7. Chapters 41.5 and 41.6 form a pair that leans more on Volumes 25 and 9 than on this volume. Chapter 41.7 needs the language models of 41.3. Chapters 41.8 and 41.9 form a pair: recognition uses the features of 41.8, and synthesis uses the pronunciation lexicon and state tying of 41.9.

For a first course, take these entries in order: [tokenization](/text-tokenization/), [subword tokenization](/subword-tokenization/), [language model](/language-model/), [n-gram language model](/n-gram-language-model/), [perplexity](/perplexity/), [Kneser-Ney smoothing](/kneser-ney-smoothing/), [word embedding](/word-embedding/), [skip-gram model](/skip-gram-model/), [transformer](/transformer/), [autoregressive decoding](/autoregressive-decoding/), [nucleus sampling](/nucleus-sampling/), [HMM part-of-speech tagger](/hmm-part-of-speech-tagger/), [dependency parsing](/dependency-parsing/), [arc-standard parsing](/arc-standard-parsing/), [IBM model 1](/ibm-model-1/), [BLEU](/bleu/), [MFCCs](/mel-frequency-cepstral-coefficients/), [word error rate](/word-error-rate/), [GMM-HMM acoustic model](/gmm-hmm-acoustic-model/) and [CTC](/connectionist-temporal-classification/). A second course can add [negative sampling](/negative-sampling/), [BERT](/bert/), [length normalization](/length-normalization/), the structured perceptron, the Eisner algorithm, [phrase-based translation](/phrase-based-translation-model/) and [text-to-speech](/text-to-speech/). Each chapter's opening page gives a longer first reading.

Each entry stands on its own; its facts box lists what it needs, by entry number.

## What you need first

From Volume 25 you need [maximum likelihood estimation](/maximum-likelihood-estimation/), the [hidden Markov model](/hidden-markov-model/) with the Viterbi and [forward-backward](/forward-backward-algorithm/) algorithms, expectation-maximization, [softmax regression](/softmax-regression/), the [cross-entropy loss](/cross-entropy-loss/) and [backpropagation](/backpropagation/). From Volume 9 come [regular expressions](/regular-expression/), finite-state transducers and [weighted automata](/weighted-automaton/), and for parsing the [context-free grammar](/context-free-grammar/), [probabilistic CKY](/probabilistic-cky/) and the [inside-outside algorithm](/inside-outside-algorithm/). From Volume 8 come the [Unicode code point](/unicode-code-point/), [UTF-8](/utf-8/) and [edit distance](/edit-distance/), and from Volume 7 the [Edmonds arborescence algorithm](/edmonds-arborescence/). The speech chapters need the [short-time Fourier transform](/short-time-fourier-transform/), the [cepstrum](/cepstrum/) and [linear predictive coding](/linear-predictive-coding/) from Volume 28. Decoders use [beam search](/beam-search/) and [A* search](/a-star-search/) from Volume 29, and many entries use [Shannon entropy](/shannon-entropy/) from Volume 24.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
