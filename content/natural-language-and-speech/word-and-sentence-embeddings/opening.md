A program that sees "cat" and "kitten" as two unrelated strings cannot tell that a sentence about one says something about the other. This chapter is about giving words, and then whole sentences, numbers that carry their meaning. The starting point is the distributional hypothesis: words used in the same contexts tend to mean similar things. Count the contexts of each word and you get a long sparse vector; compress or predict and you get a short dense one, with 50 to a few hundred dimensions. Similar words then tend to have a high cosine, and on standard analogy tests some relations between words come out as roughly constant offsets between vectors.

Most of the methods here are fitted by gradient descent on a corpus, and their quality is measured on tests such as word similarity, analogies and sentence similarity. Few of them have a guarantee beyond the optimum of their own objective. The exceptions are worth knowing: the shifted-PMI theorem says what negative sampling fits when every dot product is free, the orthogonal Procrustes map has a closed form by the SVD, the word mover's distance is the exact optimum of a transportation problem, and the association test gives an exact permutation p-value. The chapter also covers word senses, since one vector per word mixes them, and the lexical methods that work with senses and taxonomies directly.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in seven groups, read in order.

1. **Words by their contexts.** The [distributional hypothesis](/distributional-hypothesis/), the [word-context matrix](/word-context-matrix/), [pointwise mutual information](/pointwise-mutual-information/) and its [positive form](/positive-pointwise-mutual-information/), the [word embedding](/word-embedding/) as a map from words to dense vectors, and [Brown clustering](/brown-clustering/), which merges words into classes by mutual information.
2. **Word vectors by prediction.** The word2vec models, [CBOW](/continuous-bag-of-words/) and [skip-gram](/skip-gram-model/), trained with [hierarchical softmax](/hierarchical-softmax/) or [negative sampling](/negative-sampling/); the theorem that [negative sampling factorizes a shifted PMI matrix](/sgns-implicit-factorization-theorem/); [GloVe](/glove/), a weighted least squares fit to log counts; and the [subword skip-gram](/subword-information-embeddings/) of fastText.
3. **Testing and adjusting word vectors.** [Word analogies](/word-analogy/) as a test, [retrofitting](/retrofitting-word-vectors/) to a lexicon, [hard debiasing](/hard-debiasing/) and the [word embedding association test](/word-embedding-association-test/).
4. **Across languages.** [Cross-lingual mapping](/cross-lingual-embedding-mapping/) by an orthogonal matrix and retrieval with [cross-domain similarity local scaling](/cross-domain-similarity-local-scaling/) (CSLS).
5. **Word senses and taxonomies.** [Word sense disambiguation](/word-sense-disambiguation/), the [Lesk algorithm](/lesk-algorithm/), the [Yarowsky algorithm](/yarowsky-algorithm/), [Resnik similarity](/resnik-similarity/) in a taxonomy and [Hearst patterns](/hearst-patterns/) for finding hyponyms in text.
6. **Vectors in context.** The [contextual word embedding](/contextual-word-embedding/), one vector per occurrence, and [ELMo](/elmo/).
7. **Sentence and document vectors.** The [sentence embedding](/sentence-embedding/) problem, the [paragraph vector](/paragraph-vector/), [smooth inverse frequency](/smooth-inverse-frequency-embedding/) (SIF) weighting, the [word mover's distance](/word-movers-distance/), [Sentence-BERT](/sentence-bert/) and [SimCSE](/simcse/).

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these twelve entries in order: the distributional hypothesis, the word-context matrix, pointwise mutual information, positive PMI, word embedding, the skip-gram model, negative sampling, GloVe, word analogy, word sense disambiguation, the Lesk algorithm and the sentence embedding problem. They are marked *Intro* or *Core*. Work the small examples by hand: a PPMI table of three words and three contexts, the noise distribution of negative sampling with the power $3/4$, and the overlaps of a gloss with a sentence in the Lesk algorithm. Then read the shifted-PMI theorem, which ties the first two groups together. The research entries at the end of the groups, CSLS, Sentence-BERT and SimCSE, can wait for a second pass.

## Before you start

You need [cosine similarity](/cosine-similarity/) and [nearest neighbour search](/nearest-neighbor-search/) from Volume 5, and [self-information](/self-information/) and [Huffman coding](/huffman-coding/) from Volume 24. The learning methods come from Volume 25: the [embedding](/embedding/) as a learned lookup table, [softmax regression](/softmax-regression/), [logistic regression](/logistic-regression/), [principal component analysis](/principal-component-analysis/), [agglomerative clustering](/agglomerative-clustering/) and [dropout](/dropout/). The training uses [stochastic gradient descent](/stochastic-gradient-descent/) and [AdaGrad](/adagrad/) from Volume 15. Cross-lingual mapping needs the [singular value decomposition](/singular-value-decomposition/) and retrofitting the [Jacobi method](/jacobi-method/) from Volume 12. The word mover's distance is a [minimum cost flow problem](/min-cost-flow-problem/) from Volume 7, and the association test is a [permutation test](/permutation-test/) from Volume 33. Brown clustering uses the [n-gram language model](/n-gram-language-model/), ELMo the [recurrent language model](/recurrent-neural-network-language-model/), and Sentence-BERT and SimCSE start from [BERT](/bert/), all in Chapter 41.3. Hearst patterns are [regular expressions](/regular-expression/) over noun phrases (Volume 9).
