## History

The idea that a word is known by its contexts is older than the computers that count them. Zellig Harris argued in 1954 that differences of meaning go with differences of distribution, and J. R. Firth wrote in 1957 that "you shall know a word by the company it keeps". Kenneth Church and Patrick Hanks brought pointwise mutual information to lexicography in 1990 as the association ratio. At IBM, Peter Brown, Peter deSouza, Robert Mercer, Vincent Della Pietra and Jenifer Lai grouped words into classes for n-gram models, published in *Computational Linguistics* in 1992; the hierarchy their greedy merges build is now called Brown clustering.

Word senses and taxonomies had their own line. Michael Lesk compared dictionary glosses in 1986, with the pine cone and ice cream cone of his title. Marti Hearst listed six lexical patterns for hyponyms at COLING in 1992. Philip Resnik scored the similarity of two concepts by the information content of their common subsumer in 1995, and David Yarowsky bootstrapped a sense classifier from a few seed words in the same year, using the "one sense per discourse" rule he had studied with William Gale and Church.

Vectors learned by prediction came with neural language models. Yoshua Bengio and colleagues trained word vectors as part of a language model in 2003, and Frederic Morin and Bengio proposed the hierarchical softmax in 2005 to make it faster. In 2013 Tomas Mikolov and colleagues at Google published CBOW and the skip-gram model, then negative sampling, with the word2vec tool. Jeffrey Pennington, Richard Socher and Christopher Manning answered in 2014 with GloVe, a weighted fit to log counts. Omer Levy and Yoav Goldberg showed the same year that skip-gram with negative sampling, under their assumptions, factorizes a shifted PMI matrix, which tied the two traditions together. Piotr Bojanowski and colleagues added character n-grams in 2017, and Manaal Faruqui and colleagues fitted vectors to a lexicon by retrofitting in 2015.

Maps between languages and tests of bias followed. Mikolov, Quoc Le and Ilya Sutskever fitted a linear map between the vector spaces of two languages in 2013; Chao Xing and colleagues made it orthogonal in 2015, and Alexis Conneau and colleagues added CSLS against hubs in 2018. Tolga Bolukbasi and colleagues found gender analogies in word2vec vectors and proposed hard debiasing in 2016, and Aylin Caliskan, Joanna Bryson and Arvind Narayanan measured such associations with the WEAT in 2017.

For longer text, Le and Mikolov trained paragraph vectors in 2014, Matt Kusner and colleagues defined the word mover's distance in 2015, and Sanjeev Arora, Yingyu Liang and Tengyu Ma gave the SIF baseline in 2017. Contextual vectors came with ELMo (Matthew Peters and colleagues, 2018). Nils Reimers and Iryna Gurevych trained Sentence-BERT in 2019, and Tianyu Gao, Xingcheng Yao and Danqi Chen showed with SimCSE in 2021 that dropout noise alone gives useful positive pairs for contrastive training.

## Further reading

- Daniel Jurafsky and James H. Martin, *Speech and Language Processing*, third edition draft. The chapter on vector semantics and embeddings covers PPMI, word2vec and the evaluation of word vectors.
- Peter D. Turney and Patrick Pantel, "From frequency to meaning: vector space models of semantics", *Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research* 37, 2010. A survey of count-based word, context and pair matrices before word2vec.
- Omer Levy, Yoav Goldberg and Ido Dagan, "Improving distributional similarity with lessons learned from word embeddings", *Transactions of the ACL* 3, 2015. Compares count and prediction methods with the same settings and introduces context smoothing.
- Sebastian Ruder, Ivan Vulić and Anders Søgaard, "A survey of cross-lingual word embedding models", *Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research* 65, 2019. Mapping methods, joint training and their evaluation.
- Roberto Navigli, "Word sense disambiguation: a survey", *ACM Computing Surveys* 41(2), 2009. Knowledge-based, supervised and unsupervised methods for senses.
