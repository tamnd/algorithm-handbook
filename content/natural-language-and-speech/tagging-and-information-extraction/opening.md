Take the sentence "Jordan visited Paris on Monday, and he met the mayor there." A tagger marks Jordan as a proper noun and visited as a verb. A named entity recognizer marks Jordan as a person and Paris as a place. A coreference resolver decides that "he" is Jordan and "there" is Paris. An entity linker decides which Jordan this is, and a relation extractor records the fact visited(Jordan, Paris). Each step adds a layer of structure to the text, and together they turn sentences into records that a program can search and count.

Most of these tasks have the same form: give each token a label, so that the labels together are a sequence with constraints between neighbours. A tag depends on the word, and also on the tags around it: "can" is a verb after "they" and a noun after "the". So the methods are sequence models. A hidden Markov model generates the words from the tags, and discriminative models score the tags from features of the whole sentence. When the score splits into terms over neighbouring tags, the Viterbi algorithm finds the best tag sequence exactly, in time linear in the length. Coreference and relation extraction need more than one label per token: they group mentions into entities and connect pairs of entities.

The chapter mixes exact algorithms and measured methods. Viterbi decoding is exact for HMMs and linear-chain CRFs, and the perceptron makes a bounded number of mistakes on separable data. Accuracy and F1 on standard test sets are measurements, and finding the best clustering of mentions under pairwise scores is NP-hard.
