## History

Warren Weaver's memorandum of 1949 already proposed to treat a foreign text as a coded message. The statistical approach in this chapter began at IBM, where the speech group of Jelinek and Mercer applied the source-channel model of speech recognition to French and English (Brown and colleagues 1990). Sentence alignment of the Canadian parliamentary record came first: Brown, Lai and Mercer and Gale and Church gave length-based methods at the same meeting in 1991, and Gale and Church published their program in full in 1993. The same year Brown, Della Pietra, Della Pietra and Mercer described the five IBM translation models, trained by EM from Model 1 upward. Vogel, Ney and Tillmann (1996) added the HMM alignment model, Knight (1999) showed that decoding even with Model 1 and a bigram language model is NP-complete, and Och and Ney (2000, 2003) defined the alignment error rate and compared the models and the symmetrization heuristics. Fraser and Marcu (2007) later showed that a lower AER need not give better translations, and Dyer, Chahuneau and Smith (2013) replaced Model 2's distortion table with one parameter in fast_align.

Phrases replaced words as the unit of translation. Och, Tillmann and Ney (1999) used alignment templates, and Koehn, Och and Marcu (2003) gave the simple phrase-based model with phrase pairs read off word alignments by the consistency rule. Och and Ney (2002) recast the noisy channel as a log-linear model with the channel and the language model as two of its features, and Och (2003) tuned its weights for the evaluation score by minimum error rate training. Koehn (2004) described the stack decoder of Pharaoh, which the Moses toolkit (2007) kept; Tillmann (2004) and Koehn and colleagues (2005) added lexicalized reordering.

Synchronous grammars are older than statistical translation. Lewis and Stearns (1968) and Aho and Ullman (1969) defined syntax-directed translation for compilers, and Wu (1997) brought the binary form to translation as inversion transduction grammar. Chiang (2005, 2007) learned synchronous rules with gaps from word-aligned text without a parser and decoded them with a chart and cube pruning, which adapts the lazy $k$-best algorithm of Huang and Chiang (2005). Galley, Hopkins, Knight and Marcu (2004) extracted rules from target-side trees instead.

Automatic evaluation made fast development possible. Papineni, Roukos, Ward and Zhu (2002) defined BLEU, Snover and colleagues (2006) defined TER for the GALE evaluations, and Popović (2015) proposed chrF. Post (2018) argued for reporting scores with a fixed tokenization, and Rei, Stewart, Farinha and Lavie (2020) trained COMET on human judgments.

Neural translation took over from about 2015. Cho and colleagues and Sutskever, Vinyals and Le (2014) translated with recurrent encoder-decoder models, Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio (2015) added attention, and Luong, Pham and Manning (2015) compared simpler scores. In 2016 Sennrich, Haddow and Birch used back-translation of monolingual text, Kim and Rush distilled models at the sequence level, and Shen and colleagues trained for the expected loss of the metric. Johnson and colleagues (2017) trained one model for many language pairs, and Gu and colleagues (2018) produced all target words at once in non-autoregressive translation, refined by mask-predict in Ghazvininejad, Levy, Liu and Zettlemoyer (2019).

## Further reading

- Philipp Koehn, *Statistical Machine Translation* (Cambridge University Press, 2010). The standard textbook on word alignment, phrase-based and tree-based models, decoding, tuning and evaluation.
- Philipp Koehn, *Neural Machine Translation* (Cambridge University Press, 2020). The successor volume, from encoder-decoder models and attention to back-translation and multilingual models.
- Peter F. Brown, Stephen A. Della Pietra, Vincent J. Della Pietra and Robert L. Mercer, "The Mathematics of Statistical Machine Translation: Parameter Estimation", *Computational Linguistics* 19(2), 1993. The five IBM models with their EM training, worked out in full.
- Franz Josef Och and Hermann Ney, "A Systematic Comparison of Various Statistical Alignment Models", *Computational Linguistics* 29(1), 2003. The HMM and IBM models side by side, with the alignment error rate and symmetrization.
- David Chiang, "Hierarchical Phrase-Based Translation", *Computational Linguistics* 33(2), 2007. Synchronous rules with gaps, chart decoding and cube pruning.
- Daniel Jurafsky and James H. Martin, *Speech and Language Processing* (third edition draft). The chapter on machine translation gives a short modern account with encoder-decoder models and evaluation.
