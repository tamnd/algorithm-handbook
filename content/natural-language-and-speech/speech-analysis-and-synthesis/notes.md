## History

The model behind the chapter is older than digital speech. Tsutomu Chiba and Masato Kajiyama described the vocal tract as a tube with resonances in 1941, and Gunnar Fant's *Acoustic Theory of Speech Production* (1960) set out the source, the tract filter and the radiation as separate factors. Stanley Smith Stevens, John Volkmann and Edwin Newman published the mel scale from listening tests in 1937. A. Michael Noll's cepstral pitch detector (1967) and the phase vocoder of James Flanagan and Roger Golden (1966) were early computer methods for analysing speech. Lawrence Rabiner and Marvin Sambur gave their endpoint detector from energy and zero crossings in 1975.

The features used for recognition settled around 1980. Steven Davis and Paul Mermelstein compared parametric representations for word recognition and found the mel-frequency cepstrum best; their paper fixed the filter bank and the DCT that MFCCs still use. Hynek Hermansky added perceptual linear prediction in 1990, and with Nelson Morgan the RASTA filter in 1994, to make features less sensitive to the channel. On the side of enhancement, Yariv Ephraim and David Malah derived their spectral amplitude estimator in 1984, and Jongseo Sohn, Nam Soo Kim and Wonyong Sung built a voice activity detector on the same Gaussian model in 1999. David Talkin published RAPT in 1995; Matthias Mauch and Simon Dixon made YIN probabilistic as pYIN in 2014.

Coding and synthesis grew together. Manfred Schroeder and Bishnu Atal described code-excited linear prediction in 1985, and it became the basis of many speech codecs, including those of mobile phones. Dennis Klatt published his cascade and parallel formant synthesizer in 1980, and his work led to the voices of MITalk and DECtalk. Eric Moulines and Francis Charpentier described PSOLA in 1990, which made concatenation of recorded units practical. Andrew Hunt and Alan Black stated unit selection as a search over target and join costs in 1996. The statistical approach followed: Takayoshi Yoshimura, Keiichi Tokuda and colleagues built HMM-based synthesis with tied context-dependent models in 1999, and the same group published the parameter generation algorithm with dynamic features in 2000. Maximilian Bisani and Hermann Ney gave the joint-sequence model for grapheme-to-phoneme conversion in 2008.

Neural synthesis began with WaveNet in 2016, from Aäron van den Oord and colleagues at DeepMind, which modelled the waveform one sample at a time. Tacotron (Yuxuan Wang and colleagues, 2017) learned to map characters to spectrogram frames with attention. FastSpeech (Yi Ren and colleagues, 2019) replaced attention with explicit durations and produced all frames at once, and HiFi-GAN (Jungil Kong, Jaehyeon Kim and Jaekyoung Bae, 2020) did the same for the waveform. VITS (Jaehyeon Kim, Jungil Kong and Juhee Son, 2021) joined the two stages. In 2023 Chengyi Wang and colleagues treated synthesis as language modelling over the tokens of a neural codec.

## Further reading

- Lawrence R. Rabiner and Ronald W. Schafer, *Theory and Applications of Digital Speech Processing* (Pearson, 2010). The standard text on short-time analysis, linear prediction, the cepstrum and pitch detection.
- Philipos C. Loizou, *Speech Enhancement: Theory and Practice* (CRC Press, 2nd edition, 2013). Spectral subtraction, Wiener and Ephraim-Malah estimators and voice activity detection, with derivations.
- Paul Taylor, *Text-to-Speech Synthesis* (Cambridge University Press, 2009). The whole synthesis pipeline from text normalization to unit selection.
- Heiga Zen, Keiichi Tokuda and Alan W. Black, "Statistical parametric speech synthesis", *Speech Communication* 51(11) (Elsevier, 2009). A review of HMM synthesis, parameter generation and its weaknesses.
- Xuedong Huang, Alex Acero and Hsiao-Wuen Hon, *Spoken Language Processing* (Prentice Hall, 2001). A broad reference that covers signal processing, recognition and synthesis in one volume.
