## History

The first recognizers matched templates. Taras Vintsyuk applied dynamic programming to the alignment of spoken words in 1968, and Hiroaki Sakoe and Seibi Chiba published their symmetric warping with a band in 1978. In the same years Frederick Jelinek's group at IBM treated recognition as decoding a noisy channel; Lalit Bahl, Jelinek and Robert Mercer set out this maximum likelihood approach in 1983, with stack decoding as its search. Bruce Lowerre's Harpy system (1976) introduced the beam search that most later decoders use.

Hidden Markov models with Gaussian mixtures became the standard in the 1980s, and Lawrence Rabiner's tutorial of 1989 spread them widely. Steve Young, N. H. Russell and J. H. S. Thornton described token passing in 1989, and Hermann Ney and colleagues organized time-synchronous search over a lexical tree in 1992. Young, Julian Odell and Philip Woodland tied triphone states with phonetic decision trees in 1994. The same years brought speaker adaptation: MAP adaptation by Jean-Luc Gauvain and Chin-Hui Lee (1994) and MLLR by Christopher Leggetter and Woodland (1995). Bahl, Peter Brown, Peter de Souza and Mercer proposed MMI training in 1986. Mehryar Mohri, Fernando Pereira and Michael Riley recast the decoding graph as weighted transducers from 1996, with Mohri's determinization and weight pushing in 1997.

Neural networks entered through the hybrid model of Hervé Bourlard and Nelson Morgan (1994), which used a network's posteriors divided by priors as HMM likelihoods. Deep networks made the hybrid the leading method around 2012. Alex Graves and colleagues introduced CTC in 2006 and Graves the RNN transducer in 2012, and Awni Hannun and colleagues gave CTC prefix beam search in 2014. Listen, Attend and Spell (William Chan and colleagues, 2016) applied attention to recognition, and Daniel Povey and colleagues trained hybrid models without lattices by LF-MMI in 2016. The Conformer of Anmol Gulati and colleagues and the self-supervised wav2vec 2.0 of Alexei Baevski and colleagues both appeared in 2020.

Speaker recognition followed its own line, driven by the NIST evaluations, for which Alvin Martin, George Doddington and colleagues introduced the DET curve in 1997. Douglas Reynolds, Thomas Quatieri and Robert Dunn described GMM-UBM verification in 2000. Najim Dehak and colleagues reduced each recording to an i-vector in 2011, scored with the PLDA model of Sergey Ioffe and of Simon Prince and James Elder. David Snyder and colleagues replaced the i-vector with a network embedding, the x-vector, in 2018.

## Further reading

- Lawrence R. Rabiner, "A tutorial on hidden Markov models and selected applications in speech recognition", *Proceedings of the IEEE* 77(2) (IEEE, 1989). The classic account of the three HMM problems and their algorithms.
- Frederick Jelinek, *Statistical Methods for Speech Recognition* (MIT Press, 1997). The source-channel view of recognition from the IBM group, with search and language models.
- Mark Gales and Steve Young, "The application of hidden Markov models in speech recognition", *Foundations and Trends in Signal Processing* 1(3) (now Publishers, 2008). A survey of the mature GMM-HMM system: tying, adaptation, discriminative training and search.
- Mehryar Mohri, Fernando Pereira and Michael Riley, "Weighted finite-state transducers in speech recognition", *Computer Speech and Language* 16(1) (Elsevier, 2002). Composition, determinization and minimization applied to the decoding graph.
- Tomi Kinnunen and Haizhou Li, "An overview of text-independent speaker recognition: from features to supervectors", *Speech Communication* 52(1) (Elsevier, 2010). Features, GMM-UBM, supervectors and scoring for speaker recognition.
