## History

Two traditions of syntax meet in this chapter. Phrase-structure trees come from the context-free grammars of formal language theory; dependency trees come from an older line of grammar that links each word to the word it depends on. Statistical parsing began when the Penn Treebank of Marcus, Santorini and Marcinkiewicz (1993) gave about a million words of hand-checked English trees. Charniak (1996) read a grammar straight off it and showed that such a treebank grammar parses tolerably well. Magerman (1995) passed head words up the trees with a head table, which Collins revised; the head-driven models of Collins (1997, 1999) and Charniak (1997) put words into the grammar, and Eisner and Satta (1999) showed how to parse such bilexical grammars faster than the naive $O(n^5)$.

Unlexicalized refinements followed. Johnson (1998) showed that marking each node with its parent's label helps a treebank grammar, and Klein and Manning (2003) pushed hand-made splits much further. Matsuzaki, Miyao and Tsujii (2005) and Petrov, Barrett, Thibaux and Klein (2006) learned the splits by EM instead. Klein and Manning (2003) also gave A* parsing with consistent outside estimates. Neural encoders then replaced the grammar: Stern, Andreas and Klein (2017) scored spans directly and kept the cubic chart, and Kitaev and Klein (2018) added a self-attentive encoder.

Dependency parsing grew alongside. Eisner (1996) gave the cubic algorithm for projective trees, and McDonald, Pereira, Ribarov and Hajič (2005) noticed that the best non-projective tree under arc scores is a maximum spanning arborescence, found by the algorithm of Chu, Liu and Edmonds. McDonald and Pereira (2006) showed that richer factorizations make the non-projective problem hard. The transition-based line started with Yamada and Matsumoto (2003) and Nivre (2003, 2004), whose arc-standard and arc-eager systems Nivre (2008) surveyed; Nivre (2009) added the swap move, and Goldberg and Nivre (2012, 2013) gave dynamic oracles. Chen and Manning (2014) replaced the feature templates by a small neural network, and Dozat and Manning (2017) did the same for graph-based parsing with biaffine arc scores.

Lexicalized grammar formalisms are older. Joshi, Levy and Takahashi (1975) defined tree adjunct grammars, and Steedman developed combinatory categorial grammar; Vijay-Shanker and Weir (1994) proved that the two, with head grammars and linear indexed grammars, describe the same languages. Bangalore and Joshi (1999) called the step of choosing lexical categories supertagging, "almost parsing", and Clark and Curran (2007) built a wide-coverage CCG parser on it. Klein and Manning (2004) made unsupervised dependency induction work with the dependency model with valence.

The semantic side of parsing took shape with Gildea and Jurafsky (2002), who learned to label semantic roles, and with PropBank (Palmer, Gildea and Kingsbury 2005). Zettlemoyer and Collins (2005) learned CCG semantic parsers from sentences paired with logical forms. Banarescu and colleagues (2013) defined Abstract Meaning Representation, and Cai and Knight (2013) gave Smatch to compare such graphs. Large corpora such as SNLI (Bowman and colleagues 2015) made natural language inference a standard benchmark, and Gururangan and colleagues (2018) showed how much of it a model can solve from the hypothesis alone. Rhetorical structure theory goes back to Mann and Thompson (1988).

## Further reading

- Daniel Jurafsky and James H. Martin, *Speech and Language Processing* (third edition draft). The chapters on constituency grammars, parsing, dependency parsing and semantic roles cover most of this chapter at textbook pace.
- Sandra Kübler, Ryan McDonald and Joakim Nivre, *Dependency Parsing* (2009). A short book on dependency trees, graph-based and transition-based parsing, with the algorithms in full.
- Joakim Nivre, "Algorithms for Deterministic Incremental Dependency Parsing", *Computational Linguistics* 34(4), 2008. The common frame for the transition systems, with proofs of their soundness and completeness.
- Michael Collins, "Head-Driven Statistical Models for Natural Language Parsing", *Computational Linguistics* 29(4), 2003. The lexicalized PCFG in detail, with its head rules and estimation.
- Christopher D. Manning and Hinrich Schütze, *Foundations of Statistical Natural Language Processing* (1999). The probabilistic grammar chapters remain a careful account of PCFGs, treebanks and evaluation.
- Mark Steedman, *The Syntactic Process* (2000). The book-length account of combinatory categorial grammar.
