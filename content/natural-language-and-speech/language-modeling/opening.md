A language model gives a probability to every sentence, or, by the chain rule, to each next token given the tokens before it. After "I would like a cup of", the word "tea" should get a high probability and "elephant" a low one. Speech recognizers and translation systems use such a model to choose among candidate outputs, text generators sample from it, and the large pretrained models of today are language models first. The chapter asks two questions about every method: does it give a proper distribution, and how well does it predict text it has not seen? The second is measured by perplexity on held-out text.

The chapter follows two lines. The first counts n-grams. Maximum likelihood gives zero to every n-gram the training text missed, and a smoothing method is an estimator that moves some probability to those events while keeping the total at 1; modified Kneser-Ney had the lowest perplexity in the comparison of Chen and Goodman. The second line is neural: a network maps the history to a distribution, first with a fixed window, then with a recurrent state, and then with attention in the transformer. The definitions and the exactness results here are theorems, for example that FlashAttention computes the same output as standard attention. What large models can do, and how their loss falls with size, is measured.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in seven groups, read in order.

1. **Language models and their measure.** The [language model](/language-model/) as a distribution over sentences, [perplexity](/perplexity/) as the exponential of the cross-entropy per token, and the [n-gram language model](/n-gram-language-model/).
2. **Smoothing n-gram counts.** [Laplace smoothing](/laplace-smoothing/), [Good-Turing estimation](/good-turing-estimation/) and [simple Good-Turing](/simple-good-turing/), [absolute discounting](/absolute-discounting/), [Katz backoff](/katz-backoff/), [deleted interpolation](/deleted-interpolation/), [Witten-Bell smoothing](/witten-bell-smoothing/), [Kneser-Ney smoothing](/kneser-ney-smoothing/) and [modified Kneser-Ney](/modified-kneser-ney-smoothing/).
3. **Large n-gram models and adaptation.** [Stupid backoff](/stupid-backoff/), which gives scores rather than probabilities, [entropy-based pruning](/entropy-based-ngram-pruning/), the [cache language model](/cache-language-model/) and the [maximum entropy language model](/maximum-entropy-language-model/).
4. **Neural language models.** The [neural probabilistic language model](/neural-probabilistic-language-model/) of Bengio, the [recurrent neural network language model](/recurrent-neural-network-language-model/), and [noise-contrastive estimation](/noise-contrastive-estimation/), which trains without the full softmax.
5. **Attention and the transformer.** [Scaled dot-product attention](/scaled-dot-product-attention/), [multi-head attention](/multi-head-attention/), [sinusoidal positional encoding](/sinusoidal-positional-encoding/) and the [transformer](/transformer/); then [rotary position embedding](/rotary-position-embedding/), [ALiBi](/alibi-attention-bias/), [grouped-query attention](/grouped-query-attention/), [FlashAttention](/flashattention/) and the [selective state space model](/selective-state-space-model/).
6. **Pretraining.** [Causal language modeling](/causal-language-modeling/), [masked language modeling](/masked-language-modeling/), [BERT](/bert/), [replaced token detection](/replaced-token-detection/), the [generative pretrained transformer](/generative-pretrained-transformer/) and the [neural scaling law](/neural-scaling-law/).
7. **Using and aligning pretrained models.** [In-context learning](/in-context-learning/), [chain-of-thought prompting](/chain-of-thought-prompting/), [instruction tuning](/instruction-tuning/), the [preference reward model](/preference-reward-model/), [reinforcement learning from human feedback](/reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedback/), [direct preference optimization](/direct-preference-optimization/) and [low-rank adaptation](/low-rank-adaptation/).

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these twelve entries in order: language model, perplexity, n-gram language model, Laplace smoothing, Good-Turing estimation, absolute discounting, Kneser-Ney smoothing, scaled dot-product attention, multi-head attention, transformer, causal language modeling and masked language modeling. They are marked *Intro* or *Core*. Work the smoothing methods by hand on a corpus of three or four short sentences, and check that every row of each model sums to 1; then compute one attention step for three tokens. Katz backoff, Witten-Bell and deleted interpolation are short once Good-Turing and absolute discounting are clear. The entries marked *Research*, from FlashAttention to direct preference optimization, can wait for a second pass.
