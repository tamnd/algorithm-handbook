## History

Claude Shannon approximated English by sources that choose each letter or word from the few before it in 1948, and measured the entropy of printed English by asking people to guess the next letter in 1951. Statistical language models came into practice with speech recognition at IBM in the 1970s, where Frederick Jelinek and colleagues used trigram models as the prior of a noisy-channel recognizer. Jelinek and Robert Mercer gave interpolation with weights fitted on held-out data in 1980, and Slava Katz gave backoff with Good-Turing discounts in 1987. The Good-Turing estimate itself goes back to I. J. Good's paper of 1953, built on an idea of Alan Turing from their wartime codebreaking; William Gale and Geoffrey Sampson made it usable as simple Good-Turing in 1995.

The 1990s refined smoothing and added other evidence. Ian Witten and Timothy Bell studied the chance of a novel symbol for text compression in 1991, and their estimate became a smoothing method. Reinhard Kneser and Hermann Ney introduced continuation counts at ICASSP 1995. Stanley Chen and Joshua Goodman compared the methods by perplexity in 1998 and 1999 and introduced modified Kneser-Ney, which did best in their tests. Roland Kuhn and Renato De Mori added a cache of recent words in 1990, Ronald Rosenfeld combined n-grams and triggers in a maximum entropy model in 1996, and Andreas Stolcke pruned backoff models by relative entropy in 1998. Thorsten Brants and colleagues at Google trained n-gram models on web text with stupid backoff in 2007.

Neural models began with Yoshua Bengio and colleagues, whose model learned word vectors and a next-word distribution together (first version 2000, journal paper 2003). Tomas Mikolov and colleagues used a recurrent network as a language model for speech recognition in 2010. Michael Gutmann and Aapo Hyvärinen proposed noise-contrastive estimation in 2010, and Andriy Mnih and Yee Whye Teh applied it to language models in 2012.

The transformer of Ashish Vaswani and colleagues (2017) replaced recurrence by attention. In 2018 and 2019 Alec Radford and colleagues pretrained decoder-only transformers (GPT and GPT-2), and Jacob Devlin and colleagues pretrained the encoder BERT by masked language modeling; Kevin Clark and colleagues proposed replaced token detection in 2020. Tom Brown and colleagues measured in-context learning with GPT-3 in 2020. Jared Kaplan and colleagues fitted scaling laws the same year, and Jordan Hoffmann and colleagues fitted a different law and trained Chinchilla in 2022. Later work changed the parts of the layer: rotary positions (Jianlin Su and colleagues, 2021), ALiBi (Ofir Press, Noah Smith and Mike Lewis, 2022), FlashAttention (Tri Dao and colleagues, 2022), grouped-query attention (Joshua Ainslie and colleagues, 2023) and the selective state space model of Albert Gu and Dao (2023, published 2024).

Learning from preferences came from reinforcement learning. Paul Christiano and colleagues fitted a reward model to human comparisons in 2017, and Long Ouyang and colleagues used the method with instruction tuning for InstructGPT in 2022, the year Jason Wei and colleagues published FLAN and chain-of-thought prompting. Edward Hu and colleagues proposed LoRA in 2021, and Rafael Rafailov and colleagues showed in 2023 that the same objective can be fitted with one classification loss, as DPO.

## Further reading

- Daniel Jurafsky and James H. Martin, *Speech and Language Processing*, third edition draft. The chapters on n-gram models, neural networks and transformers cover the chapter at an introductory level.
- Stanley F. Chen and Joshua Goodman, "An empirical study of smoothing techniques for language modeling", *Computer Speech and Language* 13(4), 1999. The standard comparison of smoothing methods, with modified Kneser-Ney.
- Joshua T. Goodman, "A bit of progress in language modeling", *Computer Speech and Language* 15(4), 2001. Measured gains of caches, clustering, skipping and sentence mixture models over Kneser-Ney.
- Ashish Vaswani and colleagues, "Attention is all you need", NeurIPS 2017. The paper that defines scaled dot-product attention, multi-head attention and the transformer.
- Jared Kaplan and colleagues, "Scaling laws for neural language models", arXiv:2001.08361, 2020, with Jordan Hoffmann and colleagues, "Training compute-optimal large language models", NeurIPS 2022. The two main sets of fitted laws.
