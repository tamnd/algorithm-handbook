Every man is mortal, and Socrates is a man. To prove that Socrates is mortal, a resolution prover writes the premises and the negated goal as clauses: $\neg \mathit{Man}(x) \vee \mathit{Mortal}(x)$, $\mathit{Man}(s)$ and $\neg \mathit{Mortal}(s)$. It notices that $\mathit{Mortal}(x)$ and $\mathit{Mortal}(s)$ become the same atom under the substitution $x \mapsto s$, and it cuts the two clauses on that atom to get $\neg \mathit{Man}(s)$. One more cut against $\mathit{Man}(s)$ leaves the empty clause, a contradiction, so the goal follows. The prover never chose a value for $x$ by trying candidates. It computed the one substitution that the step needed, and nothing more.

Two ideas carry this proof. Unification finds the most general substitution that makes two terms equal, and resolution combines two clauses that disagree on a literal. Robinson joined them in 1965, and the result is complete: every unsatisfiable set of clauses has a refutation. It is not a decision procedure, since first-order validity is undecidable; on a satisfiable set the search may run forever. The chapter follows both ideas: the algorithms that compute unifiers and how fast they run, the strategies that keep resolution complete while cutting its search, Prolog, which is resolution run as a program, and the unification problems that arise when terms carry equations or bound variables.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in nine groups, read in order.

1. **Clauses and normal forms.** [Prenex form](/prenex-normal-form/), [Skolemization](/skolemization/) and [clausal normal form](/clausal-normal-form/) turn a first-order formula into clauses, and a [refutation](/refutation/) proves a goal by refuting its negation.
2. **Substitutions and unifiers.** [Substitutions](/substitution/), [unifiers](/unifier/), the [most general unifier](/most-general-unifier/), the [occurs check](/occurs-check/), and the [theorem](/mgu-existence-theorem/) that a most general unifier exists whenever any unifier does.
3. **Unification algorithms.** [Robinson's algorithm](/robinson-unification/), the [Martelli-Montanari](/martelli-montanari-unification/) rules, Huet's almost-linear algorithm on a [term DAG](/term-dag/), and the linear algorithm of [Paterson and Wegman](/paterson-wegman-unification/).
4. **Resolution and its completeness.** [Binary resolution](/binary-resolution/) with [factoring](/factoring/), [Herbrand's theorem](/herbrand-theorem/), the [lifting lemma](/lifting-lemma/) and the [completeness theorem](/robinson-completeness-theorem/).
5. **Restricting the search.** [Unit](/unit-resolution/), [input](/input-resolution/) and [linear](/linear-resolution/) resolution, the [set of support](/set-of-support/), [hyperresolution](/hyperresolution/), [ordered resolution](/ordered-resolution/), and the [subsumption](/subsumption/) and [indexing](/term-indexing/) that make provers fast.
6. **Logic programming.** [SLD resolution](/sld-resolution/), [answer substitutions](/answer-substitution/), [negation as failure](/sldnf-resolution/) and the [Warren Abstract Machine](/warren-abstract-machine/).
7. **Equality.** [Paramodulation](/paramodulation/), [demodulation](/demodulation/) and [superposition](/superposition/).
8. **Variations of first-order unification.** [Anti-unification](/anti-unification-problem/), [unification modulo a theory](/equational-unification/), [AC unification](/ac-unification-problem/) and [semi-unification](/semi-unification-problem/).
9. **Unification with binders.** [Higher-order unification](/higher-order-unification-problem/), its decidable [pattern fragment](/pattern-unification-problem/), [Huet's pre-unification](/huet-pre-unification/) and [nominal unification](/nominal-unification-problem/).

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these twelve entries in order: [Skolemization](/skolemization/), [clausal normal form](/clausal-normal-form/), [substitution](/substitution/), [most general unifier](/most-general-unifier/), [occurs check](/occurs-check/), [Robinson unification](/robinson-unification/), [binary resolution](/binary-resolution/), [factoring](/factoring/), [Herbrand's theorem](/herbrand-theorem/), [the completeness theorem](/robinson-completeness-theorem/), [set of support](/set-of-support/) and [SLD resolution](/sld-resolution/). They give the whole path from a formula to a proof, and the link to Prolog. The faster unification algorithms, the equality rules and the last two groups can wait until you need them; the binder group in particular leans on the [lambda calculus](/lambda-calculus/) and is the hardest part of the chapter.
