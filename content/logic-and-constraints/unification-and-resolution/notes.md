## History

Herbrand's thesis of 1930 reduced first-order unsatisfiability to a test on finite sets of ground instances, and Church and Turing showed in 1936 that no procedure decides validity. The first provers, by Gilmore and by Davis and Putnam in 1960, generated ground instances level by level and drowned in them. Prawitz proposed in 1960 to delay the choice of instances and to compute them by solving equations between terms. John Alan Robinson made this one rule in 1965: resolution with a most general unifier, with factoring, together with the unification algorithm and hyperresolution, and he proved the rule sound and refutation complete. In the same year Wos, Robinson and Carson added the set of support, and the Argonne group built the line of provers that led to Otter and Prover9.

The next decade restricted the search. Chang (1970) showed that unit and input resolution refute the same sets, Loveland and Luckham (1970) proved linear resolution complete, and Henschen and Wos (1974) showed that unit resolution is complete for Horn sets. George Robinson and Wos introduced paramodulation in 1969; Brand (1975) and Peterson (1983) removed the need for the functional reflexivity axioms. Ordered forms of paramodulation followed in the 1980s, and Bachmair and Ganzinger presented superposition in 1990, with the theory of redundancy that lets a prover delete clauses and stay complete. That calculus is the core of E, SPASS and Vampire.

Logic programming came from the same rule. Kowalski (1974) read Horn clauses as procedures, Colmerauer and Roussel ran them in Prolog (1972 and 1973), and Hill (1974) proved the completeness of linear resolution with selection for Horn clauses. Van Emden and Kowalski (1976) showed that the operational, model and fixed-point meanings agree, Clark (1978) gave negation as failure its meaning through the completion, and Apt and van Emden (1982) named SLD resolution. David H. D. Warren described his abstract machine in 1983, and later Prolog systems were built on it or defined by how they depart from it.

Unification became a subject of its own. Paterson and Wegman (1978) gave a linear algorithm, Huet's thesis (1976) an almost-linear one with union-find, and Martelli and Montanari (1982) the rule system that most textbooks now use. Plotkin and Reynolds found the least general generalization in 1970, and Plotkin (1972) showed that associativity has infinite complete sets of unifiers. Stickel (1975, in a journal in 1981) gave AC unification, and Fages proved that it terminates. For higher-order terms, Lucchesi (1972) and Huet (1973) proved undecidability from third order, Huet gave pre-unification in 1975, and Goldfarb (1981) proved second-order unification undecidable. Miller's patterns (1991) and the nominal unification of Urban, Pitts and Gabbay (2003 and 2004) found decidable fragments with most general unifiers, and Kfoury, Tiuryn and Urzyczyn (1990) proved semi-unification undecidable.

## Further reading

- J. A. Robinson, "A machine-oriented logic based on the resolution principle", *Journal of the ACM* 12(1), 1965. The original paper, which gives unification, resolution and the completeness proof in a few pages.
- Chin-Liang Chang and Richard Char-Tung Lee, *Symbolic Logic and Mechanical Theorem Proving*, Academic Press, 1973. The classic textbook on Herbrand's theorem, resolution and its refinements, with many worked examples.
- Alan Robinson and Andrei Voronkov (editors), *Handbook of Automated Reasoning*, Elsevier and MIT Press, 2001. The reference for the modern theory: resolution, superposition, term indexing, and Baader and Snyder's chapter on unification theory.
- John W. Lloyd, *Foundations of Logic Programming*, second edition, Springer, 1987. The standard account of SLD and SLDNF resolution, answers and the completion.
- Franz Baader and Tobias Nipkow, *Term Rewriting and All That*, Cambridge University Press, 1998. A careful treatment of syntactic and equational unification on the way to rewriting and completion.
