## History

The recursion is old. George Boole stated the expansion of a function by one variable in *An Investigation of the Laws of Thought* (1854), and Claude Shannon used it in 1938 to analyze relay circuits; it now carries Shannon's name. C. Y. Lee described switching circuits by binary-decision programs in 1959, and Sheldon Akers gave binary decision diagrams their name in 1978, as a compact description of a function for testing and design.

The turning point was Randal Bryant's paper *Graph-based algorithms for Boolean function manipulation* of 1986. Bryant fixed one variable order for all paths, reduced the diagrams, and proved that the result is canonical. He gave the algorithms that are still the core of every package: reduction, APPLY with its $O(|f| \cdot |g|)$ bound, restriction, composition and model counting. His paper also showed the weakness that the rest of the field has worked on: the size depends on the order. In 1990 Karl Brace, Richard Rudell and Bryant described a package built around a unique table, a computed table, the ITE operator and complemented edges, and Shin-ichi Minato, Nagisa Ishiura and Shuzo Yajima described attributed edges in the same year. The same year Jerry Burch, Edmund Clarke, Kenneth McMillan, David Dill and L. J. Hwang used BDDs to model check systems with more than $10^{20}$ states.

The order problem drew both theory and engineering. Steven Friedman and Kenneth Supowit gave an exact algorithm over subsets in 1990. Richard Rudell introduced sifting in 1993, and window permutation came from Fujita, Matsunaga and Kakuda and from Ishiura, Sawada and Yajima in 1991. Beate Bollig and Ingo Wegener proved in 1996 that improving an order is NP-complete, after Tani, Hamaguchi and Yajima had done so for shared diagrams. Bryant proved in 1991 that the middle bit of multiplication has exponential size under every order, and the hidden weighted bit function from the same paper became the standard example that separates ordered from free BDDs.

The early 1990s also brought many variants. Multi-valued decision diagrams came from Srinivasan, Kam, Malik and Brayton in 1990. Minato introduced zero-suppressed BDDs in 1993 for sets in combinatorial problems; algebraic decision diagrams (Bahar and others) and multi-terminal BDDs (Clarke, Fujita and others) appeared the same year. Udo Kebschull, Endric Schubert and Wolfgang Rosenstiel introduced functional decision diagrams, based on the positive Davio expansion, in 1992, and Drechsler, Sarabi, Theobald, Becker and Perkowski mixed the three expansions in Kronecker diagrams in 1994. Bryant and Yirng-An Chen made binary moment diagrams in 1995 to verify multipliers. Donald Knuth's section on BDDs in *The Art of Computer Programming* (2011) gathers much of this work in one place.

## Further reading

- Randal E. Bryant, *Graph-based algorithms for Boolean function manipulation*, IEEE Transactions on Computers C-35(8), 1986. The paper that started the field; it is short and still a good first source for the algorithms.
- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 4A: Combinatorial Algorithms, Part 1* (2011). Section 7.1.4 treats BDDs and ZDDs in depth, with exact counts and many exercises.
- Ingo Wegener, *Branching Programs and Binary Decision Diagrams: Theory and Applications*, SIAM (2000). The reference for lower bounds, free BDDs and the complexity of the ordering problem.
- Karl S. Brace, Richard L. Rudell and Randal E. Bryant, *Efficient implementation of a BDD package*, Design Automation Conference (1990). How a real package is organized, with the unique table, the computed table and complemented edges.
- Yves Crama and Peter L. Hammer, *Boolean Functions: Theory, Algorithms, and Applications*, Cambridge University Press (2011). A broad treatment of Boolean functions, for the background of the first group.
