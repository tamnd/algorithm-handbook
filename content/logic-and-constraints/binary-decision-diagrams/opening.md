A chip designer rewrites a circuit to make it faster and must show that the new circuit computes the same function as the old one. For a formula or a circuit this is a co-NP-complete question. If both functions are stored as reduced ordered binary decision diagrams with the same variable order, the answer is a pointer comparison: equal functions have the same diagram. A binary decision diagram is a directed acyclic graph that asks about one variable at each node and ends at 0 or 1. Its ordered and reduced form is canonical, and the operations on it run in time bounded by the product of the sizes of their arguments. This made BDDs the standard data structure for Boolean functions in hardware verification from the late 1980s on, and the engine of the first symbolic model checkers.

The price is that the size of the diagram depends on the order of the variables, sometimes enormously. The function $x_1 x_2 \vee x_3 x_4 \vee x_5 x_6$ has a diagram of 8 nodes in the order $x_1, \ldots, x_6$ and 16 nodes when the odd variables come first, and the gap grows exponentially with the number of terms. For some functions, such as the middle bit of a product, every order gives an exponential diagram. Much of the chapter is about this tension: what makes BDDs fast, and where they stop working.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in seven groups, read in order.

1. **Boolean functions and their cofactors.** The [Boolean function](/boolean-function/), the [cofactor](/cofactor/) obtained by fixing one variable, and the [Shannon expansion](/shannon-expansion/) $f = \bar{x} f|_{x=0} \vee x f|_{x=1}$, which is the recursion behind every algorithm in the chapter.
2. **From decision diagrams to a canonical form.** [Binary decision diagrams](/binary-decision-diagram/) in general, the [ordered](/ordered-binary-decision-diagram/) ones, the two [reduction rules](/bdd-reduction-rules/), and the [reduced ordered BDD](/reduced-ordered-bdd/). The [canonicity theorem](/bdd-canonicity/) says that it is unique for each function and order.
3. **Operations on BDDs.** [APPLY](/bdd-apply/) combines two diagrams with a Boolean operator in $O(|f| \cdot |g|)$ time, thanks to the [computed table](/computed-table/). [ITE](/ite-operator/) does every binary operator at once. Counting models, quantification and composition follow, and [complemented edges](/complemented-edges/) make negation free.
4. **Simplifying with a care set.** [Generalized cofactors](/generalized-cofactor/) use the inputs that do not matter to shrink a diagram; doing this optimally is NP-complete.
5. **The variable order.** The [ordering problem](/variable-ordering/), its NP-completeness, the [sifting](/bdd-sifting/) heuristic that packages use, and an exact algorithm that runs in $O(n^2 3^n)$ time.
6. **Limits and stronger models.** [Free BDDs](/free-binary-decision-diagram/) and two exponential lower bounds for ordered BDDs, for the [hidden weighted bit](/hidden-weighted-bit-lower-bound/) function and for [multiplication](/multiplier-bdd-lower-bound/).
7. **Other decision diagrams.** Diagrams for finite-domain variables, numeric outputs and families of sets, the [zero-suppressed BDD](/zero-suppressed-bdd/) above all, and two diagrams built on other expansions.
