## History

The theories came long before the solvers. Mojżesz Presburger proved in 1929 that the first-order theory of the natural numbers with addition is decidable, by quantifier elimination. Alfred Tarski found a decision method for the real numbers with addition, multiplication and order around 1930 and published it in a RAND report in 1948, with a second edition in 1951. George Collins gave cylindrical algebraic decomposition in 1975, the first method with a doubly exponential bound. Wilhelm Ackermann's book of 1954 on decidable cases of the decision problem contains the reduction that removes function symbols, now called Ackermannization.

The methods for combining theories grew out of program verification around 1980. Greg Nelson and Derek Oppen published their combination method in 1979; their completeness proof missed the need for stable infiniteness, and Oppen gave a correct proof, with the role of convexity, in 1980. In 1980 Nelson and Oppen also gave a congruence closure algorithm, and Peter Downey, Ravi Sethi and Robert Tarjan gave the $O(n \log n)$ method with a signature table. Robert Shostak published his combination method in the Journal of the ACM in 1984. The paper contains serious errors, and Harald Rueß and Natarajan Shankar gave a corrected version with full proofs in 2001. William Pugh presented the omega test in 1991 as an exact test for array dependences in compilers.

The fast SAT solvers of 2001 changed the field. In 2002 the lazy solvers MathSAT, CVC and ICS combined a SAT search with theory conflict clauses and backjumping. Harald Ganzinger, George Hagen, Robert Nieuwenhuis, Albert Oliveras and Cesare Tinelli named DPLL(T) at CAV 2004, joining theory propagation with explanations to a CDCL engine, and Nieuwenhuis, Oliveras and Tinelli gave the abstract rule system in the Journal of the ACM in 2006. In the same year Bruno Dutertre and Leonardo de Moura published the incremental simplex method of the Yices solver. The SMT-LIB initiative started in 2003 and gave solvers a common language, and the yearly competition SMT-COMP compares them on its benchmarks.

Quantifiers and synthesis came next. The Simplify prover of David Detlefs, Greg Nelson and James Saxe (2005) matched triggers in its e-graph to instantiate quantifiers, and de Moura and Nikolaj Bjørner made e-matching efficient for Z3 in 2007. Armando Solar-Lezama and his coauthors introduced counterexample-guided inductive synthesis with program sketching in 2006. Rajeev Alur and others defined syntax-guided synthesis in 2013. In 2012 Sicun Gao, Jeremy Avigad and Edmund Clarke defined δ-complete decision procedures for nonlinear formulas over the reals.

## Further reading

- Daniel Kroening and Ofer Strichman, *Decision Procedures: An Algorithmic Point of View*, second edition (2016). A textbook that builds the decision procedures for equality, arithmetic, bit-vectors and arrays, and the lazy and eager ways of joining them to SAT.
- Clark Barrett, Roberto Sebastiani, Sanjit A. Seshia and Cesare Tinelli, *Satisfiability Modulo Theories*, Chapter 33 of the *Handbook of Satisfiability*, second edition (2021). The standard survey of SMT, with the lazy and eager approaches, theory solvers and combination.
- Aaron R. Bradley and Zohar Manna, *The Calculus of Computation*, first edition (2007). Decision procedures for program verification, with congruence closure, arrays and the Nelson-Oppen method, and full proofs.
- Saugata Basu, Richard Pollack and Marie-Françoise Roy, *Algorithms in Real Algebraic Geometry*, second edition (2006). The algebra behind Tarski's theorem and cylindrical algebraic decomposition.
- Alexander Schrijver, *Theory of Linear and Integer Programming*, first edition (1986). The theory behind linear arithmetic over the reals and the integers.
