A logic formula states what must be true; a constraint states what a solution must satisfy. This volume is about the programs that decide such statements: whether a Boolean formula has a satisfying assignment, whether a set of equations over numbers and functions is consistent, whether values can be given to variables so that every constraint holds, whether one clause set refutes itself, and whether a finite-state system can ever reach a bad state. Most of these questions are NP-hard or worse, and the volume is about the methods that answer them anyway on the instances people meet.

The methods run under a great deal of everyday engineering. Chip makers check new circuits with [SAT](/sat/) solvers, [BDDs](/reduced-ordered-bdd/) and model checkers before it is made. Compilers, static analyzers and program verifiers send their questions to [SMT](/smt-problem/) solvers. Schedulers, timetables and configuration tools are written as [constraint programs](/constraint-programming/). Prolog and type inference run on [unification](/unification-problem/), and the protocols of distributed systems are checked by searching their state spaces with [temporal logic](/temporal-logic/) as the specification language. Entries in other volumes are cited by number, so a prerequisite such as [depth-first search](/depth-first-search/) from Volume 7 can be found at once.

## Why logic and constraints get a volume of their own

The six chapters share a small set of ideas that come back again and again in new dress. In this one volume you will meet:

- **Search with inference at every node.** [DPLL](/dpll/) assigns a variable and then [propagates units](/unit-propagation/); a CSP solver assigns a variable and then [prunes domains](/constraint-propagation/). Cheap inference after each choice is what turns blind backtracking into a usable solver.
- **Learning from failure.** [Conflict-driven clause learning](/cdcl/) turns each dead end into a new clause, and [nogood learning](/nogood-learning/) does the same for constraint problems. A search that remembers why it failed does not fail the same way twice.
- **Resolution as the common proof system.** The clauses a CDCL solver learns are resolvents, so its runs are [resolution proofs](/cdcl-simulates-resolution/), and the [pigeonhole lower bound](/haken-resolution-lower-bound/) limits all of them. In first-order logic, [resolution](/robinson-resolution/) with a [most general unifier](/most-general-unifier/) is the rule behind provers and Prolog.
- **Canonical forms.** A [reduced ordered BDD](/reduced-ordered-bdd/) is unique for each function and variable order, so equivalence becomes a pointer test. [Congruence closure](/congruence-closure/) and [arc consistency](/arc-consistency/) compute unique least or greatest objects too, as fixed points.
- **Fixed points.** Reachable states, CTL formulas, arc-consistent domains and the [least model](/horn-sat/) of a Horn formula are all fixed points of monotone maps, and the [Knaster-Tarski theorem](/knaster-tarski-theorem/) says why they exist.
- **Dividing a problem among solvers.** [DPLL(T)](/dpll-t/) lets a SAT solver handle the Boolean structure and a theory solver the arithmetic; [Nelson-Oppen](/nelson-oppen/) combines theories; [IC3](/ic3-pdr/) and [CEGAR](/cegar/) build proofs of safety out of many small SAT or SMT queries.
- **Sharp lines of difficulty.** [2-SAT](/2-sat/) and [Horn-SAT](/horn-sat/) take linear time and 3-SAT is NP-complete; [Schaefer's theorem](/schaefer-dichotomy-theorem/) and the [CSP dichotomy theorem](/csp-dichotomy-theorem/) say that every problem of this kind falls on one side or the other.

## The chapters

**Chapter 16.1, SAT solving**, starts from [literals](/literal/), [clauses](/clause/) and [conjunctive normal form](/conjunctive-normal-form/), and from the [Tseitin transformation](/tseitin-transformation/) that brings any circuit into it. It gives the tractable cases, 2-SAT, Horn-SAT and [XOR-SAT](/xor-sat/), then the complete solvers: DPLL and CDCL with [first-UIP learning](/first-uip/), [two watched literals](/two-watched-literals/), [VSIDS](/vsids/) and [restarts](/restarts/). It covers local search with [WalkSAT](/walksat/) and [Schöning's algorithm](/schoning-algorithm/), preprocessing, [DRAT proofs](/drat-proof/) of unsatisfiability, [MaxSAT](/max-sat/), the random [satisfiability threshold](/satisfiability-threshold/) and [QBF](/qbf/).

**Chapter 16.2, SMT**, adds theories to SAT. It defines the [SMT problem](/smt-problem/) and the [lazy approach](/lazy-smt/) built on DPLL(T), then the theory solvers: [congruence closure](/congruence-closure/) for equality with free functions, [simplex](/simplex-for-smt/) for linear real arithmetic, the [omega test](/omega-test/) and [Presburger arithmetic](/presburger-arithmetic/) for integers, [bit-blasting](/bit-blasting/) for machine words, and arrays and strings. It explains how theories are combined, how solvers handle quantifiers with [e-matching](/e-matching/), and how SMT is used for optimization and [program synthesis](/cegis/).

**Chapter 16.3, Constraint satisfaction**, defines the [constraint satisfaction problem](/constraint-satisfaction-problem/) and its [constraint graph](/constraint-graph/). It gives the consistency algorithms, from [AC-3](/ac-3/) to [path](/path-consistency/) and [k-consistency](/k-consistency/), and the search that uses them: [forward checking](/forward-checking/), [maintaining arc consistency](/maintaining-arc-consistency/), the [minimum-remaining-values](/minimum-remaining-values/) heuristic and backjumping. It covers [global constraints](/global-constraint/) such as [all-different](/all-different-constraint/), local search with [min-conflicts](/min-conflicts/), [tree-structured problems](/tree-structured-csp/) and the dichotomy theorems.

**Chapter 16.4, Binary decision diagrams**, builds the reduced ordered BDD from the [Shannon expansion](/shannon-expansion/), proves it [canonical](/bdd-canonicity/), and gives [APPLY](/bdd-apply/), [ITE](/ite-operator/) and quantification. It shows how much the [variable order](/variable-ordering/) matters, how [sifting](/bdd-sifting/) improves it, and which functions, such as the [middle bit of multiplication](/multiplier-bdd-lower-bound/), have no small diagram at all. It ends with other decision diagrams, the [zero-suppressed BDD](/zero-suppressed-bdd/) above all.

**Chapter 16.5, Unification and resolution**, moves to first-order logic. It gives [substitutions](/substitution/), the most general unifier and the unification algorithms, then [resolution](/robinson-resolution/) and its completeness, and the restrictions that tame its search. [SLD resolution](/sld-resolution/) is the engine of Prolog. The chapter ends with equality through [paramodulation](/paramodulation/) and [superposition](/superposition/), unification modulo equational theories, and [higher-order unification](/higher-order-unification-undecidability/), which is undecidable.

**Chapter 16.6, Model checking**, verifies finite-state systems against [temporal logic](/temporal-logic/). It gives the [Kripke structure](/kripke-structure/), [CTL](/computation-tree-logic/) and [LTL](/linear-temporal-logic/), the [labelling algorithm](/ctl-labelling-algorithm/) for CTL and the [automata-theoretic approach](/automata-theoretic-approach/) for LTL with [nested depth-first search](/nested-depth-first-search/). It covers the fight against [state explosion](/state-explosion-problem/), by [partial-order reduction](/partial-order-reduction/), by [symbolic model checking](/symbolic-model-checking/) with BDDs, and by SAT in [bounded model checking](/bounded-model-checking/) and IC3. It then turns to abstraction, the [mu-calculus](/modal-mu-calculus/) and [parity games](/parity-game/), and systems with probabilities, clocks and stacks.

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course, read these entries in this order: [SAT](/sat/), [conjunctive normal form](/conjunctive-normal-form/), [2-SAT](/2-sat/), [unit propagation](/unit-propagation/), [DPLL](/dpll/), [CDCL](/cdcl/) and [WalkSAT](/walksat/) in Chapter 16.1; [SMT problem](/smt-problem/), [congruence closure](/congruence-closure/) and [DPLL(T)](/dpll-t/) in Chapter 16.2; [constraint satisfaction problem](/constraint-satisfaction-problem/), [arc consistency](/arc-consistency/), [AC-3](/ac-3/) and [maintaining arc consistency](/maintaining-arc-consistency/) in Chapter 16.3; [reduced ordered BDD](/reduced-ordered-bdd/) and [BDD APPLY](/bdd-apply/) in Chapter 16.4; [most general unifier](/most-general-unifier/) and [resolution](/robinson-resolution/) in Chapter 16.5; and [Kripke structure](/kripke-structure/), [CTL model checking](/ctl-model-checking/) and [LTL model checking](/ltl-model-checking/) in Chapter 16.6. Do the exercises marked ◦ and • at the end of each of these chapters. That is a complete first unit on logic and constraints.

The chapters can be read in other orders. Chapters 16.1, 16.3 and 16.4 stand on their own. Chapter 16.2 needs CDCL from Chapter 16.1, Chapter 16.5 needs clauses from it, and Chapter 16.6 uses BDDs from Chapter 16.4 and SAT from Chapter 16.1. If you know the basics already, start with the opening page of each chapter. It names the groups in the chapter and the order to read them. The entries marked *Advanced* and *Research* are the place to go deeper.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained.

## What you need first

You should be able to read a short program and follow a proof by induction. From Volume 1 you need [asymptotic notation](/asymptotic-notation/), and from Volume 2 [backtracking](/generic-backtracking-algorithm/), the pattern under every complete solver here. From Volume 3 you need [hash tables](/hash-table/) and the [disjoint-set forest](/disjoint-set-forest/). From Volume 7 you need [directed graphs](/directed-graph/), [depth-first search](/depth-first-search/), [strongly connected components](/strongly-connected-components/) and [bipartite matching](/bipartite-matching/). [Terms](/term/) and the [Büchi automaton](/buchi-automaton/) come from Volume 9, and the [simplex method](/simplex-method/) from Volume 15. The hardness results use [NP-completeness](/np-complete/), the [Cook-Levin theorem](/cook-levin-theorem/) and [PSPACE](/pspace/) from Volume 39; you can take them on trust at first.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
