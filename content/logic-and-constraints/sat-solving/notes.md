## History

Satisfiability began as a part of automated theorem proving. Martin Davis and Hilary Putnam published their procedure in 1960, inside a proof method for first-order logic whose propositional part had to test sets of ground clauses. It eliminated variables by resolution and used too much memory on the machines of the time. In 1962 Davis, George Logemann and Donald Loveland replaced elimination by splitting on a variable, and the result, DPLL, was the standard complete method for the next three decades. G. S. Tseitin's paper of 1968, *On the complexity of derivation in propositional calculus*, gave the clause form with new gate variables that still bears his name, and studied the length of resolution proofs. David Plaisted and Steven Greenbaum published the one-polarity form in 1986.

Complexity theory put the problem at the centre. Stephen Cook proved SAT NP-complete in 1971, and Leonid Levin found the same result independently in 1973. Richard Karp's reductions of 1972 included the reduction to 3-SAT by splitting long clauses. The easy classes came soon after: Bengt Aspvall, Michael Plass and Robert Tarjan gave the linear-time 2-SAT algorithm in 1979, and William Dowling and Jean Gallier gave linear-time Horn satisfiability in 1984. In 1985 Armin Haken proved that every resolution refutation of the pigeonhole formulas has exponential size, the first such lower bound.

Local search arrived in 1992 with GSAT, by Bart Selman, Hector Levesque and David Mitchell, which solved hard random 3-CNF formulas out of reach of the DPLL solvers of the day. WalkSAT, by Selman, Henry Kautz and Bram Cohen, followed in 1994. Random walks also gave the best proven bounds. Ramamohan Paturi, Pavel Pudlák, Michael Saks and Francis Zane presented PPSZ in 1998, Uwe Schöning his random walk for $k$-SAT in 1999, and Timon Hertli extended the PPSZ bound for 3-SAT to all formulas in 2011. In 2002 Marc Mézard, Giorgio Parisi and Riccardo Zecchina derived survey propagation from the cavity method of statistical physics.

The solvers of today descend from GRASP, by João Marques-Silva and Karem Sakallah (1996), which added conflict analysis, clause learning and non-chronological backtracking to DPLL. Chaff, by Matthew Moskewicz and others (2001), added two watched literals and VSIDS. MiniSat, by Niklas Eén and Niklas Sörensson (2003), fixed the design that most solvers still follow. Gilles Audemard and Laurent Simon introduced the literal block distance in 2009. The SAT Competition now certifies unsatisfiable answers with DRAT proofs, checked by DRAT-trim of Nathan Wetzler, Marijn Heule and Warren Hunt (2014). LRAT, with hints for each step, followed in 2017.

## Further reading

- Armin Biere, Marijn Heule, Hans van Maaren and Toby Walsh (eds.), *Handbook of Satisfiability*, second edition (2021). The standard reference, with chapters on CDCL, preprocessing, encodings, proofs, local search, MaxSAT, counting and QBF.
- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 4B: Combinatorial Algorithms, Part 2*, first edition (2022). Section 7.2.2.2 builds solvers from DPLL to lookahead and clause learning, with history and many exercises.
- Daniel Kroening and Ofer Strichman, *Decision Procedures: An Algorithmic Point of View*, second edition (2016). Chapters 1 and 2 give normal forms, the Tseitin encoding and a compact account of CDCL, and the later chapters lead into the next chapter.
- Christos H. Papadimitriou, *Computational Complexity*, first edition (1994). It places SAT, 2-SAT, Horn-SAT and QBF among the complexity classes.
- David P. Williamson and David B. Shmoys, *The Design of Approximation Algorithms*, first edition (2011). The approximation algorithms for MaxSAT, from random assignment to semidefinite rounding.
