An online algorithm must answer each request before it sees the next one. A cache that holds $k$ pages must decide, at each miss, which page to throw out, without knowing which pages the program will ask for later. A scheduler must put each job on a machine as the job arrives. A skier who does not know how long the season lasts must decide each day whether to rent skis again or to buy them. A streaming algorithm faces a related limit from the other side: it sees the whole input, but only once and in order, and it has far too little memory to keep it. A router that counts the packets of a billion flows, or a database that estimates the number of distinct users in a log, keeps a summary of a few kilobytes and answers from that.

This volume is about algorithms that work under these two limits, on the future they cannot see and on the past they cannot store. It starts with the oldest online problem in computing, paging, then turns to online scheduling, to the general theory of competitive analysis, to sketches that summarize a stream in small space, and to sliding windows, where old items must be forgotten.

## Why online and streaming algorithms get a volume of their own

Both kinds of algorithm are judged against something they cannot compute. The same few ideas carry across the chapters:

- **Compare with the offline optimum.** The [competitive ratio](/competitive-ratio/) bounds the cost of an online algorithm by a factor times the cost of the best algorithm that knows the whole input. The [break-even strategy](/break-even-strategy/) for [ski rental](/ski-rental-problem/) is within a factor $2 - 1/B$ of it, and LRU is within a factor $k$ for a [paging](/paging-problem/) cache of $k$ pages.
- **Lower bounds from an adversary.** An adversary who sees the moves of a deterministic algorithm can always request the page it just evicted. This [argument](/online-caching-lower-bound/) shows that no deterministic paging rule beats $k$, and its relatives give most of the lower bounds of the volume.
- **Randomness against a fixed input.** Against an [oblivious adversary](/oblivious-adversary/), [randomized marking](/randomized-marking/) pays $O(\log k)$ times the optimum instead of $k$. In streams, random hash functions let a [count-min sketch](/count-min-sketch/) or [HyperLogLog](/hyperloglog/) answer within a small error with high probability.
- **Potential functions.** [Move-to-front](/move-to-front/), [double coverage](/double-coverage/) and the [work function algorithm](/work-function-algorithm/) are analysed by a potential that measures how far the online state is from the optimal one, as in [amortized analysis](/potential-function/).
- **Summaries that lose a little.** A streaming algorithm keeps a [linear sketch](/linear-sketch/) or a few counters and promises an [(ε, δ) guarantee](/epsilon-delta-guarantee/). The [Misra-Gries summary](/misra-gries-summary/) finds every item that fills more than a $1/k$ share of the stream with $k - 1$ counters.
- **Lower bounds from communication.** A streaming algorithm with $s$ bits of memory is a message of $s$ bits from the first half of the stream to the second. The [INDEX lower bound](/index-problem-lower-bound/) turns this into proofs that some answers need linear space.

## How this volume relates to the others

- **Volume 1** supplies the [potential function](/potential-function/), the [adversary argument](/adversary-argument/), [Yao's minimax principle](/yao-minimax-principle/) and the probability tools: [expected value](/expected-value/), the inequalities of [Markov](/markov-inequality/) and [Chebyshev](/chebyshev-inequality/), and the [Chernoff bound](/chernoff-bound/).
- **Volume 3** builds the structures that sketches and windows rest on: the [universal hash family](/universal-hash-family/), the [binary heap](/binary-heap/), the [deque](/deque/) and the [circular buffer](/circular-buffer/). Its chapter on probabilistic structures has the [Bloom filter](/bloom-filter/), the [count-min error bound](/count-min-error-bound/) and a [Flajolet-Martin sketch](/flajolet-martin-sketch/); Chapter 19.4 treats the same sketches as streaming algorithms, with their problems and lower bounds.
- **Volume 17** gives [reservoir sampling](/reservoir-sampling/), [pairwise independence](/pairwise-independence/) and [k-wise independence](/k-wise-independence/), which the sketches and the window samplers use.
- **Volume 18** studies the offline versions of several problems here: [set cover](/set-cover/), [scheduling](/scheduling-notation/) and the [list scheduling bound](/list-scheduling-bound/). An approximation ratio compares a fast algorithm with the optimum; a competitive ratio compares an algorithm that cannot see the future with it. Many proofs look alike, and the [online primal-dual method](/online-primal-dual/) borrows the language of [LP duality](/lp-duality/) from Volume 15.

## The chapters

**Chapter 19.1, Paging and caching online**, is about the cache. It defines the paging problem, proves that Belady's rule of evicting the page used furthest in the future is optimal offline, and shows that LRU, FIFO and every marking algorithm are $k$-competitive and that no deterministic rule does better. Randomized marking reaches $2H_k$ and the harmonic bound shows that $H_k$ is the limit. The chapter also covers list update and move-to-front, weighted paging and the Landlord rule, and the $k$-server problem with its open conjecture.

**Chapter 19.2, Online scheduling**, assigns jobs as they arrive. It covers machine models and the notation for online problems, list scheduling and the lower bounds for makespan, restricted assignment with its $\Theta(\log m)$ ratio, flow time and the optimality of SRPT, non-clairvoyant scheduling, and speed scaling to save energy.

**Chapter 19.3, Competitive analysis**, is the theory under the other chapters. It defines online and offline algorithms, the competitive ratio and the adversaries, and then works through the classical problems: ski rental, the linear search problem, the secretary problem and the prophet inequality, online matching with Ranking, metrical task systems and work functions, the experts problem with multiplicative weights, online convex optimization, and online versions of set cover, Steiner tree, facility location and knapsack. It ends with algorithms that use predictions or advice.

**Chapter 19.4, Streaming sketches**, summarizes a stream in small space. It covers the models with insertions only and with deletions, majority and frequent items with Misra-Gries and Space-Saving, the count-min and count sketches, the AMS sketch for the second moment, distinct counting from Flajolet-Martin to HyperLogLog, quantile summaries from Greenwald-Khanna to KLL, sketches of graphs, and the communication lower bounds.

**Chapter 19.5, Sliding windows**, answers questions about only the most recent items. It defines count-based, time-based, tumbling, hopping and session windows, moving averages and exponential decay, and then gives the window algorithms: the monotone deque for the maximum, two-stacks and DABA queues for any associative operator, the DGIM algorithm and exponential histograms for counting, and smooth histograms, samplers and a sliding HyperLogLog.

## How to read this volume

The basic definitions are in Chapter 19.3, although it is the third chapter. Read its first entries before the others. If this is your first course, read these entries in this order. From Chapter 19.3: [online algorithm](/online-algorithm/), [competitive ratio](/competitive-ratio/), the [ski rental problem](/ski-rental-problem/), the [break-even strategy](/break-even-strategy/) and the [secretary problem](/secretary-problem/). From Chapter 19.1: the [paging problem](/paging-problem/), [Belady's algorithm](/belady-algorithm/), the [deterministic lower bound](/online-caching-lower-bound/), the [marking algorithm](/marking-algorithm/) and move-to-front. From Chapter 19.2: [list scheduling](/list-scheduling-competitive-ratio/) and [SRPT](/srpt/). From Chapter 19.4: the [streaming model](/streaming-model/), the Misra-Gries summary, the count-min sketch and HyperLogLog. From Chapter 19.5: the [sliding window model](/sliding-window-model/), the [sliding window maximum](/sliding-window-maximum/) and the [DGIM algorithm](/dgim-algorithm/). Do the exercises marked ◦ and • at the end of these chapters. That is a complete first unit on online and streaming algorithms.

If you already know the basics, start with the opening page of each chapter. It names the groups in the chapter and the order to read them. Chapters 19.1 and 19.2 lean on the definitions of Chapter 19.3; Chapters 19.4 and 19.5 can be read without them. The entries marked *Advanced* and *Research* are the place to go deeper.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained. Entries in other volumes are cited by number too.

## What you need first

You should be able to read a short program and follow a proof by induction. From Volume 1 you need asymptotic notation, amortized analysis with a potential function, and discrete probability: expectation and its linearity, variance, and the inequalities of Markov and Chebyshev; the sketches also use the Chernoff bound. From Volume 3 you need hash tables, heaps, queues and deques. The [randomized algorithm](/randomized-algorithm/) entry of Volume 2 is a good first page if algorithms that flip coins are new to you.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
