A cache of three pages serves the requests $1\,2\,3\,4\,1\,2\,5\,1\,2\,3\,4\,5$. An algorithm that knows the whole sequence has 7 faults: at each fault it evicts the page whose next request is furthest away. LRU, which evicts the page used least recently, has 10. FIFO has 9, and with a fourth page in the cache FIFO has 10, more than with three. None of the online rules can know that page 5 comes back at the end. The question of this chapter is how much that ignorance costs in the worst case, and which rules keep the cost small.

The answer is the first result of competitive analysis. Sleator and Tarjan proved in 1985 that LRU never has more than $k$ times the faults of the optimum, plus a constant, for a cache of $k$ pages, and that no deterministic rule does better. Randomness lowers the factor to about $\ln k$. The same questions, asked of linked lists, of servers that move in a metric space, and of pages with different costs, give the rest of the chapter. Its definitions come from Chapter 19.3: an [online algorithm](/online-algorithm/), the [offline optimum](/offline-optimum/) and the [competitive ratio](/competitive-ratio/).

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups, read in order.

1. **The paging model.** A [page fault](/page-fault/) costs 1, and the [paging problem](/paging-problem/) asks for few faults. [Demand paging](/demand-paging/) shows that loading pages early never helps, so a [replacement policy](/cache-replacement-policy/) is all an algorithm chooses. The [write-back policy](/write-back-policy/) adds a cost for evicting changed pages.
2. **The offline optimum.** [Belady's algorithm](/belady-algorithm/) evicts the page needed furthest in the future, and an exchange argument proves its [optimality](/belady-optimality/). [Stack algorithms](/stack-algorithms/) give the faults of every cache size in one pass and cannot show Belady's anomaly.
3. **Deterministic online paging.** The [lower bound](/online-caching-lower-bound/) $k$ comes from an adversary on $k + 1$ pages. [Marking phases](/marking-phases/) split the requests into blocks of $k$ distinct pages, and every [marking algorithm](/marking-algorithm/), including [flush when full](/flush-when-full/), meets the bound. The [Sleator-Tarjan bound](/sleator-tarjan-paging-bound/) gives $k/(k - h + 1)$ against an optimum with $h$ pages.
4. **Randomized paging and predictions.** [Randomized marking](/randomized-marking/) is $2H_k$-competitive against an oblivious adversary, and the [harmonic bound](/harmonic-paging-bound/) shows that $H_k$ is the best possible. [Learning-augmented marking](/learning-augmented-marking/) uses predicted next requests.
5. **Rules in hardware and databases.** [Tree PLRU](/plru-replacement/) and [2Q](/two-q-replacement/) are cheap rules in real caches; in their usual settings neither is competitive.
6. **List update.** In the [list update problem](/list-update-problem/), [move-to-front](/move-to-front/) is 2-competitive and the [transposition rule](/transposition-rule/) is not competitive.
7. **Servers on a metric.** The [$k$-server problem](/k-server-problem/) generalizes paging. [Double coverage](/double-coverage/) is $k$-competitive on the line, the [work function algorithm](/work-function-algorithm/) is $(2k - 1)$-competitive everywhere, and the [$k$-server conjecture](/k-server-conjecture/) is open.
8. **Weights and sizes.** The [weighted paging problem](/weighted-paging-problem/) gives each page a cost, and the [Landlord algorithm](/landlord-algorithm/) is $k$-competitive for files of any size and cost.

## A first reading

Read the [paging problem](/paging-problem/) first, with its running example, and then [demand paging](/demand-paging/) and [Belady's algorithm](/belady-algorithm/). Run Belady's rule and LRU by hand on a short sequence; ten requests and a cache of three pages are enough to see every idea. Next read the [deterministic lower bound](/online-caching-lower-bound/), [marking phases](/marking-phases/) and the [marking algorithm](/marking-algorithm/), which together prove that LRU is $k$-competitive. Then read [randomized marking](/randomized-marking/) and the [list update problem](/list-update-problem/) with [move-to-front](/move-to-front/). That is a first unit of nine entries.

The proof of [Belady optimality](/belady-optimality/) and the [harmonic bound](/harmonic-paging-bound/) are worth reading next, the second because it is the model for randomized lower bounds through Yao's principle. The $k$-server group, Landlord and learning-augmented marking are research-level and can wait. The hardware rules of the fifth group stand apart from the theory and can be read at any time.
