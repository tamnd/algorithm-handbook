## History

Scheduling theory began with offline rules. Wayne Smith gave his ratio rule for weighted completion time in 1956, and Ronald Graham proved in 1966 that [list scheduling](/list-scheduling/) is within $2 - \frac1m$ of the best makespan. Graham's rule never looks ahead, so his bound was an online result before the word existed. Linus Schrage and Louis Miller studied the shortest remaining processing time rule for queues in 1966, and Schrage proved in 1968 that [SRPT](/srpt/) minimizes the total flow time on one machine. In 1979 Graham, Eugene Lawler, Jan Karel Lenstra and Alexander Rinnooy Kan fixed the [three-field notation](/graham-three-field-notation/) still in use.

The study of online makespan as a game against an adversary began with Ulrich Faigle, Walter Kern and György Turán in 1989, who showed that list scheduling is optimal for two and three machines. Gábor Galambos and Gerhard Woeginger improved on $2 - \frac1m$ for every $m \ge 4$ in 1993. Yair Bartal, Amos Fiat, Howard Karloff and Rakesh Vohra (1995) gave the first ratio bounded away from 2, and David Karger, Steven Phillips and Eric Torng (1996), Susanne Albers (1999), and Rudolf Fleischer and Michaela Wahl (2000) lowered it to about 1.9201. John Rudin's lower bound of about 1.88 dates from 2001. For machines with eligible sets, Yossi Azar, Joseph Naor and Raphael Rom (1992) proved that [greedy](/greedy-restricted-assignment/) is within one of $\log_2 m$ and that no algorithm does much better. James Aspnes, Azar, Fiat, Serge Plotkin and Orli Waarts (STOC 1993) reached $O(\log m)$ on unrelated machines.

Flow time with arrivals over time came next. Rajeev Motwani, Phillips and Torng (1994) showed that schedulers that do not know job sizes have no good ratio. Bala Kalyanasundaram and Kirk Pruhs answered in 1995 with resource augmentation: with a little extra speed, such a scheduler competes with one that knows the sizes. Stefano Leonardi and Danny Raz analysed SRPT on parallel machines in 1997, Luca Becchetti and Leonardi gave the randomized multilevel feedback rule its $O(\log n)$ bound in 2004, and Michael Bender, Soumen Chakrabarti and S. Muthukrishnan studied [maximum stretch](/online-max-stretch/) in 1998. For completion time without preemption, Han Hoogeveen and Arjen Vestjens gave the delayed SPT rule and its matching bound of 2 in 1996.

Deadlines brought their own line of work. Michael Dertouzos and Aloysius Mok showed in 1989 that no online rule keeps all deadlines on several machines. Sanjoy Baruah and coauthors proved the bound 4 for value under overload in 1992, and Gilad Koren and Dennis Shasha matched it in 1995. Baruch Awerbuch, Azar and Plotkin gave the exponential price rule for [admission control](/online-admission-control/) in 1993, and Richard Lipton and Andrew Tomkins, Woeginger, and Faigle and Willem Nawijn studied online intervals in 1994 and 1995. Cynthia Phillips, Clifford Stein, Torng and Joel Wein (1997) showed that extra speed or extra machines rescue EDF. For [machine minimization](/online-machine-minimization/), Lin Chen, Nicole Megow and Kevin Schewior reached $O(\log m)$ in 2016, and Sungjin Im, Benjamin Moseley, Pruhs and Stein reached $O(\log \log m)$ in 2017. Speed scaling began with Frances Yao, Alan Demers and Scott Shenker in 1995, who gave the offline optimum, [AVR](/avr/) and [Optimal Available](/optimal-available/). Nikhil Bansal, Tracy Kimbrel and Pruhs proved $\alpha^\alpha$ for OA and gave [BKP](/bkp-speed-scaling/) in 2004, and Bansal, Ho-Leung Chan, Dmitriy Katz and Pruhs proved the deterministic lower bound $e^{\alpha - 1}/\alpha$ in 2009.

## Further reading

- Kirk Pruhs, Jiří Sgall and Eric Torng, "Online scheduling", in Joseph Leung (ed.), *Handbook of Scheduling*, Chapman and Hall/CRC, 2004: the standard survey of the whole chapter, from list scheduling to flow time.
- Jiří Sgall, "On-line scheduling", in *Online Algorithms: The State of the Art*, LNCS 1442, Springer, 1998: a careful account of makespan and the models of online scheduling.
- Yossi Azar, "On-line load balancing", in the same volume, 1998: greedy, restricted assignment and unrelated machines, with their lower bounds.
- Allan Borodin and Ran El-Yaniv, *Online Computation and Competitive Analysis*, Cambridge University Press, 1998: competitive analysis in textbook form, with load balancing and admission control.
- Nikhil Bansal, Tracy Kimbrel and Kirk Pruhs, "Speed scaling to manage energy and temperature", *Journal of the ACM* 54(1), 2007: the analysis of OA and of BKP.
