## History

The subject began at Bell Labs in the late 1940s. Claude Shannon's paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" of 1948 proved that every channel has a capacity and that codes can make the error rate as small as wanted at any rate below it, but the proof used random codes and gave no way to decode them. Marcel Golay published the codes that carry his name in 1949, and Richard Hamming published his single-error-correcting codes in 1950. David Slepian set out the theory of linear group codes in 1956, and Peter Elias introduced product codes in 1954, convolutional codes in 1955 and list decoding in 1957.

The algebraic codes followed. Reed and Muller described their codes in 1954, and Eugene Prange studied cyclic codes in 1957. Hocquenghem in 1959 and Bose and Ray-Chaudhuri in 1960 found the BCH codes, and Irving Reed and Gustave Solomon published their codes over finite fields in 1960. Peterson gave the first decoder for binary BCH codes in 1960, and Gorenstein and Zierler extended it to nonbinary codes in 1961. Chien's search came in 1964 and Forney's formula for error values in 1965. Elwyn Berlekamp's book of 1968 contained a fast decoder, and James Massey recast it in 1969 as the synthesis of the shortest linear feedback shift register. Valery Goppa defined his codes in 1970. The bounds came in the same years: Gilbert in 1952, Varshamov in 1957, Plotkin and Griesmer in 1960, MacWilliams's identity in 1963, Singleton in 1964, Delsarte's linear programming bound in 1973 and the bound of McEliece, Rodemich, Rumsey and Welch in 1977. In 1978 Berlekamp, McEliece and van Tilborg proved that decoding a general linear code is NP-hard, and Alexander Vardy proved the same for the minimum distance in 1997.

A second line of work used probability rather than algebra. Robert Gallager defined low-density parity-check codes in his doctoral work, published in 1962. David Forney introduced concatenated codes and generalized minimum distance decoding in 1966. Andrew Viterbi gave his algorithm for convolutional codes in 1967, and Bahl, Cocke, Jelinek and Raviv gave the bitwise MAP algorithm in 1974. Michael Tanner described codes on graphs in 1981.

The modern era opened in 1993, when Berrou, Glavieux and Thitimajshima presented turbo codes that came within about a decibel of the Shannon limit. MacKay and Neal rediscovered LDPC codes in 1996, the year that Sipser and Spielman introduced expander codes, and Richardson and Urbanke made density evolution a design tool in 2001. Madhu Sudan list decoded Reed-Solomon codes beyond half the distance in 1997, and Guruswami and Sudan improved the radius in 1999. Michael Luby published LT codes in 2002 and Amin Shokrollahi Raptor codes in 2006. Erdal Arıkan introduced polar codes in 2009, the first explicit codes proved to reach the symmetric capacity of every binary-input memoryless channel with a decoder of cost $\Theta(N \log N)$, and in 2016 they were chosen for the control channels of 5G, with LDPC codes for the data channels.

## Further reading

- F. J. MacWilliams and N. J. A. Sloane, *The Theory of Error-Correcting Codes*, North-Holland, 1977. The classic reference for algebraic codes, bounds and weight enumerators.
- Shu Lin and Daniel J. Costello, Jr., *Error Control Coding*, second edition, Pearson Prentice Hall, 2004. An engineering textbook that covers block, convolutional, turbo and LDPC codes with their decoders and performance curves.
- Ron M. Roth, *Introduction to Coding Theory*, Cambridge University Press, 2006. A rigorous course on algebraic codes, bounds, list decoding and decoding algorithms, with many exercises.
- David J. C. MacKay, *Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms*, Cambridge University Press, 2003. A readable account of capacity, sparse graph codes and message passing.
- Tom Richardson and Rüdiger Urbanke, *Modern Coding Theory*, Cambridge University Press, 2008. The reference for LDPC and turbo codes, iterative decoding and density evolution.
