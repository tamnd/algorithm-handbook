## History

Dictionary compression grew out of work by Abraham Lempel and Jacob Ziv. In *On the Complexity of Finite Sequences* (1976) they measured a sequence by the number of steps in which it can be built by copying, which is the complexity of the last group. The next year they turned the idea into a coder: *A Universal Algorithm for Sequential Data Compression* (1977) gave the sliding-window method now called LZ77. *Compression of Individual Sequences via Variable-Rate Coding* (1978) gave LZ78, with its list of phrases, and proved that on every individual sequence it does as well, in the limit, as any finite-state compressor. For a stationary ergodic source this means that it reaches the entropy rate; Wyner and Ziv proved the same for the sliding window in 1994. Fixed dictionaries are older: Schuegraf and Heaps showed in 1974 that the best parse with a fixed dictionary is a shortest path.

The early 1980s made the methods practical. James Storer and Thomas Szymanski, in *Data Compression via Textual Substitution* (1982), studied texts written as plain symbols mixed with pointers to other copies. The coder named after them, LZSS, uses a pointer only when it is shorter than the text it replaces, with a flag bit per token. Michael Rodeh, Vaughan Pratt and Shimon Even showed in 1981 that a suffix tree finds the LZ77 parse in linear time. Terry Welch published LZW in *A Technique for High-Performance Data Compression* (1984). It became the Unix program compress and the coder of GIF. The licence claims of the patent holder on GIF software in the 1990s led to the PNG format, which uses DEFLATE; the US patent expired in 2003.

The formats came next. Phil Katz's PKZIP introduced DEFLATE, which Peter Deutsch wrote down as RFC 1951 in 1996, and Jean-loup Gailly and Mark Adler built gzip and zlib on it. Jonathan Forbes and Tomi Poutanen wrote LZX for the Amiga in 1995, and Microsoft took it into CAB files. Markus Oberhumer released LZO in 1996, and Igor Pavlov released LZMA with 7-Zip in 2001. The fast byte formats of the 2010s, Yann Collet's LZ4 and Google's Snappy, both appeared in 2011. Collet's Zstandard followed in 2015 and became RFC 8878. Jyrki Alakuijala and Zoltán Szabadka announced Brotli in 2015, and it became RFC 7932 in 2016.

Grammars give a second line. Philip Gage described byte pair encoding in 1994. Craig Nevill-Manning and Ian Witten built Sequitur, an online grammar builder, in 1997, and Jesper Larsson and Alistair Moffat gave Re-Pair in *Off-Line Dictionary-Based Compression* (2000). John Kieffer and En-hui Yang made grammar-based codes a theory in 2000, and Moses Charikar and his coauthors set out *The Smallest Grammar Problem* in 2005, with its hardness and its approximations. In 2016 Rico Sennrich, Barry Haddow and Alexandra Birch reused byte pair encoding to split words for neural machine translation, and the tokenizers of many language models descend from that use.

Later work treats a parse as a measure of repetition. Maxime Crochemore and Lucian Ilie computed the longest previous factor array and the factorization in linear time in 2008. Sebastian Kreft and Gonzalo Navarro introduced LZ-End in 2010 for fast access to highly repetitive text, and Kempa and Saha proved in 2022 that it has at most a factor $O(\log^2 n)$ more phrases than LZ77.

## Further reading

- Timothy C. Bell, John G. Cleary and Ian H. Witten, *Text Compression* (Prentice Hall, 1990). The classic account of the LZ77 and LZ78 families and their variants.
- David Salomon and Giovanni Motta, *Handbook of Data Compression*, fifth edition (Springer, 2010). A wide catalogue of methods and formats, for coders not covered here.
- Khalid Sayood, *Introduction to Data Compression*, fifth edition (Morgan Kaufmann, 2017). A textbook with a clear chapter on dictionary techniques next to the entropy coders they use.
- Thomas M. Cover and Joy A. Thomas, *Elements of Information Theory*, second edition (Wiley, 2006). Chapter 13 proves that the Lempel-Ziv codes are universal.
- Markus Lohrey, *Algorithmics on SLP-Compressed Strings: A Survey* (Groups Complexity Cryptology, 2012). How to search and compute on a string given as a straight-line program, without decompressing it.
