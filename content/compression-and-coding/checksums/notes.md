## History

Checks on numbers are far older than computers. The dates of casting out nines are uncertain: secondary sources credit Aryabhata II, about 950, and Ibn Sina, about 1020, who called it the Hindu method. Fibonacci taught it in the Liber Abaci of 1202. The modern check digit begins with Hans Peter Luhn of IBM, who filed his patent for a machine to verify numbers in January 1954; it was granted in 1960, and his rule now ends every payment card number. Jacobus Verhoeff studied the errors that people make when they copy numbers, and in Error Detecting Decimal Codes (1969) he gave a scheme on the dihedral group of order 10 that catches every single error and every adjacent swap. H. Michael Damm reached the same guarantee with one quasigroup table in his Marburg thesis of 2004.

Richard Hamming's paper of 1950 put error detection and correction on a mathematical footing. W. Wesley Peterson and D. T. Brown described cyclic codes for error detection in 1961, with the shift-register circuit that computes them and the proof that they catch bursts. Software CRCs came later. Dilip Sarwate published the byte-wise table method in 1988, and Ross Williams's guide of 1993 set out the parameter model, with width, poly, init, refin, refout and xorout, that Greg Cook's catalogue uses today. Kounavis and Berry introduced slicing-by-8 in 2005, and an Intel white paper of 2009 by Vinodh Gopal and colleagues showed how to fold a CRC with the carry-less multiply instruction.

The arithmetic checksums grew with the network. William Plummer compared checksum designs for TCP in the note IEN 45 of 1978. The ones' complement sum became the checksum of the IP and TCP specifications of 1981, and RFC 1071 of 1988 by Braden, Borman and Partridge explained how to compute it fast. RFC 1141 of 1990 gave an incremental update for routers, and RFC 1624 of 1994 corrected its handling of the two zeros. John Fletcher published his checksum in 1982, and Mark Adler's variant with the prime 65521 became the check of zlib in RFC 1950 (1996). Andrew Tridgell and Paul Mackerras described the rsync algorithm with its rolling checksum in a technical report of 1996.

Measurement and theory then sharpened the choices. Leung-Yan-Cheong and Hellman showed in 1976 that some codes do worse than $2^{-r}$, and Wolf, Michelson and Levesque computed undetected error probabilities of linear codes from the dual code in 1982. Castagnoli, Bräuer and Herrmann searched for 24- and 32-bit generators in 1993 and found the polynomial now called CRC-32C, which iSCSI adopted and SCTP took in place of Adler-32 in RFC 3309. Stone and Partridge measured real traffic in 2000 and found that the TCP checksum misses far more errors than its 16 bits suggest. Koopman and Chakravarty mapped the Hamming distance of every generator of widths 3 to 16 in 2004, and Maxino and Koopman compared the checksums with CRCs on embedded networks in 2009.

## Further reading

- Ross N. Williams, *A painless guide to CRC error detection algorithms*, 1993. The clearest account of how CRCs are computed, from long division to tables, and the source of the parameter model.
- Philip Koopman and Tridib Chakravarty, "Cyclic redundancy code (CRC) polynomial selection for embedded networks", DSN 2004. How to choose a generator by its Hamming distance at the lengths you need, with tables.
- Theresa C. Maxino and Philip J. Koopman, "The effectiveness of checksums for embedded control networks", IEEE Transactions on Dependable and Secure Computing, 2009. A careful comparison of parity, sums, Fletcher, Adler-32 and CRCs on the same errors.
- Joseph A. Gallian, "Error detection methods", ACM Computing Surveys, 1996. A short survey of check digit schemes, from ISBN and UPC to Verhoeff, with the group theory behind them.
- Shu Lin and Daniel J. Costello, Jr., *Error Control Coding*, second edition, Pearson Prentice Hall, 2004. The textbook treatment of cyclic codes, burst detection and the undetected error probability.
- Jonathan Stone and Craig Partridge, "When the CRC and TCP checksum disagree", ACM SIGCOMM 2000. What real networks do to data, and why the checksums of the Internet miss more than theory suggests.
