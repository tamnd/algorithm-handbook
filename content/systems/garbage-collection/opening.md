A program in Java builds a list of a million records, sorts it, prints the first ten and returns. Nothing in the source frees the list. The runtime still gets the memory back, because after the return no variable of the program can lead to the list any more. The part of the runtime that finds such memory and reuses it is the garbage collector. It sees the heap as a directed graph: the objects are the vertices, the pointer fields are the edges, and the stacks, registers and global variables are the roots. An object that no path from a root reaches can never be used again, so it may be freed. Freeing by hand gets this wrong in two ways, too early, which leaves a dangling pointer, and never, which leaks. A collector that frees only unreachable objects makes the first error impossible.

Two families of methods divide the field. A tracing collector searches the graph from the roots and reclaims what the search does not reach. Reference counting keeps in each object the number of pointers to it and frees the object when the number drops to zero, but on its own it never frees a cycle. Every design then trades three costs against each other: the total work, the extra memory, and the pauses that the running program sees. The safety of a collector, and of each barrier it uses, is proved in a stated model. The pause times and throughput of real collectors are measured, and so is the observation that most objects die young.

## A first reading

Take these twelve entries in order: heap reachability, garbage, tracing garbage collection, mark-and-sweep, copying collection, Cheney's copying collector, the space-time tradeoff, reference counting, the generational hypothesis, generational garbage collection, tricolor marking and snapshot-at-the-beginning. They give the problem, the two tracing methods and their costs, the counting method and why it misses cycles, the reason generations work, and the invariant that lets a collector run beside the program.

Skip at first the refinements of one method: Schorr–Waite marking, the two compactors, weighted reference counting, the train algorithm and Beltway. Skip the production collectors until group 7 is clear, since each of them is a choice of barrier, region layout and pacing on top of it. Conservative collection and finalization can be read at any time.

## Before you start

Tracing is graph search, so read [graph reachability](/graph-reachability/), [depth-first search](/depth-first-search/) and [breadth-first search](/breadth-first-search/) in Volume 7. The objects of a dead cycle lie in one [strongly connected component](/strongly-connected-components/) that no root reaches. From Volume 3 you need the [pointer](/pointer/), the [stack](/stack/) and [queue](/queue/) of the search, and the [hash table](/hash-table/) of remembered sets. Chapter 26.3 supplies the allocator side: the [free list](/free-list/) that a sweep fills, [bump-pointer allocation](/bump-pointer-allocation/) after a copy, [external fragmentation](/external-fragmentation/), and the [Robson bound](/robson-bound/) on what a non-moving heap can need.

The concurrent collectors rest on Volume 22. The barriers use atomic operations such as [compare-and-swap](/compare-and-swap/). The entries model the mutator and the collector as an interleaving of atomic steps, which is [sequential consistency](/sequential-consistency/) from Volume 3; on real hardware the order of the instructions inside a barrier matters too. Chapter 22.3 solves a close problem without tracing: [safe memory reclamation](/safe-memory-reclamation/) with [hazard pointers](/hazard-pointers/), [epoch-based reclamation](/epoch-based-reclamation/) and [lock-free reference counting](/lock-free-reference-counting/). Parallel marking balances its work by [work stealing](/work-stealing/) from Volume 20, and it must decide when all threads are done, which is [termination detection](/termination-detection/) from the volume on distributed algorithms. The lifetime models use the [geometric distribution](/geometric-distribution/) and [linearity of expectation](/linearity-of-expectation/), and the view of tracing and counting as two fixed points of one equation rests on the [Knaster–Tarski theorem](/knaster-tarski-theorem/).
