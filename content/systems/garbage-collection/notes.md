## History

Garbage collection began with Lisp. In 1960 John McCarthy described a collector that marks the cells reachable from the registers and puts the rest on the free list, and in the same year George Collins proposed keeping a count of references in each cell. Edwards wrote the two-finger compactor for the LISP of the Q-32 computer, and the LISP 2 system of 1966 gave its name to the sliding compactor with a forwarding word. Schorr and Waite (1967) marked in constant extra space by reversing pointers, as Deutsch did independently. Fenichel and Yochelson (1969) copied the live cells between two semispaces, and C. J. Cheney (1970) did the copy breadth-first with no stack at all.

The 1970s brought collectors that run beside the program. Guy Steele (1975) described a collector for a multiprocessor, and Dijkstra, Lamport, Martin, Scholten and Steffens (1978) gave the three colors and the invariant that later work on concurrent marking states its barriers against. Henry Baker (1978) made copying incremental with a read barrier and a fixed amount of scanning per allocation, and Rodney Brooks (1984) replaced the barrier's test by an indirection word. Deutsch and Bobrow (1976) cut the cost of reference counting by not counting the roots. Taiichi Yuasa (1990) gave the snapshot barrier, which shades the overwritten value.

Generations came next. Lieberman and Hewitt (1983) proposed collecting young objects more often, and David Ungar's generation scavenging (1984) made the idea practical. Andrew Appel (1987) argued that with enough memory, copying collection costs less than stack allocation. Hudson and Moss (1992) proposed the train algorithm for large old generations, and Seligmann and Grarup (1995) repaired its completeness argument. Boehm and Weiser (1988) collected C programs without type information, and Bartlett (1988) let a copying collector work with ambiguous roots. Paul Wilson surveyed the field in 1992, and the textbook of Jones and Lins appeared in 1996.

Later work turned the methods into parts of one theory and of production systems. Bacon and Rajan (2001) collected cycles by trial deletion in time linear in the candidates' subgraph, and Bacon, Cheng and Rajan (2004) described tracing and counting as the least and greatest fixed points of one set of equations. Blackburn, Jones, McKinley and Moss (2002) framed generational designs as Beltway configurations. In the HotSpot JVM, the concurrent collector of Printezis and Detlefs (2000) became CMS, which was removed in JDK 14; Garbage-First (Detlefs and others, 2004) has been the default since JDK 9; Shenandoah and ZGC became product features in JDK 15, and ZGC became generational in JDK 21. Azul's Pauseless collector (2005) and C4 (2011) moved objects behind a self-healing load barrier. Hertz and Berger (2005) measured how much memory a collector needs to match explicit freeing.

## Further reading

- Richard Jones, Antony Hosking and Eliot Moss, *The Garbage Collection Handbook: The Art of Automatic Memory Management*, second edition, CRC Press, 2023. The standard reference, from mark-sweep to concurrent and real-time collection.
- Richard Jones and Rafael Lins, *Garbage Collection: Algorithms for Automatic Dynamic Memory Management*, Wiley, 1996. An older textbook, with careful treatments of reference counting and compaction.
- Paul R. Wilson, Uniprocessor garbage collection techniques, International Workshop on Memory Management, LNCS 637, 1992. A short survey that is still a good first overview.
- David F. Bacon, Perry Cheng and V. T. Rajan, A unified theory of garbage collection, OOPSLA 2004. Tracing and reference counting as duals, and real collectors as hybrids of the two.
- Andrew W. Appel, Garbage collection can be faster than stack allocation, *Information Processing Letters* 25(4), 1987. Four pages that state the space-time tradeoff of copying.
