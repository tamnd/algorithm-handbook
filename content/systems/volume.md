Every computer runs algorithms that no user calls by name. When a program reads a file, the operating system looks for the block in its page cache and, if it is not there, picks a page to drop. The scheduler decides which of a hundred ready threads runs next. The allocator finds 48 free bytes for a new object, and later the garbage collector finds that the object is dead. A database chooses the order of three joins. TCP decides how fast to send by watching for lost packets, a load balancer sends each request to one of forty servers, and an API gateway refuses the hundred-and-first request in a minute. Each of these is an algorithm with a precise rule and a cost.

This volume is about those algorithms. Its eight chapters hold 442 entries: caching, scheduling, memory allocation, garbage collection, the algorithms inside a database, the algorithms of networks, load balancing and rate limiting.

## Why systems algorithms get a volume of their own

A systems algorithm is judged on a machine and a workload. A few ideas come back in every chapter:

- **The cost model is the machine.** Cost is counted in misses, disk seeks, pauses, round trips or bytes sent. The [memory hierarchy](/memory-hierarchy/) makes a miss in a [cache](/cache/) a hundred times dearer than a hit, and the [external memory model](/external-memory-model/) counts the block transfers of a database.
- **Decisions are made online.** A cache must evict before it sees the next request, and a scheduler must run a job before it knows how long the others will take. The worst-case tool is the [competitive ratio](/competitive-ratio/) of Volume 19: from an empty cache, [LRU](/lru/) with $k$ pages never misses more than $k$ times as often as the optimal offline policy, and no deterministic policy does better.
- **Every bound names its model.** A guarantee is a worst case over all inputs, an average over a stated workload such as the [independent reference model](/independent-reference-model/), or a measurement on a trace. The [Liu-Layland bound](/liu-layland-utilization-bound/) is a proof; the error of a [sliding window counter](/sliding-window-counter/) on real traffic is a measurement. The entries say which.
- **Each decision must be cheap.** A rule that runs on every memory access or every packet can spend a few nanoseconds and a few bits. [CLOCK](/clock/) keeps one bit per page instead of an ordered list, and [GCRA](/gcra/) keeps one timestamp per flow.
- **Many users share one resource.** Threads share a processor, flows share a link and keys share a cluster. [Max-min fairness](/max-min-fairness/), tail latency and the largest load matter as much as the mean.
- **Correctness comes before speed.** A collector must never free a live object, a database must keep its committed data through a crash, and a limiter must never let through more than its limit.

## The chapters

**Chapter 26.1, Caching policies**, covers the cache, the [hit ratio](/cache-hit-ratio/) and [locality of reference](/locality-of-reference/); [Belady's MIN](/belady-optimal/), [FIFO](/fifo-replacement/) with [Belady's anomaly](/belady-anomaly/), LRU, [LFU](/lfu/) and CLOCK; [reuse distance](/reuse-distance/) and [miss ratio curves](/miss-ratio-curve/); the [competitive bound for LRU](/lru-competitiveness/); the policies that mix recency and frequency, from [2Q](/two-q/) to [ARC](/arc/) and [LIRS](/lirs/); admission and quick demotion with [TinyLFU](/tinylfu/), [S3-FIFO](/s3-fifo/) and [SIEVE](/sieve/); objects of different sizes with [GreedyDual](/greedy-dual/); and [TTL expiration](/ttl-expiration/) and [invalidation](/cache-invalidation/) in application caches.

**Chapter 26.2, Scheduling**, decides which job runs next. It covers [first-come first-served](/fcfs-scheduling/), [shortest job first](/shortest-job-first/), which [minimizes the average wait](/sjf-optimality/) when all jobs are present at the start, [round robin](/round-robin/), the [multilevel feedback queue](/multilevel-feedback-queue/), [stride scheduling](/stride-scheduling/) and the [Completely Fair Scheduler](/completely-fair-scheduler/). For real-time systems it covers [rate-monotonic scheduling](/rate-monotonic-scheduling/) with the Liu-Layland test, [earliest deadline first](/earliest-deadline-first/), which is [optimal on one preemptive processor](/edf-optimality/), [response-time analysis](/response-time-analysis/) and [priority inversion](/priority-inversion/). It also has disk scheduling from [SSTF](/shortest-seek-time-first/) to [C-SCAN](/c-scan/), [Smith's rule](/smiths-rule/) and [Johnson's rule](/johnsons-rule/), and cluster scheduling with [backfilling](/backfilling/) and [dominant resource fairness](/dominant-resource-fairness/).

**Chapter 26.3, Memory allocation**, finds room for blocks of many sizes in one heap. It covers [external](/external-fragmentation/) and [internal fragmentation](/internal-fragmentation/), [free lists](/free-list/) and the fits from [first fit](/first-fit-allocation/) to [best fit](/best-fit-allocation/), [boundary tags](/boundary-tags/) and [coalescing](/coalescing/), [segregated free lists](/segregated-free-lists/) and the [buddy system](/buddy-system/); [slab](/slab-allocator/), [arena](/arena-allocation/) and [bump-pointer allocation](/bump-pointer-allocation/); [TLSF](/tlsf/) and the designs of [dlmalloc](/dlmalloc/), [jemalloc](/jemalloc/), [tcmalloc](/tcmalloc/) and [mimalloc](/mimalloc/). Its theory is [Knuth's fifty-percent rule](/knuth-fifty-percent-rule/) and [Robson's bound](/robson-bound/): an allocator that never moves blocks can be forced to use about $\tfrac12 M \log_2 n$ words for live size $M$ and block sizes up to $n$.

**Chapter 26.4, Garbage collection**, frees the objects a program can no longer reach. It covers [heap reachability](/heap-reachability/) from the [roots](/gc-roots/), [mark-and-sweep](/mark-and-sweep/), [Cheney's copying collector](/cheney-copying-collector/), [mark-compact](/mark-compact/), [reference counting](/reference-counting/) and [cycle collection](/cycle-collection/); [tricolor marking](/tricolor-marking/) with [write](/write-barrier/) and [read barriers](/read-barrier/); the [generational hypothesis](/generational-hypothesis/) and [generational collection](/generational-garbage-collection/) with [remembered sets](/remembered-set/) and [card marking](/card-marking/); [incremental](/incremental-garbage-collection/), [concurrent](/concurrent-garbage-collection/) and [real-time](/baker-real-time-collector/) collectors; [conservative collection](/conservative-garbage-collection/); the collectors [G1](/garbage-first-collector/), [Shenandoah](/shenandoah-collector/) and [ZGC](/z-garbage-collector/); and the [space-time tradeoff](/gc-space-time-tradeoff/) that makes collection cheaper as memory grows.

**Chapter 26.5, Database algorithms**, covers the algorithms inside a database system: the [relational algebra](/relational-algebra/) and the joins, from [nested loops](/nested-loop-join/) and [hash join](/hash-join/) to [sort-merge join](/sort-merge-join/) and the worst-case optimal [generic join](/generic-join/) within the [AGM bound](/agm-bound/); [query optimization](/query-optimization/) with [cardinality estimates](/cardinality-estimation/) and [System R join enumeration](/selinger-join-enumeration/); [normalization](/normalization/) from [functional dependencies](/functional-dependency/) to [BCNF](/boyce-codd-normal-form/); transactions with [two-phase locking](/two-phase-locking/), [timestamp ordering](/timestamp-ordering/), [multiversion concurrency control](/multiversion-concurrency-control/) and [snapshot isolation](/snapshot-isolation/); recovery with [write-ahead logging](/write-ahead-logging/) and [ARIES](/aries-recovery/); [Datalog](/datalog/) with [semi-naive evaluation](/semi-naive-evaluation/); and top-$k$ queries with the [threshold algorithm](/threshold-algorithm/).

**Chapter 26.6, Networking algorithms**, moves packets between computers. It covers reliable transfer with the [sliding window protocol](/sliding-window-protocol/), [flow control](/flow-control/) and [congestion control](/congestion-control/), from [AIMD](/aimd/) and [slow start](/slow-start/) to [TCP Reno](/tcp-reno/), [CUBIC](/tcp-cubic/) and [BBR](/bbr-congestion-control/); [active queue management](/active-queue-management/) with [RED](/red-random-early-detection/) and [CoDel](/codel/); [packet scheduling](/packet-scheduling/) by [fair queueing](/fair-queueing/) and [deficit round robin](/deficit-round-robin/); [distance-vector](/distance-vector-routing/), [link-state](/link-state-routing/) and [path-vector routing](/path-vector-routing/), and [longest prefix match](/longest-prefix-match/); switch scheduling with [iSLIP](/islip/); medium access from [slotted ALOHA](/slotted-aloha/) to [CSMA/CD](/csma-cd/) with [binary exponential backoff](/binary-exponential-backoff/); the retry policy of [exponential backoff](/exponential-backoff/); and the overlays [Chord](/chord/) and [Kademlia](/kademlia/).

**Chapter 26.7, Load balancing and hashing**, spreads work over many servers. It covers the [load balancer](/load-balancer/) at layer 4 and layer 7, rules from [round robin](/round-robin-load-balancing/) and [least connections](/least-connections/) to [Peak EWMA](/peak-ewma/), [health checking](/health-checking/), [failover](/failover/) and [load shedding](/load-shedding/); [sharding](/sharding/) by hash and by range; [consistent hashing](/consistent-hashing/), under which only about $1/n$ of the keys move when a server joins, with [virtual nodes](/virtual-nodes/) and [bounded loads](/bounded-load-consistent-hashing/); [rendezvous](/rendezvous-hashing/), [jump](/jump-consistent-hash/) and [Maglev hashing](/maglev-hashing/); and the [power of two choices](/power-of-two-choices/), which with high probability lowers the largest load of $n$ jobs on $n$ servers from about $\ln n / \ln\ln n$ to $\ln\ln n/\ln 2 + O(1)$.

**Chapter 26.8, Rate limiting**, bounds how fast a client may send. It covers the [rate-limiting problem](/rate-limiting-problem/), where the greedy rule is optimal for unit requests; the [fixed window counter](/fixed-window-counter/), the [sliding window log](/sliding-window-log/) and the sliding window counter; the [token bucket](/token-bucket/), which passes at most $r\tau + b$ in any interval of length $\tau$, the equivalent [leaky bucket meter](/leaky-bucket-meter/) and GCRA; [policing](/traffic-policing/), [shaping](/traffic-shaping/) and the [three color markers](/single-rate-three-color-marker/); [admission control](/admission-control/), [throttling](/throttling/) and [backpressure](/backpressure/); and [distributed rate limiting](/distributed-rate-limiting/).

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course, read these entries in this order: the cache, LRU, CLOCK, the competitive bound for LRU, round robin, shortest job first, earliest deadline first, rate-monotonic scheduling, first fit, the buddy system, mark-and-sweep, generational collection, hash join, two-phase locking, write-ahead logging, the sliding window protocol, AIMD, consistent hashing, the power of two choices and the token bucket. Do the exercises marked ◦ at the end of each chapter you touch.

If you already know the basic algorithms, start with the opening page of each chapter. It names the groups in the chapter and the order to read them. The chapters can be read in any order.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained. Entries in other volumes are cited by number too.

## What you need first

You should be able to read a short program and follow a proof by induction or by an [exchange argument](/exchange-argument/). From Volume 1 you need the memory hierarchy, expected values, [balls into bins](/balls-into-bins/) and the [Chernoff bound](/chernoff-bound/); from Volume 3, the [hash table](/hash-table/), the [linked list](/linked-list/), the [binary heap](/binary-heap/) and the [B-tree](/b-tree/). The worst-case results on caching and allocation use the [paging problem](/paging-problem/) and the competitive ratio of Volume 19, and the average-case results use the [Markov chain](/markov-chain/) of Volume 17. Garbage collection and transactions assume threads and locks, as in the [mutex](/mutex/) and [linearizability](/linearizability/) entries of Volume 22, and replicated databases rest on [Paxos](/paxos/) and [Raft](/raft/) of Volume 21.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
