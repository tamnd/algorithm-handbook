A service that runs on many servers needs a rule that sends each request, connection or key to one of them. Take a cache tier of 10 servers that holds 100,000 keys, each on server $h(k) \bmod 10$. The keys are spread evenly, but when an 11th server is added, 90,840 of them change server, and almost every cached value is lost. At least 9,091 keys must move if the 11 servers are to end up equal. A [hash ring](/hash-ring/) with 100 points per server moves 8,772 keys in the same test, and [rendezvous hashing](/rendezvous-hashing/) moves 9,061. Requests without keys have a different problem. If 1000 requests go to 1000 servers chosen at random, the busiest server gets 5.5 of them on average over 200 seeded runs, not 1. Looking at two random servers and taking the less loaded brings the maximum down to $\ln\ln n/\ln 2 + O(1)$ with high probability. The same choices come up in web front ends, sharded databases, distributed caches, RPC clients and the equal-cost paths of a data-centre network.

The chapter states each guarantee with its model, and there are four kinds. Some are exact invariants that hold for every arrival order: round robin gives each backend $\lfloor m/n \rfloor$ or $\lceil m/n \rceil$ of $m$ requests, and Maglev gives each backend $\lfloor M/N \rfloor$ or $\lceil M/N \rceil$ table slots. Some are probability bounds that hold with high probability or in expectation, when choices are random or the hash behaves like a random function; the maximum-load results and the bounds for consistent hashing are of this kind. Some hold in a queueing model with Poisson arrivals and exponential service, such as the optimality of joining the shortest queue. The rest are measurements. Least response time and peak EWMA have no optimality result, only invariants of their estimates, and the movement of Maglev hashing is measured, not proved.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these twelve entries in order: the load balancing problem, the load balancer, hash partitioning, random load balancing, round robin, least connections, the power of two choices, the dynamic partitioning problem, consistent hashing, virtual nodes, rendezvous hashing and health checking. They give the problem in both its forms, the three families of dispatch rules, the main result about random choices, the ring and its main rival, and the part of a balancer that decides which backends may receive traffic.

Skip at first the proofs of the three theorem entries: the maximum load, the theorem for $d$ choices and the max load bound of consistent hashing. Their statements are in the entries that use them. Skip also spread and load, CARP, multi-probe consistent hashing and direct server return. Come back to Maglev hashing and bounded loads after the first pass; they show what is given up when a method is not monotone.
