A database answers queries over data much larger than memory, for many users at once, and must survive a crash. Take the join of a table of sailors, 500 pages on disk, with a table of their boat reservations, 1000 pages, using 102 pages of memory. A [nested-loop join](/nested-loop-join/) that reads Reserves once for each page of Sailors costs $500 + 500 \cdot 1000 = 500{,}500$ page reads. A [block nested-loop join](/block-nested-loop-join/) reads Reserves once for each 100 pages of Sailors, which comes to 5500. A [Grace hash join](/grace-hash-join/) partitions both tables once and then joins each pair of partitions, for $3(500 + 1000) = 4500$. Choosing among such methods, keeping them correct while other users change the data, and recovering after a crash are the subject of this chapter.

Each claim in the chapter rests on a stated model, and it helps to know which kind of claim you are reading. I/O costs of joins, sorts and index scans count page transfers between disk and a buffer of $B$ pages, in the [external memory model](/external-memory-model/) or the page model of the textbooks. Most of these counts hold only under a memory condition, and the condition is part of the claim. The serializability of a locking or timestamp protocol and the correctness of a recovery protocol are theorems about all schedules and all crash points. The decision procedures of design theory and query containment are proved, and so are their complexity bounds. The optimizer is different. Its [selectivity](/selectivity/) and [cardinality estimates](/cardinality-estimation/) assume uniform and independent values, its [cost model](/cost-model/) is an approximation, and the quality of the plans it picks is measured on benchmarks, not proved. A [System R dynamic program](/selinger-join-enumeration/) finds the best left-deep plan only for its own estimates.

Group 10 is logic more than systems. Datalog is a fragment of logic programming, and its entries sit here because database engines evaluate it with joins.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these fifteen entries in order: relational algebra, the iterator model, the block nested-loop join, the sort-merge join, the hash join, the Grace hash join, cardinality estimation, the System R enumeration, the clustered index, serializability, two-phase locking, snapshot isolation, write-ahead logging, ARIES and the attribute closure algorithm. They give the operators and their costs, how a plan is chosen, what an index changes, two ways to run transactions side by side, how a crash is repaired, and the algorithm that design theory rests on.

Skip at first group 4, whose bounds need hypergraphs and fractional covers, and the research entries of group 5: index selection, learned indexes, database cracking and view selection. Skip also view serializability, the Thomas write rule and the DRed algorithm, which refine entries you will already have read. Come back to group 6 when you need ranked answers from several sources.
