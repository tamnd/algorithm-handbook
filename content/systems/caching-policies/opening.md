A cache keeps a few items close at hand so that most requests never reach the slow store behind it. When the cache is full and a new item arrives, a replacement policy chooses the item to drop, and that choice is the whole subject of this chapter. Take a cache of 3 pages and the 20 requests 7 0 1 2 0 3 0 4 2 3 0 3 2 1 2 0 1 7 0 1. [Belady's MIN](/belady-optimal/), which knows the future, misses 9 times. [LRU](/lru/), which drops the page used least recently, misses 12 times; [CLOCK](/clock/), its one-bit approximation, misses 14 times; [FIFO](/fifo-replacement/), which drops the oldest page, misses 15 times. The same question comes up in a processor's L1 cache, the page cache of an operating system, the buffer pool of a database, a content delivery network and a key-value store in front of a web service. The answers differ because the costs differ: a hardware cache has a few nanoseconds and a few bits per line, while a web cache can afford a sketch of request counts.

Two cost models run through the chapter, and every claim names one of them. The first is the worst case. A policy with cache size $k$ is compared with the offline optimum on the worst request sequence, sometimes with a smaller cache of $h$ items for the optimum. LRU, FIFO and CLOCK are $k$-competitive, and no deterministic policy does better; the bounds themselves are proved in Volume 19, *Online and streaming*, in its chapter on paging. The second is the average case. Requests are drawn from a stated distribution, such as the [independent reference model](/independent-reference-model/) or a Zipf law, or they come from a recorded trace, and the policy is judged by its hit ratio. Most policies after LRU were designed for the second model. They have no worst-case bound better than $k$, and some have none at all, but on real traces they hit more often.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these fourteen entries in order: the cache, the hit ratio, locality of reference, Belady's MIN, FIFO, LRU, the LRU cache, CLOCK, write-back, the reuse distance, the competitive bound for LRU, the independent reference model, ARC and the cache-aside pattern. They give the problem, the optimum, the classic policies and how to build one, the two cost models, one adaptive policy and the way applications use a cache.

Skip at first the policies that refine others: MQ, LRFU, CAR, CLOCK-Pro, LFU-DA and GDSF. Skip also the research entries on measurement and learning: SHARDS, hyperbolic caching and LHD. Come back to group 8 next. TinyLFU, S3-FIFO and SIEVE are recent, simple, and used in practice, and they show how far a FIFO queue with a few bits can go.

## Before you start

You need the [memory hierarchy](/memory-hierarchy/) from Volume 1, and from Volume 19 the [paging problem](/paging-problem/), the idea of a [cache replacement policy](/cache-replacement-policy/) and the [competitive ratio](/competitive-ratio/). The LRU bound uses [marking phases](/marking-phases/), random replacement needs [randomized online algorithms](/randomized-online-algorithm/), and GreedyDual starts from [weighted paging](/weighted-paging-problem/). The proof that MIN is optimal is an [exchange argument](/exchange-argument/). The data structures come from Volume 3: the [hash table](/hash-table/) and the [doubly linked list](/doubly-linked-list/) of the LRU cache, the [queue](/queue/) of FIFO, the [binary heap](/binary-heap/) of LRFU and LFU-DA, the [Fenwick tree](/fenwick-tree/) of exact miss ratio curves, a [hash function](/hash-function/) for SHARDS, and the [Bloom filter](/bloom-filter/) and [count-min sketch](/count-min-sketch/) of TinyLFU. The independent reference model is a [Markov chain](/markov-chain/), hyperbolic caching and LHD use [random sampling](/random-sampling/), the set-associative cache splits addresses with [bit manipulation](/bit-manipulation/), and the cache-aside pattern is a study of [race conditions](/race-condition/).
