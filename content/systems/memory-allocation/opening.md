A program calls `malloc` and `free` many times a second, with sizes and lifetimes that no one knows in advance. The [allocator](/allocator/) behind these calls must answer each request at once, and it may never move a block it has handed out. Take a heap of 64 words in which every block carries a header word and a footer word. Requests for 10, 2, 6, 2, 14 and 2 words fill it from address 0 with blocks of 12, 4, 8, 4, 16 and 4 words, and 16 words stay free at the top. Free the first, third and fifth block. Now 52 words are free, but in four pieces of 12, 8, 16 and 16 words, so a request for 15 words, which needs a block of 17, fails. That is [external fragmentation](/external-fragmentation/). Rounding a request up to a block size loses space inside the block, which is [internal fragmentation](/internal-fragmentation/). Every method in the chapter trades these two losses against the time per call, in the `malloc` of a C library, the slab caches of a kernel, the arenas of a compiler and the thread caches of a server.

Three models of cost run through the chapter, and every guarantee names one of them. The first is the worst case: at most $M$ words are live at any time, every block has between 1 and $n$ words, and an adversary picks the sequence of requests. In this model [Robson's bound](/robson-bound/) shows that any allocator that never moves blocks can be forced to use about $\tfrac12 M \log_2 n$ words, while for power-of-two sizes, giving each size its own region never needs more than $M(\log_2 n + 1)$. The second is a random model, in which a uniformly chosen live block is freed in a steady state; there [Knuth's fifty-percent rule](/knuth-fifty-percent-rule/) counts the free blocks. The third is measurement on traces of real or synthetic programs. Most of the heuristics of the chapter, such as first fit, best fit, segregated lists, slabs and arenas, have no worst-case theorem better than Robson's. What they have are invariants that always hold and numbers measured on traces, and the entries keep the two apart.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in seven groups, read in order.

1. **The problem and its costs.** The allocator and its rules, external and internal fragmentation, and [memory compaction](/memory-compaction/), which removes external fragmentation by moving blocks when the references to them can be fixed.
2. **Free lists and fits.** The [free list](/free-list/), [coalescing](/coalescing/) and [boundary tags](/boundary-tags/), the [sequential fits](/sequential-fit/) ([first fit](/first-fit-allocation/), [next fit](/next-fit-allocation/), [best fit](/best-fit-allocation/) and [worst fit](/worst-fit/)), the [bitmap allocator](/bitmap-allocator/) and [deferred coalescing](/deferred-coalescing/).
3. **Size classes and buddy systems.** [Size classes](/size-classes/), [segregated free lists](/segregated-free-lists/), the [buddy system](/buddy-system/) with its [Fibonacci](/fibonacci-buddy-system/) and [lazy](/lazy-buddy-system/) variants, and [TLSF](/tlsf/), which takes $O(1)$ time per call in the worst case.
4. **Pools and slabs.** The [memory pool](/memory-pool/) for blocks of one size, and the [slab allocator](/slab-allocator/) of kernels, which keeps freed objects built.
5. **Stacks, bump pointers and regions.** [Stack allocation](/stack-allocation/), [bump-pointer allocation](/bump-pointer-allocation/), [arenas](/arena-allocation/), [region-based memory management](/region-based-memory-management/) and the [thread-local allocation buffer](/thread-local-allocation-buffer/) of garbage-collected runtimes.
6. **Bounds on fragmentation.** The model of [online memory allocation](/online-memory-allocation/), the fifty-percent rule, Robson's bound and [offline dynamic storage allocation](/offline-dynamic-storage-allocation/), where all lifetimes are known and the problem is NP-hard.
7. **Allocators in practice.** [dlmalloc](/dlmalloc/), [tcmalloc](/tcmalloc/), [jemalloc](/jemalloc/), [Hoard](/hoard-allocator/), the [lock-free allocator](/lock-free-memory-allocator/) of Michael, and [mimalloc](/mimalloc/).

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these thirteen entries in order: the allocator, external fragmentation, internal fragmentation, the free list, coalescing, boundary tags, first fit, best fit, size classes, segregated free lists, the buddy system, the arena and Robson's bound. They give the rules of the game, the two kinds of waste, the classic list allocator, the two ways to bound rounding, the shortcut for objects that die together, and the limit that no placement policy can beat.

Skip at first the variants that refine a method: next fit, worst fit, deferred coalescing, the Fibonacci and lazy buddy systems, and the thread-local allocation buffer. Skip also the research entries: offline dynamic storage allocation, the lock-free allocator and mimalloc. Come back to group 7 to see how the ideas combine in the allocators that programs use every day.

## Before you start

The entries count time in the [word RAM](/word-ram/) of Volume 1, and the [memory hierarchy](/memory-hierarchy/) explains why an allocator that keeps related objects close is faster. From Volume 3 you need the [linked list](/linked-list/), the [doubly linked list](/doubly-linked-list/) and the [stack](/stack/), and for indexed fits a [balanced binary search tree](/balanced-binary-search-tree/). Bitmap allocators and TLSF use the [bit set](/bit-set/) and the [trailing zero count](/trailing-zero-count/), and the Fibonacci buddy system uses the [Fibonacci numbers](/fibonacci-numbers/). The bounds of group 6 use the [online algorithm](/online-algorithm/) of Volume 19, and the offline problem needs [NP-hardness](/np-hard/) and the [approximation ratio](/approximation-ratio/). The fits share their names with [first fit](/first-fit-bin-packing/) and [best fit](/best-fit-bin-packing/) for [bin packing](/bin-packing/), but items in a bin never leave, while blocks in a heap are freed and leave holes. The lock-free allocator needs [compare-and-swap](/compare-and-swap/), the [ABA problem](/aba-problem/) and the [Treiber stack](/treiber-stack/) from Volume 22, and the slab allocator gives [type-stable memory](/type-stable-memory/). Freeing memory without an explicit call is the subject of the next chapter: the thread-local allocation buffer assumes [generational garbage collection](/generational-garbage-collection/), and memory compaction has a garbage-collected twin, [heap compaction](/heap-compaction/).
