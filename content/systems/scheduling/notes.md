## History

Scheduling theory began in operations research in the 1950s. S. M. Johnson solved the two-machine flow shop in 1954, J. R. Jackson gave the earliest-due-date rule for maximum lateness in 1955, and W. E. Smith proved the ratio rule for weighted completion time in 1956. J. M. Moore published the algorithm for the number of late jobs in 1968, together with a simpler form due to T. J. Hodgson. Projects got their own methods at the same time: James Kelley and Morgan Walker described the critical path method in 1959, and Malcolm, Roseboom, Clark and Fazar described PERT for the Polaris program in the same year. Two decades later, Lenstra, Rinnooy Kan and Brucker (1977) mapped which problems are NP-hard, and Graham, Lawler, Lenstra and Rinnooy Kan (1979) fixed the three-field notation that the field still uses.

Operating systems met the problem with time-sharing. The CTSS system of Corbató, Merwin-Daggett and Daley (1962) used several queues with quanta that doubled from level to level, the first multilevel feedback queue. Leonard Kleinrock analyzed round robin and its limit, processor sharing, in 1967, and Linus Schrage proved in 1968 that shortest remaining processing time minimizes the mean flow time. Peter Denning compared disk policies in 1967 and proposed SCAN; Teorey and Pinkerton (1972), Hofri (1980) and Seltzer, Chen and Ousterhout (1990) refined the comparison as disks changed. Blasgen, Gray, Mitoma and Price named the convoy effect in 1979.

Real-time scheduling has one founding paper. In 1973 C. L. Liu and James Layland proved that earliest deadline first meets every deadline of an implicit-deadline task set with $U \le 1$, that rate-monotonic priorities are optimal among fixed ones, and the bound $n(2^{1/n} - 1)$. Dertouzos (1974) proved the general optimality of EDF on one processor, and Dhall and Liu (1978) showed that it fails on several. Leung and Whitehead (1982) introduced deadline-monotonic priorities, Mok's thesis (1983) treated least laxity first and sporadic tasks, and Joseph and Pandya (1986) gave exact response-time analysis. Sha, Rajkumar and Lehoczky published the inheritance and ceiling protocols in 1990; turning on priority inheritance fixed the inversion that kept resetting the Mars Pathfinder lander in 1997. Holte, Mok, Rosier, Tulchinsky and Varvel posed pinwheel scheduling in 1989, and Akitoshi Kawamura proved in 2024 that density $5/6$ is always enough, as Chan and Chin had conjectured.

Shares, timers and clusters came later. Waldspurger and Weihl introduced lottery scheduling in 1994 and stride scheduling in 1995. Varghese and Lauck described hashed and hierarchical timing wheels in 1987. Ingo Molnar's Completely Fair Scheduler entered Linux 2.6.23 in 2007; Linux 6.6 replaced it in 2023 with EEVDF, a policy that Stoica and Abdel-Wahab had proposed in 1995. On parallel machines, Ousterhout proposed coscheduling, now gang scheduling, in 1982, and Lifka's EASY scheduler of 1995 brought backfilling into use. Yao, Demers and Shenker gave the optimal offline speeds for deadlines in 1995, Topcuoglu, Hariri and Wu published HEFT in 2002, and Ghodsi and others proposed dominant resource fairness in 2011.

## Further reading

- Michael L. Pinedo, *Scheduling: Theory, Algorithms, and Systems*, fifth edition, Springer, 2016. The standard text on machine scheduling, with the one-machine rules, flow shops and their complexity.
- Peter Brucker, *Scheduling Algorithms*, fifth edition, Springer, 2007. Shorter and more formal than Pinedo, with exact algorithms and reductions for each class of problem.
- Giorgio C. Buttazzo, *Hard Real-Time Computing Systems: Predictable Scheduling Algorithms and Applications*, third edition, Springer, 2011. Periodic tasks, EDF and rate-monotonic analysis, servers and resource protocols, with clear examples.
- Jane W. S. Liu, *Real-Time Systems*, Prentice Hall, 2000. Cyclic executives, fixed and dynamic priorities and schedulability tests, proved with care.
- Remzi H. Arpaci-Dusseau and Andrea C. Arpaci-Dusseau, *Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces*. Chapters 7 to 9 are a short and free account of CPU scheduling, the multilevel feedback queue and proportional share.
- Mor Harchol-Balter, *Performance Modeling and Design of Computer Systems: Queueing Theory in Action*, Cambridge University Press, 2013. Response times of FCFS, processor sharing and SRPT under random arrivals.
