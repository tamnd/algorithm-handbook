A scheduler decides which job gets a resource next and for how long. Take three jobs that are ready at the same moment, with CPU bursts of 12, 2 and 4 ms, in that order. First come, first served runs them as they came, and the mean wait is $(0 + 12 + 14)/3 \approx 8.7$ ms. Shortest job first runs 2, 4, 12, and the mean wait falls to $(0 + 2 + 6)/3 \approx 2.7$ ms. Round robin with a 1 ms quantum has a mean wait of 5 ms, but every job runs within 2 ms of the start, which is what a person at a keyboard notices. The same total work gives three different answers, and each is the best answer to a different question.

The question changes from one system to the next. A desktop kernel wants short response times and no starved thread. A control computer must finish each periodic task before its next release, every time, and the answer has to be known before the system ships. A disk moves its head across the cylinders, and the order of the requests decides how far it travels. A cluster runs parallel jobs that each need many processors at once, and divides memory and processors among users who want them in different proportions. A factory floor or a building project has machines, precedence arcs and due dates.

Two kinds of claim run through the chapter, and every entry says which one it makes. Some rules are theorems in a stated model: earliest deadline first meets every deadline on one preemptive processor whenever any schedule does, and Johnson's rule gives the least makespan of a two-machine flow shop. Other rules are designed by invariants and judged by measurement: the multilevel feedback queue, the Completely Fair Scheduler and backfilling have no optimality theorem, and the numbers in their entries come from traces or small simulations. Many problems in between are NP-hard, and the entries say how hard and in which model.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these thirteen entries in order: the scheduling metrics, CPU scheduling, FCFS, shortest job first, round robin, the multilevel feedback queue, stride scheduling, disk scheduling, real-time scheduling, rate-monotonic scheduling, response-time analysis, earliest deadline first and priority inversion. They give the measures, the policies of a kernel and the reasons for each, one exact result for disks, and the two families of real-time priorities with their exact tests.

Skip at first the entries that restate a result in another form: the optimality pages of SJF, rate-monotonic priorities and EDF, and the second Johnson page. Skip also the research entries: pinwheel scheduling, the YDS algorithm and the constant bandwidth server. Groups 9 and 10 can be read on their own, after the general scheduling problem; they are closer to operations research than to kernels.
