## History

The leaky bucket came from the design of fast packet networks. Turner described it in 1986, in "New directions in communications (or which way to the information age?)" in IEEE Communications Magazine, as a way to bound the rate and the bursts that a user sends into the network. The ATM standards of the 1990s made it a contract: the generic cell rate algorithm of ITU-T Recommendation I.371 and of the ATM Forum traffic management specification decides whether each cell conforms, in a virtual scheduling form and in an equivalent continuous-state leaky bucket form. Credit-based flow control for ATM, by Kung, Blackwell and Chapman at SIGCOMM 1994, is the backpressure side of the same networks.

Cruz gave the theory in 1991. "A calculus for network delay" bounds the traffic of a flow in every interval of length $\tau$ by $\sigma + \rho\tau$ and derives delay and buffer bounds for networks of such flows; the token bucket is the meter for that constraint. The IETF used it to describe traffic in Integrated Services, in RFC 2212 and RFC 2215 of 1997, and Le Boudec and Thiran built the general theory of arrival curves, service curves, policers and greedy shapers on min-plus algebra in *Network Calculus* (2001). Their book proves that the leaky bucket controller and GCRA give the same constraint.

Differentiated Services moved the meters to the edge of the network. RFC 2475 of 1998 defined meters, markers, shapers and droppers, and in 1999 Heinanen and Guerin published the single rate and two rate three color markers as RFC 2697 and RFC 2698; RFC 4115 later gave a two-rate marker with an excess rate. Admission control grew alongside: Jamin, Danzig, Shenker and Zhang gave a measurement-based algorithm for Integrated Services in 1997, and Breslau, Jamin and Shenker compared such algorithms in 2000. Flach and his coauthors measured policing on the Internet at SIGCOMM 2016 and found that policed transfers lose many more packets than others.

The web brought the window counters. HTTP status 429 was defined in RFC 6585 in 2012. Fixed windows have no single source; they are the plain way to state API quotas. In 2017 Cloudflare described the sliding window counter with its measured error, and Figma described a variant with many small windows. Google's *Site Reliability Engineering* (2016) described client-side throttling from the ratio of requests to accepts. For limits across many servers, Raghavan, Vishwanath, Ramabhadran, Yocum and Snoeren gave global token bucket, random drop and flow proportional share limiters in "Cloud Control with Distributed Rate Limiting" at SIGCOMM 2007.

## Further reading

- Jean-Yves Le Boudec and Patrick Thiran, *Network Calculus: A Theory of Deterministic Queuing Systems for the Internet*, Springer LNCS 2050 (2001). The theory behind the chapter: arrival curves, the leaky bucket controller, GCRA, shapers and the delay and backlog bounds, with proofs.
- Rene L. Cruz, "A calculus for network delay, Part I: Network elements in isolation", IEEE Transactions on Information Theory 37(1), 1991. The paper that put the $(\sigma, \rho)$ constraint at the base of delay analysis.
- Andrew S. Tanenbaum and David J. Wetherall, *Computer Networks*, fifth edition. A textbook account of the leaky and token buckets, traffic shaping and admission control in a network.
- Betsy Beyer, Chris Jones, Jennifer Petoff and Niall Richard Murphy (editors), *Site Reliability Engineering: How Google Runs Production Systems*, O'Reilly (2016). The chapters on overload describe throttling, load shedding and retries in real services.
- Barath Raghavan, Kashi Vishwanath, Sriram Ramabhadran, Kenneth Yocum and Alex C. Snoeren, "Cloud Control with Distributed Rate Limiting", Proceedings of ACM SIGCOMM 2007. The source for enforcing one limit at many sites, with the designs and their measurements.
