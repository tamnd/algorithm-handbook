A public API lets each key make 100 requests a minute and answers the 101st with status 429, Too Many Requests, and a time to retry. An internet provider sells a 10 Mbit/s line on a faster link and drops the packets that go over the contract. A container gets 50 milliseconds of processor time in every 100 milliseconds and waits for the rest. A message queue tells a producer to stop because the consumer has fallen behind. Each of these decides, at the moment a request arrives, whether it may go now, later or never, and each decision must take a few operations and a few words of memory per client.

The chapter treats these decisions as one problem. A limit is a family of time intervals, each with a cap on how much may pass in it: $n$ requests in every window of length $w$, or at most $r\tau + b$ units in every interval of length $\tau$. A limiter is *sound* if its output never breaks a cap and *greedy* if it refuses a request only when passing it would. For requests of one unit the greedy rule is optimal for every such family, even against an offline limiter that sees the future, so the token bucket and the sliding log are optimal for their own limits. With requests of many sizes no online rule is optimal. These results, the equivalence of the token bucket, the leaky bucket meter and GCRA, the burst bounds and the worst-case overshoot of the sliding counter are proved in the entries. The later entries on overload, flow control and many servers mix proved bounds with invariants, queueing models and measurements, and each says which is which.

## What the chapter covers

1. **The problem.** The [rate-limiting problem](/rate-limiting-problem/) defines limits as interval caps, proves that greedy is optimal for unit requests, and gives the memory lower bound for an exact sliding window. The [rate limiter](/rate-limiter/) is the abstract type, with operations to try, reserve and wait, and seven laws.
2. **Window counters.** The [fixed window counter](/fixed-window-counter/) lets $2n$ requests pass across a window edge. The [sliding window log](/sliding-window-log/) is exact and keeps up to $n$ timestamps. The [sliding window counter](/sliding-window-counter/) keeps three numbers, lets at most $2n - 1$ pass in any window, and can refuse when the window is empty.
3. **Token and leaky buckets.** The [token bucket](/token-bucket/) enforces $r\tau + b$ with two numbers per key. The [leaky bucket meter](/leaky-bucket-meter/) makes the same decisions, and the [generic cell rate algorithm](/gcra/) makes them with one timestamp. [Burst tolerance](/burst-tolerance/) computes how much each limit lets through at once and at a peak rate.
4. **Policing, shaping and marking.** [Traffic policing](/traffic-policing/) drops or marks packets out of profile; the [leaky bucket queue](/leaky-bucket-queue/) and [traffic shaping](/traffic-shaping/) delay them instead. The [single rate](/single-rate-three-color-marker/) and [two rate three color markers](/two-rate-three-color-marker/) mark packets green, yellow or red with two buckets.
5. **Overload and flow control.** [Admission control](/admission-control/) accepts new work only if the accepted work still meets its target. [Throttling](/throttling/) delays work instead of refusing it, and [backpressure](/backpressure/) stops a producer by credits, windows or demand.
6. **One limit over many servers.** [Distributed rate limiting](/distributed-rate-limiting/) enforces one global bucket at many sites, by a static split, a shared count or leased tokens.

Two pairs of names are easy to confuse. Backpressure here is flow control between a producer and a consumer; [backpressure routing](/backpressure-routing/) in Chapter 26.6 is a routing and scheduling policy for networks of queues. The retry policy of [exponential backoff](/exponential-backoff/) is what a client does after a 429; [binary exponential backoff](/binary-exponential-backoff/) is the collision rule of Ethernet. Both sit in Chapter 26.6.

## A first reading

Read the rate-limiting problem, the rate limiter, the fixed window counter, the sliding window log, the sliding window counter, the token bucket, the leaky bucket meter, burst tolerance, traffic policing, the leaky bucket queue, traffic shaping, throttling and backpressure. That covers the limits in use, the proofs of their bounds, and the difference between refusing, delaying and stopping work. GCRA, the three color markers, admission control and distributed rate limiting can wait for a second reading.
