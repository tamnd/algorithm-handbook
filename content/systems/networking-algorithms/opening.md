A sender puts 1500-byte packets on a path of 100 Mbit/s with a round trip of 10 ms. If it waits for each acknowledgement before it sends the next packet, the link carries 1.19 Mbit/s; with 85 packets in flight it is full. When many senders open such windows at once, the queues of the routers on the path overflow, and lost packets are sent again into the same queues. In October 1986 this happened on the early Internet: the throughput between Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory and the University of California at Berkeley, 400 yards apart, fell from 32 kbit/s to 40 bit/s. The answer that TCP still builds on is the [congestion control](/congestion-control/) of Van Jacobson (1988). Meanwhile each router matches every destination against hundreds of thousands of prefixes, and its switch fabric picks a new matching of inputs to outputs every few nanoseconds. This chapter is about the algorithms at the ends of a connection, in the routing protocols and in the router itself.

The guarantees come in four kinds. Some are proved in the worst case for a stated model. [Distance-vector routing](/distance-vector-routing/) is correct after $\lceil I/c \rceil + n - 1$ synchronous rounds, a [tree bitmap](/tree-bitmap/) lookup needs at most $\lfloor W/s \rfloor + 2$ memory reads, and [weighted fair queueing](/weighted-fair-queueing/) delays a token-bucket flow with $\rho \le g$ by at most $\sigma/g + L_{max}/r$. Some hold in expectation or with high probability over random choices: [Chord](/chord/) finds a key in $O(\log n)$ hops with high probability when the identifiers are random, and [parallel iterative matching](/parallel-iterative-matching/) needs at most $\lceil \log_2 N \rceil + 4/3$ iterations in expectation. Some concern queues under random arrivals: [max-weight switch scheduling](/switch-scheduling/) keeps every queue stable for every admissible i.i.d. load, which is proved with a Lyapunov function. The last kind holds only in a fluid or periodic model, or is measured. The square-root law of [TCP Reno](/tcp-reno/) comes from a periodic model of losses, the equilibrium of [TCP Vegas](/tcp-vegas/) from a fluid model, and the behaviour of [CUBIC](/tcp-cubic/), [BBR](/bbr-congestion-control/) and [CoDel](/codel/) from simulations and measurements. Most of TCP congestion control is of this kind.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these fifteen entries in order: reliable data transfer, the sliding window protocol, Jacobson's RTO estimation, congestion control, AIMD, TCP Reno, active queue management, max-min fairness, the routing problem, distance-vector routing, link-state routing, BGP, longest prefix match, fair queueing and switch scheduling.

Skip at first the historical steps and the variants: the alternating bit protocol, Tahoe, RED and PIE. Skip also the research entries, compact routing, network coding, backpressure routing and HiCuts, and the theory of interconnection networks in group 10. Come back next to CUBIC and BBR, which most hosts run today, and read them as engineering with measurements, not as theorems.
