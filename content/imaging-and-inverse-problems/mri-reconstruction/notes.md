## History

Nuclear magnetic resonance in bulk matter was found in 1946, by Felix Bloch at Stanford and by Edward Purcell at Harvard, and Bloch's paper of that year set out the equations of motion that carry his name. For a quarter of a century NMR was a tool of chemistry. In 1973 Paul Lauterbur showed in Nature that field gradients turn the resonance into an image, by recording projections and reconstructing them as in tomography. Kumar, Welti and Ernst replaced the projections by phase encoding in 1975, which made the image an inverse Fourier transform. In 1983 Ljunggren and Twieg independently described a scan as a path through k-space, the view that every later method uses. Lauterbur and Peter Mansfield shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2003.

The 1990s brought the first ways of scanning less. Roemer and colleagues introduced the phased array of receive coils and the root-sum-of-squares combination in 1990. Partial Fourier methods were published by Feinberg and by Margosian in 1986, and the homodyne method of Noll, Nishimura and Macovski and the POCS method of Haacke, Lindskog and Lin both appeared in 1991. Jackson and colleagues studied gridding kernels in 1991, and Pipe and Menon gave the iterative density compensation in 1999, the year Pipe also published PROPELLER.

Parallel imaging followed. Sodickson and Manning showed with SMASH in 1997 that coil arrays can replace phase encoding steps. Pruessmann, Weiger, Scheidegger and Boesiger published SENSE in 1999, with the g-factor, and extended it to any trajectory with conjugate gradients in 2001. Griswold and colleagues published GRAPPA in 2002. Lustig and Pauly generalized it as SPIRiT in 2010, and Uecker and colleagues linked the kernel and the maps with ESPIRiT in 2014.

Sparsity and learning came last. Lustig, Donoho and Pauly applied compressed sensing to MRI in 2007, a year after the theory of Candès, Romberg and Tao. Tsao, Boesiger and Pruessmann published k-t BLAST and k-t SENSE in 2003, Liang proposed the partial separability model in 2007, and Otazo, Candès and Sodickson the low-rank plus sparse model in 2015. Ma and colleagues introduced MR fingerprinting in 2013, and Hammernik and colleagues trained a variational network for accelerated MRI in 2018.

## Further reading

- Zhi-Pei Liang and Paul C. Lauterbur, *Principles of Magnetic Resonance Imaging: A Signal Processing Perspective*, IEEE Press (2000). The signal equation, k-space and reconstruction from the point of view of signal processing.
- E. Mark Haacke, Robert W. Brown, Michael R. Thompson and Ramesh Venkatesan, *Magnetic Resonance Imaging: Physical Principles and Sequence Design*, Wiley-Liss (1999). The physics, the sequences and partial Fourier methods in detail.
- Klaas P. Pruessmann, Markus Weiger, Markus B. Scheidegger and Peter Boesiger, *SENSE: sensitivity encoding for fast MRI*, Magnetic Resonance in Medicine 42(5) (1999). The unfolding, the noise matrix and the g-factor.
- Michael Lustig, David L. Donoho and John M. Pauly, *Sparse MRI: the application of compressed sensing for rapid MR imaging*, Magnetic Resonance in Medicine 58(6) (2007). The paper that brought random undersampling and sparsity to MRI.
- Jeffrey A. Fessler, *Model-based image reconstruction for MRI*, IEEE Signal Processing Magazine 27(4) (2010). A survey of the iterative methods, with field maps and non-Cartesian data.
