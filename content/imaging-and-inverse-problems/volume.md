A CT scanner never sees the inside of a patient. It measures how much X-rays weaken along many lines, and a program turns those numbers into a picture of a slice. An MRI scanner measures Fourier coefficients of the image, one line of them at a time. A radio telescope measures correlations between pairs of antennas, a seismic survey records echoes at the surface, and an X-ray diffraction camera records intensities whose phases are lost. In each case the data are the effect and the image is the cause, and the image has to be computed. A small change in the data can make a large change in the answer.

This volume collects the algorithms that compute such images and the theory that says when they work. It starts with regularization, then the variational and learned methods built on it. Then come six kinds of imaging: tomography, magnetic resonance, ultrasound and radar, seismic imaging, phase retrieval and coherent imaging, and deconvolution in microscopy and astronomy. It has eight chapters and 251 entries.

## Why imaging and inverse problems gets a volume of its own

The six kinds of imaging use different physics but meet the same mathematics. A few ideas run through the whole volume:

- **Ill-posedness.** A compact [forward operator](/forward-operator/) has singular values that fall to zero, so its inverse is unbounded and noise in the data is amplified without limit. The [Picard condition](/picard-condition/) says when the data can come from a solution at all, and the [degree of ill-posedness](/degree-of-ill-posedness/) measures how fast the singular values fall.
- **Regularization with proved rates.** [Truncated SVD](/truncated-svd/) and [Tikhonov regularization](/tikhonov-regularization/) replace the inverse by a bounded one. Under the [source condition](/source-condition/) $x^\dagger = (A^*A)^\nu w$, Tikhonov's error is of order $\delta^{2\nu/(2\nu+1)}$ for $0 < \nu \le 1$, and it never does better than $\delta^{2/3}$, the saturation set by its [qualification](/qualification-of-regularization/) of 1.
- **The noise level matters.** By the [Bakushinskii veto](/bakushinskii-veto/), no rule that ignores the noise level $\delta$ converges for every ill-posed problem and all noise. The [discrepancy principle](/morozov-discrepancy-principle/) uses $\delta$ and is proved to converge; [generalized cross-validation](/generalized-cross-validation/) and the [L-curve](/l-curve-method/) are heuristics that work under extra conditions on the noise.
- **Stopping as regularization.** [Landweber iteration](/landweber-iteration/), [CGLS](/cgls/), SIRT, MLEM and Richardson-Lucy show [semi-convergence](/semi-convergence/): the error first falls, then grows, and the stopping index plays the role of the parameter.
- **Sparsity under stated conditions.** The [null space property](/null-space-property/) is the exact condition for $\ell_1$ recovery of every sparse vector; the restricted isometry property and coherence are sufficient conditions. The [Candes-Romberg-Tao theorem](/candes-romberg-tao-theorem/) needs about $s \log N$ random Fourier samples for a fixed sparse signal.
- **Learned priors with and without proofs.** [Plug-and-play priors](/plug-and-play-priors/) converge when the denoiser is the proximal map of a convex function, or under stated Lipschitz conditions. With a trained network as the denoiser, convergence and image quality are measured, not proved.
- **Exact formulas for complete data.** The [Fourier slice theorem](/fourier-slice-theorem/) is exact, and [filtered back-projection](/filtered-back-projection/) inverts the Radon transform for complete continuous data. [Limited-angle](/limited-angle-tomography/) data still fix a compactly supported object, but the problem is severely ill-posed.
- **Nonconvex problems.** [Full waveform inversion](/full-waveform-inversion/) fails by [cycle skipping](/cycle-skipping/) from a poor starting model. The [error reduction algorithm](/error-reduction-algorithm/) never increases its error but stagnates, and [hybrid input-output](/hybrid-input-output/) works well with no convergence proof.

## The chapters

**Chapter 46.1, Regularization of inverse problems** (35 entries), is the base of the volume. It defines the [inverse problem](/inverse-problem/) and the [well-posed problem](/well-posed-problem/), analyses compact operators by the [singular value expansion](/singular-value-expansion/), and treats filter methods, Krylov methods such as [LSQR](/lsqr/), and rules for the parameter, including the [Lepskii principle](/lepskii-balancing-principle/) and [SURE](/stein-unbiased-risk-estimate/). It also covers the [Bayesian inverse problem](/bayesian-inverse-problem/), whose [linear Gaussian](/linear-gaussian-inverse-problem/) case gives a weighted Tikhonov solution, and with samplers such as [preconditioned Crank-Nicolson](/preconditioned-crank-nicolson-mcmc/).

**Chapter 46.2, Variational and learned reconstruction** (30 entries), covers [variational regularization](/variational-regularization/) with priors such as [total generalized variation](/total-generalized-variation/), the recovery conditions of sparsity and the [Donoho-Tanner phase transition](/donoho-tanner-phase-transition/), and solvers such as [split Bregman](/split-bregman-method/) and the [Chambolle-Pock algorithm](/chambolle-pock-algorithm/), which converges for $\tau\sigma\|K\|^2 < 1$. It then turns to learned methods: [regularization by denoising](/regularization-by-denoising/), [LISTA](/lista/), [learned primal-dual](/learned-primal-dual/), the [deep image prior](/deep-image-prior/) and [diffusion posterior sampling](/diffusion-posterior-sampling/).

**Chapter 46.3, Tomographic reconstruction** (42 entries), starts from the [Radon transform](/radon-transform/) and the [Beer-Lambert law](/beer-lambert-law/). It covers analytic methods for parallel, fan and cone beams, where [Feldkamp-Davis-Kress](/feldkamp-davis-kress/) is approximate and exact cone-beam reconstruction needs the [Tuy condition](/tuy-condition/), as the [Katsevich algorithm](/katsevich-algorithm/) has on a helix. Then come projectors, algebraic methods such as [SART](/sart/), emission methods such as [MLEM](/mlem-reconstruction/), incomplete data, artifacts, [discrete tomography](/discrete-tomography/), cryo-electron microscopy and the [Calderon problem](/calderon-problem/) of impedance imaging.

**Chapter 46.4, MRI reconstruction** (29 entries), goes from the [Bloch equations](/bloch-equations/) to [k-space](/k-space/) and partial Fourier methods. Parallel imaging with [SENSE](/sense/) and [GRAPPA](/grappa/) pays a [g-factor](/g-factor/) noise penalty, at least 1, on top of the $\sqrt R$ loss. Non-Cartesian scans need [density compensation](/density-compensation/) and gridding by the [nonuniform FFT](/nonuniform-fft/), which approximates the exact transform to a chosen precision. [Compressed sensing MRI](/compressed-sensing-mri/) needs incoherent, [variable density](/variable-density-sampling/) sampling. Low-rank dynamic models, [MR fingerprinting](/mr-fingerprinting/) and [susceptibility mapping](/quantitative-susceptibility-mapping/) complete the chapter.

**Chapter 46.5, Ultrasound and radar imaging** (31 entries), forms images from echoes. It covers [B-mode imaging](/b-mode-image-formation/), adaptive beamformers such as the [Capon beamformer](/capon-beamformer/), synthetic aperture ultrasound with the [total focusing method](/total-focusing-method/), Doppler and [speckle](/speckle/). For radar it covers [SAR image formation](/sar-image-formation/) by the range-Doppler, chirp scaling and [omega-k](/omega-k-algorithm/) algorithms and by [fast factorized backprojection](/fast-factorized-backprojection/), autofocus, interferometry and [phase unwrapping](/phase-unwrapping/). [Photoacoustic tomography](/photoacoustic-tomography/) and [diffraction tomography](/diffraction-tomography/), which holds under the Born approximation for weak scatterers, are here too.

**Chapter 46.6, Seismic imaging** (25 entries), goes from [common midpoint stacking](/common-midpoint-stacking/), [semblance velocity analysis](/semblance-velocity-analysis/) and the [Dix formula](/dix-formula/) to [predictive deconvolution](/predictive-deconvolution/) and multiple removal. Migration follows: [Kirchhoff](/kirchhoff-migration/), phase-shift and [reverse time migration](/reverse-time-migration/). Migration applies the adjoint of the modeling operator, not its inverse; [least-squares migration](/least-squares-migration/) fits the data by iterating with it. The [adjoint-state method](/adjoint-state-method/) gives the gradient for [traveltime tomography](/traveltime-tomography/) and full waveform inversion.

**Chapter 46.7, Phase retrieval and coherent imaging** (26 entries), recovers an object from intensities alone. Uniqueness holds for almost every object in two or more dimensions, by [Bruck and Sodin](/bruck-sodin-uniqueness/), but not in one, and needs [oversampling](/oversampling-in-phase-retrieval/). The chapter covers crystallography, projection algorithms such as [RAAR](/raar/) and [shrinkwrap](/shrinkwrap/), [ptychography](/ptychography/), holography, and the methods with guarantees for random measurements, [PhaseLift](/phaselift/) and [Wirtinger flow](/wirtinger-flow/).

**Chapter 46.8, Deconvolution and astronomical imaging** (33 entries), starts with the [optical transfer function](/optical-transfer-function/), the [Abbe limit](/abbe-diffraction-limit/) and the [Rayleigh criterion](/rayleigh-criterion/), which is a convention. It covers blind deconvolution, imaging through the atmosphere, radio [aperture synthesis](/aperture-synthesis/) with [Hogbom CLEAN](/hogbom-clean/), which has no general guarantee of reaching the true sky, and super-resolution: [structured illumination](/structured-illumination-microscopy/), [single-molecule localization](/single-molecule-localization/) and the [super-resolution theorem](/super-resolution-total-variation-theorem/) for spikes with a minimum separation.

## How to read this volume

Chapter 46.1 comes first: every other chapter uses its definitions, and several use its methods. SIRT in 46.3 is a weighted Landweber iteration, least-squares migration in 46.6 runs CGLS, and the deep image prior in 46.2 and iterative back-projection in 46.8 rely on semi-convergence or Landweber. Chapter 46.5 draws on the Fourier slice theorem and back-projection of 46.3 and on Stolt migration of 46.6. Chapters 46.7 and 46.8 meet in Fourier ptychography and phase diversity. After 46.1 the chapters can be read in any order.

For a first course, take these entries in order: [inverse problem](/inverse-problem/), [singular value expansion](/singular-value-expansion/), [Picard condition](/picard-condition/), [truncated SVD](/truncated-svd/), [spectral filter factors](/spectral-filter-factors/), [discrepancy principle](/morozov-discrepancy-principle/), [semi-convergence](/semi-convergence/), [Landweber iteration](/landweber-iteration/), [variational regularization](/variational-regularization/), [null space property](/null-space-property/), [Radon transform](/radon-transform/), [Fourier slice theorem](/fourier-slice-theorem/), [filtered back-projection](/filtered-back-projection/), [MLEM](/mlem-reconstruction/), [k-space](/k-space/), [SENSE](/sense/), [B-mode imaging](/b-mode-image-formation/), [Kirchhoff migration](/kirchhoff-migration/), [phase retrieval](/phase-retrieval/), [error reduction](/error-reduction-algorithm/) and [dirty image](/dirty-image/). A second course can add Tikhonov convergence rates, the Bakushinskii veto, plug-and-play priors, the Tuy condition, compressed sensing MRI, SAR backprojection, full waveform inversion, Wirtinger flow and the super-resolution theorem. The learning path *Imaging and inverse problems* goes through the volume step by step.

Each entry stands on its own; its facts box lists what it needs, by entry number.

## What you need first

Most of the volume rests on Volume 12: the [singular value decomposition](/singular-value-decomposition/), [least squares](/least-squares-problem/), the [conjugate gradient method](/conjugate-gradient-method/), the [Kaczmarz method](/kaczmarz-method/) and the [wave equation](/wave-equation/). From Volume 11 come the [discrete Fourier transform](/discrete-fourier-transform/), the [fast Fourier transform](/fast-fourier-transform/) and the nonuniform FFT. From Volume 28 come [compressed sensing](/compressed-sensing/), [iterative shrinkage-thresholding](/iterative-shrinkage-thresholding/), [total variation denoising](/total-variation-denoising/), the [point spread function](/point-spread-function/), the [Wiener filter](/wiener-filter/), [Richardson-Lucy deconvolution](/richardson-lucy-deconvolution/) and [delay-and-sum beamforming](/delay-and-sum-beamforming/). Volume 15 gives the [proximal operator](/proximal-operator/), [FISTA](/fista/) and [ADMM](/admm/). Volume 25 gives [expectation-maximization](/expectation-maximization/) and [Bayes' theorem](/bayes-theorem/), and Volume 33 the [Cramer-Rao bound](/cramer-rao-bound/). Radar uses [pulse compression](/pulse-compression/) from Volume 44, and samplers use [Metropolis-Hastings](/metropolis-hastings/) from Volume 17.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers.
