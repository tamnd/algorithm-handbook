## History

The theory begins with Jacques Hadamard. In 1902, and at length in his Yale lectures of 1923, he called a boundary value problem well posed when its solution exists, is unique and depends continuously on the data, and he gave the Cauchy problem for the Laplace equation as a problem that fails the last condition. The tools for the integral equations behind them were already there: Erhard Schmidt gave the singular value expansion of an integral operator in 1907, and Émile Picard stated in 1910 the condition on the coefficients of the data under which a first-kind equation has a solution.

Physicists and engineers met ill-posed problems anyway, and the methods of the 1950s and 1960s came from them. Louis Landweber published his iteration for Fredholm equations of the first kind in 1951, and Magnus Hestenes and Eduard Stiefel the conjugate gradient method in 1952. David Phillips (1962) and Andrei Tikhonov (1963) proposed to stabilize the discretized equation with a smoothness penalty, which is now called Tikhonov regularization. Tikhonov and Glasko proposed the quasi-optimality criterion in 1965, and Vladimir Morozov the discrepancy principle in 1966. In geophysics George Backus and Freeman Gilbert designed averaging kernels for Earth models in papers from 1967 to 1970.

The 1970s and 1980s turned these recipes into a theory and a set of numerical tools. Craven and Wahba (1979) and Golub, Heath and Wahba (1979) introduced generalized cross-validation, six years after Mallows' $C_p$, and Stein's unbiased risk estimate followed in 1981. Paige and Saunders published LSQR in 1982. Groetsch (1984) wrote the rates of Tikhonov regularization under source conditions, Bakushinskii (1984) proved that a rule that ignores the noise level cannot converge for every ill-posed problem, and Nemirovskii (1986) showed that the conjugate gradient method with the discrepancy principle is order optimal. Hansen introduced the L-curve in 1992, Lepskii's balancing principle from statistics (1990) was carried over by Mathé and Pereverzev in 2003, and the monograph of Engl, Hanke and Neubauer (1996) became the standard reference.

The statistical view goes back to work on statistical inversion in the 1970s, such as Franklin's paper of 1970, but its function-space form is recent. Kaipio and Somersalo (2005) set out the finite-dimensional Bayesian approach for imaging. Andrew Stuart (2010) gave conditions under which the posterior depends continuously on the data in the Hellinger metric, so the Bayesian problem is well posed in Hadamard's sense. Samplers that work on the function space followed: Cotter, Roberts, Stuart and White named preconditioned Crank-Nicolson MCMC in 2013, and Bardsley, Solonen, Haario and Laine introduced randomize-then-optimize in 2014.

## Further reading

- Heinz W. Engl, Martin Hanke and Andreas Neubauer, *Regularization of Inverse Problems* (Kluwer, 1996). The standard account of the Hilbert-space theory: regularization methods, qualification, rates, parameter rules and iterative methods.
- Per Christian Hansen, *Discrete Inverse Problems: Insight and Algorithms* (SIAM, 2010). A short practical book on the discrete side: the Picard plot, filter factors, the L-curve and GCV, with test problems.
- Curtis R. Vogel, *Computational Methods for Inverse Problems* (SIAM, 2002). Parameter choice rules, including UPRE, and the numerical linear algebra of large problems.
- Jari Kaipio and Erkki Somersalo, *Statistical and Computational Inverse Problems* (Springer, 2005). The Bayesian approach in finite dimensions, with priors, sampling and imaging examples.
- Andrew M. Stuart, "Inverse problems: a Bayesian perspective", *Acta Numerica* 19 (2010). The Bayesian approach on function spaces and its well-posedness.
