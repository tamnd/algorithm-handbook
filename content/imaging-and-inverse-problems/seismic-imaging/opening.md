Seismic imaging maps the inside of the Earth from waves recorded at its surface. In a marine survey an air gun fires every few seconds, and thousands of receivers on cables behind the ship record the echoes from rock layers kilometres below. A reflector 1200 m down in rock where sound travels at 3000 m/s sends its echo back after 0.8 s, and the same reflector seen at a receiver 1500 m away arrives later, along a hyperbola. Earthquake seismology uses the same physics with the earthquakes as sources. A survey can hold terabytes of data, and the medium is described by velocities that are themselves unknown.

The work has two parts that feed each other. Velocity analysis estimates how fast waves travel at each depth, from the curvature of reflections. Migration moves each recorded reflection back to the point where it happened, using that velocity. Migration is the adjoint of a linear forward operator, not its inverse, so a migrated image puts reflectors in the right places when the velocity is right, but does not give their true amplitudes; least-squares migration and full waveform inversion go further by fitting the data. Full waveform inversion is a nonconvex least-squares problem: its results depend on the starting model, and a model too far off leads to cycle skipping. Several of these methods moved to other fields. The Stolt mapping is the core of the omega-k algorithm in radar, and the adjoint-state method is the same computation as reverse-mode differentiation.

## A first reading

Start with the seismic reflection model, normal moveout correction and common midpoint stacking: they explain what a trace is and how the classic stacked section is made. Then read semblance velocity analysis and the Dix formula for velocities. For migration, read the imaging condition, the exploding reflector model and seismic migration, then Kirchhoff and phase-shift migration as the two basic methods. Reverse time migration and full waveform inversion, with cycle skipping, show where the field stands now. Traveltime tomography is a good short detour for the linear algebra of inversion.

Skip at first the two deconvolution methods, the multiple removal group, finite-difference, split-step and Stolt migration, the adjoint-state method, least-squares migration, the Geiger method and interferometry. They refine the main line or serve one task.

## Before you start

The physics is the [wave equation](/wave-equation/) of Volume 12; reverse time migration solves it with the [finite difference method](/finite-difference-method/) under the [Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy condition](/courant-friedrichs-lewy-condition/), and finite-difference migration uses the [Crank-Nicolson method](/crank-nicolson-method/) and the [Thomas algorithm](/thomas-algorithm/), all in Volume 12. Travel times obey the [eikonal equation](/eikonal-equation/), and the inversions use [linear least squares](/least-squares-problem/), [Tikhonov regularization](/tikhonov-regularization/), the [Levinson-Durbin recursion](/levinson-durbin-recursion/) and the [Neumann series](/neumann-series/), also in Volume 12. The trace model rests on [discrete convolution](/discrete-convolution/), [autocorrelation](/autocorrelation/) and [cross-correlation](/cross-correlation/) from Volume 28. Fourier methods use the [discrete Fourier transform](/discrete-fourier-transform/), the [fast Fourier transform](/fast-fourier-transform/) and the [convolution theorem](/convolution-theorem/) of Volume 11. The Geiger method is the [Gauss-Newton algorithm](/gauss-newton-algorithm/), and full waveform inversion is a [nonlinear least squares](/nonlinear-least-squares/) problem, both treated in Volume 38. The adjoint-state method matches reverse-mode [automatic differentiation](/automatic-differentiation/) of Volume 25. From Chapter 46.1 you need the [inverse problem](/inverse-problem/), the [forward operator](/forward-operator/) and [CGLS](/cgls/).
