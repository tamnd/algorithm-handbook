## History

Sums of differences are as old as the calculus of finite differences, which grew alongside the calculus of Newton and Leibniz. The rule that a sum of differences telescopes, and its partner, summation by parts, were standard tools of that calculus by the eighteenth century. Niels Henrik Abel used summation by parts in 1826, in his paper on the binomial series in the first volume of Crelle's journal, to prove the inequality that now carries his name and the continuity theorem for power series. Dirichlet used the same method for his convergence test. In 1978 R. William Gosper gave a decision procedure for indefinite hypergeometric summation, which finds a discrete antiderivative of a hypergeometric term when one exists of the same kind and proves that none exists otherwise.

As a programming technique, the running total is too simple to have one inventor. Kenneth Iverson's APL made it an operator of the language, the scan, in the early 1960s. In Jon Bentley's *Programming Pearls* a cumulative array is the step that lets the quadratic algorithm for the maximum subvector sum each candidate range in constant time. Guy Blelloch's survey *Prefix Sums and Their Applications* (1993) collected the uses of the all-prefix-sums operation, mainly as a primitive for parallel algorithms.

The two-dimensional table came from graphics. Franklin Crow introduced the summed-area table in 1984 for filtered texture mapping: the average colour of a texture over the rectangle that a pixel covers is four lookups. Paul Viola and Michael Jones renamed it the integral image in 2001 and used it to compute rectangle features for face detection in real time; Rainer Lienhart and Jochen Maydt added tables for rectangles turned by 45 degrees in 2002. The difference array, the inverse operation, is folklore. In competitive programming it is often called the Imos method, after a note of Kentaro Imajo (2012) that treats it in one and two dimensions and with higher-order differences.

The dynamic problem has a separate history. Michael Fredman posed the partial sums problem in 1982 and proved a logarithmic bound in an algebraic model where the structure may only add and subtract stored values; Andrew Yao studied the semigroup model, where subtraction is not allowed, in 1982 and 1985. In 1989 Fredman and Michael Saks moved to Yao's cell-probe model, invented the chronogram method, and proved the $\Omega(\log n / \log \log n)$ bound for prefix parity, list indexing and subset rank. Paul Dietz gave matching structures for list indexing and subset rank the same year. Peter Fenwick described his tree for cumulative frequency tables in 1994, for adaptive arithmetic coding. The gap between $\log n / \log \log n$ and $\log n$ for word-sized sums was closed by Mihai Pătraşcu and Erik Demaine, who proved the $\Omega(\log n)$ bound with the information transfer method (journal version 2006).

## Further reading

- Ronald L. Graham, Donald E. Knuth and Oren Patashnik, *Concrete Mathematics*, second edition (1994). Chapter 2 treats sums, and Section 2.6 develops finite calculus, falling powers and summation by parts; the best place to learn to find closed forms for sums.
- Antti Laaksonen, *Competitive Programmer's Handbook*. Chapter 9 covers static range queries with prefix sums, the two-dimensional form, and difference arrays with a Fenwick tree, in a few short pages.
- Jon Bentley, *Programming Pearls*, second edition (2000). Column 8 shows the cumulative array at work inside a sequence of ever faster algorithms for one problem.
- Guy E. Blelloch, *Prefix Sums and Their Applications*, in John H. Reif (editor), *Synthesis of Parallel Algorithms* (1993). A survey of what prefix sums over any associative operation can compute, with the parallel algorithms for them.
- Mihai Pătraşcu and Erik D. Demaine, *Logarithmic Lower Bounds in the Cell-Probe Model*, SIAM Journal on Computing 35(4), 2006. The tight bounds for dynamic partial sums, with a clear account of the earlier results.
