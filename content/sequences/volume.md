A sequence is the simplest shape that data can have: items in a row, each with a position. Logs, prices over time, sensor readings, DNA, the lines of a file, the bookings of a room and the ranking of search results are all sequences. Most of the questions people ask about them come in a few kinds. What is the total over a stretch? Which stretch is best? What do two sequences have in common? How far is this order from that one? Which of these time spans overlap? This volume collects the algorithms that answer such questions.

The volume has five chapters. The first two work with *contiguous* pieces of one array: sums over ranges, and the best subarray under some rule. The third drops contiguity and studies *subsequences*, the items left after some are deleted, as in the longest common subsequence behind every `diff` tool. The fourth studies the order of a sequence itself, through permutations and the inversions that measure their disorder. The fifth treats intervals on a line, such as meetings, jobs and genome segments, and asks how they overlap, cover and fit together.

## The ideas this volume teaches

The problems are small and concrete, and most of them have short solutions. The solutions use a handful of ideas again and again:

- **Precompute, then subtract.** [Prefix sums](/prefix-sums/) turn the sum of any range into the difference of two numbers. Then a question about subarrays becomes a question about pairs of prefix sums, as in [subarray sum equals k](/subarray-sum-equals-k/), which a hash table answers in expected linear time.
- **One scan with an invariant.** [Kadane's algorithm](/kadane-algorithm/) finds the maximum subarray in one pass, keeping the best sum that ends at the current position. The [sliding window](/sliding-window-positive-sum/) and the [histogram stack](/histogram-stack-algorithm/) are scans of the same kind; each is proved with a loop invariant and paid for with an amortized count.
- **Dynamic programming over prefixes.** The [longest common subsequence](/longest-common-subsequence/) of two sequences is built from the answers for their prefixes, in a table of $mn$ entries. Much of Chapter 6.3 is about doing better than the plain table: in less space with [Hirschberg's algorithm](/hirschberg-lcs/), in less time when the sequences are similar with the [Myers diff algorithm](/myers-diff/).
- **Divide and conquer, and data structures as counters.** [Counting inversions](/counting-inversions/) takes $\Theta(n^2)$ time by brute force and $O(n \log n)$ by counting during a [merge sort](/merge-sort/) or with a [Fenwick tree](/fenwick-tree/).
- **Sort, then sweep.** Most interval problems become easy once the endpoints are sorted. A sweep over them finds the [maximum overlap](/sweep-maximum-overlap/), [merges intervals](/interval-merging-sort/) and measures the length of a union.
- **Greedy choices with a proof.** [Interval covering](/interval-covering-greedy/) and [interval piercing](/interval-piercing-greedy/) are solved by greedy rules whose correctness rests on an exchange or "stays ahead" argument. When weights enter, the greedy rule fails and [dynamic programming](/weighted-interval-dp/) takes over.
- **Structure theorems.** Some answers are theorems about all sequences. The [Erdős-Szekeres theorem](/erdos-szekeres-theorem/) says every long enough sequence has a long monotone subsequence. The [interval coloring depth theorem](/interval-coloring-depth-theorem/) says the number of rooms needed for a set of meetings equals the most meetings that overlap at one time.
- **Limits.** Several entries prove that no algorithm can do much better. The [Fredman-Saks lower bound](/fredman-saks-partial-sums-lower-bound/) limits dynamic prefix sums, and the [Backurs-Indyk lower bound](/backurs-indyk-lcs-lower-bound/) shows that the longest common subsequence has no strongly subquadratic algorithm unless the strong exponential time hypothesis fails.

## The chapters

**Chapter 6.1, Prefix sums and differences**, is the foundation of the volume. It solves the [range sum query](/range-sum-query/) with [prefix sums](/prefix-sums/) and its mirror, the range add, with the [difference array](/difference-array/), in one and two dimensions. It explains both through the [telescoping sum](/telescoping-sum/) and the calculus of finite sums, and ends with the [partial sums problem](/partial-sums-problem/), where the array changes between queries.

**Chapter 6.2, Subarray problems**, asks for the best contiguous piece of an array. The center is the [maximum subarray problem](/maximum-subarray-problem/), solved in linear time by [Kadane's algorithm](/kadane-algorithm/) and in $\Theta(n \log n)$ by [divide and conquer](/maximum-subarray-divide-and-conquer/). Around it are its circular, product and two-dimensional forms, the counting problems solved with prefix sums and a hash table, the windows solved with two pointers, and the [largest rectangle in a histogram](/largest-rectangle-in-histogram/), solved with a monotonic stack.

**Chapter 6.3, Subsequence problems**, allows gaps. It covers the [longest common subsequence](/longest-common-subsequence/) and its [dynamic program](/lcs-dynamic-programming/), the [shortest edit script](/shortest-edit-script/) that diff tools print, the [longest increasing subsequence](/longest-increasing-subsequence/) in $O(n \log n)$ time, and counting problems on subsequences. It ends with faster methods for special inputs and with the conditional lower bound for the general case.

**Chapter 6.4, Inversions and permutations**, studies order itself. It defines the [permutation](/permutation/), its [cycles](/permutation-cycles/) and its [inversions](/inversion/); counts inversions in $O(n \log n)$ time; and shows that a random permutation has $n(n-1)/4$ of them [on average](/expected-number-of-inversions/). Then come the codes that number permutations, such as the [Lehmer code](/lehmer-code/), the distances that compare two rankings, such as the [Kendall tau distance](/kendall-tau-distance/), and the rearrangement problems of genome biology, such as [sorting by reversals](/sorting-by-reversals/).

**Chapter 6.5, Interval problems**, works with intervals on a line. It merges, intersects and covers them, finds the [maximum overlap](/maximum-overlap/), and schedules them, with a greedy rule when all intervals count the same and with [weighted interval scheduling](/weighted-interval-scheduling/) when they have weights. It ends with the structure of interval families: the [Helly property](/helly-property-intervals/), the [consecutive ones property](/consecutive-ones-property/) and the [PQ-tree](/pq-tree/) that represents all orders satisfying it.

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course, read these entries in this order. From Chapter 6.1: [prefix sums](/prefix-sums/) and the [difference array](/difference-array/). From Chapter 6.2: the [maximum subarray problem](/maximum-subarray-problem/), [Kadane's algorithm](/kadane-algorithm/), [prefix sum hash counting](/prefix-sum-hash-counting/) and the [histogram stack algorithm](/histogram-stack-algorithm/). From Chapter 6.3: [subsequence](/subsequence/), the [longest common subsequence](/longest-common-subsequence/), the [LCS dynamic program](/lcs-dynamic-programming/) and the [longest increasing subsequence](/longest-increasing-subsequence/). From Chapter 6.4: [permutation](/permutation/), [inversion](/inversion/), [counting inversions by merge sort](/inversion-count-by-merge-sort/) and the [cycle decomposition](/permutation-cycles/). From Chapter 6.5: [interval merging by sorting](/interval-merging-sort/), the [sweep for maximum overlap](/sweep-maximum-overlap/) and the [weighted interval scheduling dynamic program](/weighted-interval-dp/). Do the exercises marked ◦ and • at the end of these chapters. That is a complete first unit on sequences.

The chapters depend on each other only lightly. Chapter 6.1 comes first, since prefix sums are used throughout Chapter 6.2. The longest increasing subsequence of Chapter 6.3 is used again in Chapter 6.4, for the Ulam distance and measures of presortedness. Otherwise the chapters can be read in any order. The opening page of each chapter names its groups and the order to read them, and the entries marked *Advanced* and *Research* are the place to go deeper.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained. Entries in other volumes are cited by number too: 1.4.14 is the [loop invariant](/loop-invariant/) in Volume 1, and 2.7.9 is the [sliding window](/sliding-window/) in Volume 2.

## What you need first

You should be able to read a short program and follow a proof by induction. From Volume 1 you need [asymptotic notation](/asymptotic-notation/), [loop invariants](/loop-invariant/) and, for the average-case results, [linearity of expectation](/linearity-of-expectation/). From Volume 2 you need [two pointers](/two-pointers/) and the [sliding window](/sliding-window/), [optimal substructure](/optimal-substructure/) and [tabulation](/tabulation/) for the dynamic programs, and the [greedy method](/greedy-algorithm/) for the interval problems. From Volume 3 you need [arrays](/array/), [hash tables](/hash-table/), the [monotonic stack](/monotonic-stack/) and the [Fenwick tree](/fenwick-tree/). From Volume 4 you need [merge sort](/merge-sort/), and from Volume 5 [binary search](/binary-search/). A few advanced entries draw on graph theory from Volume 7; their facts boxes say where.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
