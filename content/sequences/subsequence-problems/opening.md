Run `diff` on two versions of a file and it prints the lines you removed and the lines you added. Behind that output is one question: which lines do the two versions have in common, in the same order? The lines that stay form a [common subsequence](/longest-common-subsequence/) of the two files, and the longer it is, the shorter the list of changes. A subsequence keeps some items of a sequence in their order and may skip any number of items between them. A substring may not skip. Allowing gaps makes subsequence problems harder than substring problems, and it is the reason most of them are solved by dynamic programming rather than by a scan.

This chapter collects the problems in which the answer is a subsequence: find one, count them, or find the best one under some rule. Most of the methods are tables filled cell by cell. A few are greedy, and a few use binary search, bit operations or divide and conquer to beat the plain table.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in five groups, read in order.

1. **Subsequences and how to find them.** What a [subsequence](/subsequence/) is, and how to [test](/subsequence-checking/) whether a pattern is one with a single greedy scan. Then counting: how many times a pattern [occurs](/count-subsequence-occurrences/) as a subsequence, how many [distinct subsequences](/distinct-subsequences/) a string has, and the [shortest window](/minimum-window-subsequence/) that holds a pattern. The [subsequence automaton](/subsequence-automaton/) answers many tests against one text.
2. **The best subsequence of one sequence.** The heaviest subsequence with [no two items adjacent](/max-sum-non-adjacent-subsequence/), and the [longest increasing subsequence](/longest-increasing-subsequence/), found in $O(n \log n)$ time with piles and binary search. The [Erdős–Szekeres theorem](/erdos-szekeres-theorem/) and [Dilworth's theorem](/dilworth-theorem/) say how increasing and decreasing subsequences trade off.
3. **The longest common subsequence.** The problem, its [optimal substructure](/lcs-optimal-substructure/), and the [table that solves it](/lcs-dynamic-programming/) in $\Theta(mn)$ time. The [shortest common supersequence](/shortest-common-supersequence/) and the [longest palindromic subsequence](/longest-palindromic-subsequence/) reduce to it.
4. **Edit scripts and diff.** The [shortest edit script](/shortest-edit-script/) of insertions and deletions, and the two algorithms behind `diff`: [Myers' algorithm](/myers-diff/), fast when the files differ little, and [Hunt–Szymanski](/hunt-szymanski/), fast when few lines match.
5. **Less space, fewer steps, and a limit.** [Hirschberg's algorithm](/hirschberg-lcs/) finds a longest common subsequence in linear space. The [bit-parallel method](/bit-parallel-lcs/) packs a column of the table into machine words. The [conditional lower bound](/backurs-indyk-lcs-lower-bound/) says that, unless a widely believed hypothesis fails, no algorithm runs in $O(n^{2-\varepsilon})$ time.

For a first reading, take these eleven entries in order: [subsequence](/subsequence/), [subsequence checking](/subsequence-checking/), [the maximum-sum non-adjacent subsequence](/max-sum-non-adjacent-subsequence/), [the longest increasing subsequence](/longest-increasing-subsequence/), [the Erdős–Szekeres theorem](/erdos-szekeres-theorem/), [the longest common subsequence](/longest-common-subsequence/), [its optimal substructure](/lcs-optimal-substructure/), [the LCS table](/lcs-dynamic-programming/), [the shortest edit script](/shortest-edit-script/), [the shortest common supersequence](/shortest-common-supersequence/) and [Hirschberg's algorithm](/hirschberg-lcs/). The LCS table is the model for the whole chapter: once you can fill it and trace back through it, most other entries are a change to the recurrence or to the order in which cells are filled.

## Before you start

You need dynamic programming from Volume 2: [optimal substructure](/optimal-substructure/), [tabulation](/tabulation/), [traceback](/traceback/) and the [rolling array](/rolling-array-space-reduction/) that cuts a table to one row. The longest palindromic subsequence is an example of [interval DP](/interval-dp/), and Hirschberg's algorithm uses [divide and conquer](/divide-and-conquer-paradigm/). For the longest increasing subsequence you need [binary search](/binary-search/), and it helps to have seen [patience sorting](/patience-sorting/). Subsequence checking is a [two-pointer](/two-pointers/) scan. Dilworth's theorem leans on [bipartite matching](/bipartite-matching/) and [Kőnig's theorem](/konig-theorem/) from Volume 7. The bit-parallel method assumes the [word RAM](/word-ram/), and the lower bound uses the [strong exponential time hypothesis](/strong-exponential-time-hypothesis/).
