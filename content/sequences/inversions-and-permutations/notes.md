## History

Inversions are older than computers. Gabriel Cramer used the parity of the number of inversions to fix the signs in his rule for solving linear equations in 1750, and the sign of a permutation has been part of the theory of determinants ever since. In the nineteenth century Augustin-Louis Cauchy and Arthur Cayley wrote permutations as products of cycles, and Cayley noted that the fewest swaps that turn one arrangement into another is $n$ minus the number of cycles. Percy MacMahon showed in the early twentieth century that the inversion count and the major index have the same distribution, and Dominique Foata later gave a bijection that explains it. The method for the next permutation in lexicographic order is much older: Nārāyaṇa Paṇḍita described it in the fourteenth century.

Statistics brought the distances. Charles Spearman proposed the footrule in 1906 as a measure of how far two rankings disagree, and Maurice Kendall introduced his $\tau$ in 1938, with the inversion count at its centre. Persi Diaconis and Ronald Graham proved in 1977 that the footrule lies between the Kendall distance plus the Cayley distance and twice the Kendall distance. Diaconis's book of 1988 then treated these distances as metrics on the symmetric group. William Knight gave an $O(n \log n)$ algorithm for Kendall's $\tau$ in 1966, by counting inversions while merging. The Kleinberg–Tardos textbook made the same idea a standard example of divide and conquer.

Computer science brought the algorithms on permutations. Derrick Lehmer used the code that bears his name to number permutations around 1960, and Wendy Myrvold and Frank Ruskey gave a linear-time ranking in 2001 for an order other than the lexicographic one. Donald Knuth's *Fundamental Algorithms* (1968) showed how to invert a permutation in place, and asked which permutations a stack can produce; the answer is the ones that avoid a pattern. Robert Tarjan and Vaughan Pratt studied networks of stacks and queues in 1972 and 1973. Rodica Simion and Frank Schmidt counted the permutations that avoid sets of patterns of length 3 in 1985. Adam Marcus and Gábor Tardos proved the Stanley–Wilf conjecture in 2004: for every pattern, the number of permutations of $[n]$ that avoid it grows at most exponentially. Prosenjit Bose, Jonathan Buss and Anna Lubiw showed in 1998 that testing for a pattern is NP-complete. Faith Fich, Ian Munro and Patricio Poblete analyzed in 1995 how much memory a read-only permutation needs.

The puzzles with restricted moves have two sources. Jacob Goodman posed the pancake problem in 1975 under the name Harry Dweighter, and William Gates and Christos Papadimitriou gave the first bounds on it in 1979. In biology, the order of genes along a chromosome changes by reversals and block moves. Sridhar Hannenhalli and Pavel Pevzner showed in 1995 that sorting a signed permutation by reversals takes polynomial time. Alberto Caprara proved the unsigned version NP-hard soon after, and Vineet Bafna and Pevzner gave the first approximation for sorting by transpositions. Laurent Bulteau, Guillaume Fertin and Irena Rusu proved sorting by transpositions NP-hard in 2012 and pancake sorting NP-hard in 2015.

## Further reading

- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 3: Sorting and Searching*, second edition (1998). Section 5.1 covers inversions, inversion tables, the Mahonian numbers and permutations of multisets, with their history and many exercises.
- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 1: Fundamental Algorithms*, third edition (1997). Section 1.3.3 treats cycles, inverses and applying a permutation in place, and Section 2.2.1 has the problems on stacks that started the study of patterns.
- Richard P. Stanley, *Enumerative Combinatorics, Volume 1*, second edition (2012). Chapter 1 covers cycles, inversions, descents and the major index from the point of view of counting.
- Jon Kleinberg and Éva Tardos, *Algorithm Design* (2006). Section 5.3 counts inversions by merge sort and explains why it matters for comparing rankings.
- Persi Diaconis, *Group Representations in Probability and Statistics* (1988). Chapter 6 compares the metrics on permutations, including the Kendall, Cayley, Spearman and Ulam distances.
