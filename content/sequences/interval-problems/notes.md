## History

The oldest result in the chapter comes from geometry. Eduard Helly proved in a paper published in 1923 that if every $d + 1$ of a finite family of convex sets in $d$ dimensions meet, then all of them meet. The case $d = 1$ is the [Helly property](/helly-property-intervals/) of intervals. The graph theory of intervals began in the 1960s. Delbert Fulkerson and Oliver Gross showed in 1965 that a graph is an interval graph exactly when its clique matrix has the [consecutive ones property](/consecutive-ones-property/), and Alan Tucker characterized that property by forbidden submatrices in 1972. Kellogg Booth and George Lueker introduced the [PQ-tree](/pq-tree/) in 1976. It tests the property in linear time, and with it they recognized interval graphs and tested planarity in linear time.

The sort-and-scan algorithms are folklore, and their lower bounds came in the 1970s and 1980s. Victor Klee asked in the *American Mathematical Monthly* in 1977 whether the measure of a union of $n$ intervals can be found in fewer than $O(n \log n)$ steps. Michael Fredman and Bruce Weide answered no in 1978, for the linear decision tree model. Michael Ben-Or's 1983 bound for algebraic computation trees gives the $\Omega(n \log n)$ bound for element distinctness, and the reductions by point intervals carry it to merging, depth, piercing and scheduling. For containment, the scan that keeps the intervals no other interval covers is a one-dimensional relative of the maxima algorithm of H. T. Kung, F. Luccio and F. P. Preparata (1975).

The scheduling problems grew out of operations research and compilers. Interval scheduling and interval partitioning are now standard examples of greedy proofs, and weighted interval scheduling is a standard first example of dynamic programming in Kleinberg and Tardos's textbook. Esther Arkin and Ellen Silverberg solved the weighted problem on $k$ identical machines as a minimum cost flow in 1987. In the same year Mihalis Yannakakis and Fanica Gavril studied the maximum $k$-colorable subgraph of a chordal graph, which is the same problem in graph terms. Martin Carlisle and Errol Lloyd gave the fast algorithms for the unweighted case in 1995. In compilers, Massimiliano Poletto and Vivek Sarkar's linear scan register allocation (1999) runs the partitioning greedy over the live ranges of variables.

The query structures come from computational geometry. Herbert Edelsbrunner and Edward McCreight proposed the centered interval tree around 1980, and McCreight's priority search trees (1985) answer stabbing queries in $O(\log n + k)$ time with linear space. In artificial intelligence, James Allen's 1983 paper on temporal intervals introduced the thirteen relations of [Allen's interval algebra](/allens-interval-algebra/) and reasoned with them by path consistency. Marc Vilain and Henry Kautz showed in 1986 that consistency for the full algebra is NP-complete. Bernhard Nebel and Hans-Jürgen Bürckert found in 1995 the largest tractable subclass that contains the basic relations, the ORD-Horn relations.

## Further reading

- Jon Kleinberg and Éva Tardos, *Algorithm Design* (2006). Chapters 4 and 6 give interval scheduling, interval partitioning and weighted interval scheduling with careful stays-ahead and exchange proofs. This is the best first source for groups 3 to 5.
- Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest and Clifford Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, fourth edition (2022). The section on activity selection and the section on interval trees as augmented search trees cover the greedy and the query structure.
- Mark de Berg, Otfried Cheong, Marc van Kreveld and Mark Overmars, *Computational Geometry: Algorithms and Applications*, third edition (2008). Chapter 10 presents centered interval trees, priority search trees and segment trees for stabbing queries.
- Martin Charles Golumbic, *Algorithmic Graph Theory and Perfect Graphs*, second edition (2004). Its chapter on interval graphs covers the Helly property, the clique matrix and the link between interval families and perfect graphs.
- Kellogg S. Booth and George S. Lueker, "Testing for the consecutive ones property, interval graphs, and graph planarity using PQ-tree algorithms", *Journal of Computer and System Sciences* (1976). The original paper on PQ-trees, with the templates and the linear-time bound.
