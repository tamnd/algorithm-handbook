An interval is a stretch of a line from one end point to another: a meeting from 9 to 11, a gene from base 1200 to base 3400 of a chromosome, the range of addresses a program has mapped, the lifetime of a variable in compiled code. Most questions about such things are questions about a set of intervals. Which moments is the room busy? How many rooms do the meetings need? Which genes overlap this region? At how few moments must a guard walk through a museum to see every visitor, if each visitor stays for a known time?

The problems of this chapter are small to state, and most of them have a short algorithm: sort the intervals or their end points, then make one pass. What makes them worth a chapter is the care they need. Every problem comes in three versions, for closed intervals $[l, r]$, half-open intervals $[l, r)$ and open intervals $(l, r)$, and the versions differ exactly when two intervals touch. The closed intervals $[1, 3]$ and $[3, 5]$ meet, so two meetings with these times need two rooms; the half-open $[1, 3)$ and $[3, 5)$ do not, so one room is enough. The entries state each test for each type, and many of the bugs in real interval code come from a comparison written with $<$ where it needed $\le$.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in seven groups, read in order.

1. **Merging, union and intersection.** [Interval merging](/interval-merging/) replaces a list of intervals by the disjoint pieces of their union, by [sorting and one scan](/interval-merging-sort/). The same scan gives the [length of the union](/interval-union-length/), and a [two-pointer scan](/interval-list-intersection-two-pointers/) intersects two tidy lists in linear time.
2. **Nesting and the relations between two intervals.** [Interval containment](/interval-containment/) finds the intervals that lie inside others. [Allen's interval algebra](/allens-interval-algebra/) names the thirteen ways two intervals can relate.
3. **Depth and partitioning.** The [maximum overlap](/maximum-overlap/), or depth, is the largest number of intervals through one point; a [sweep](/sweep-maximum-overlap/) finds it. The [depth theorem](/interval-coloring-depth-theorem/) says that the depth is also the fewest rooms the intervals need, and the [partitioning greedy](/interval-partitioning-greedy/) finds such an assignment.
4. **Covering and piercing.** The fewest intervals that [cover](/interval-covering/) a target, and the fewest points that [pierce](/interval-piercing/) every interval. Both fall to a greedy with a stays-ahead proof.
5. **Choosing intervals with weights.** [Weighted interval scheduling](/weighted-interval-scheduling/) picks disjoint intervals of largest total weight, by a [dynamic program](/weighted-interval-dp/). With $k$ machines it becomes a minimum cost flow.
6. **Queries on a stored set.** [Stabbing queries](/interval-stabbing-query/) and [overlap queries](/interval-overlap-query/) on a set stored in advance, answered by [interval tree search](/interval-tree-search/).
7. **Orders in which sets are intervals.** The [consecutive ones property](/consecutive-ones-property/) and the [PQ-tree](/pq-tree/), which tests it in linear time.

Two facts run through all the groups. First, sorting is the main cost: almost every algorithm here runs in $\Theta(n \log n)$ time for the sort and $O(n)$ for the rest. Second, that cost cannot be avoided in general. Point intervals $[x_j, x_j]$ turn [element distinctness](/element-distinctness/) into merging, depth, containment, piercing and scheduling, so each of them needs $\Omega(n \log n)$ operations in the algebraic decision tree model.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these thirteen entries in order: [interval merging](/interval-merging/), [interval merging by sorting](/interval-merging-sort/), [maximum overlap](/maximum-overlap/), [the sweep for maximum overlap](/sweep-maximum-overlap/), [the interval partitioning greedy](/interval-partitioning-greedy/), [the interval coloring depth theorem](/interval-coloring-depth-theorem/), [interval covering](/interval-covering/), [the interval covering greedy](/interval-covering-greedy/), [interval piercing](/interval-piercing/), [the interval piercing greedy](/interval-piercing-greedy/), [weighted interval scheduling](/weighted-interval-scheduling/), [its dynamic program](/weighted-interval-dp/) and [interval stabbing queries](/interval-stabbing-query/). Read the tables of touching end points in each of them closely; they are the part people get wrong.

On a first pass you can skip Allen's algebra, the union length, the queries other than stabbing, and the two entries on $k$ machines. The last group, on the consecutive ones property and PQ-trees, is advanced: it needs some graph theory and is best left for a second reading.

## Before you start

You need [sorting](/sorting-problem/) from Volume 4 and [binary search](/binary-search/) from Volume 5, and [loop invariants](/loop-invariant/) from Volume 1 for the proofs. From Volume 2 you need the [greedy method](/greedy-algorithm/) with its [stays-ahead](/greedy-stays-ahead/) proofs, [activity selection](/activity-selection/) and [interval partitioning](/interval-partitioning/), the [sweep line technique](/sweep-line-technique/), [two pointers](/two-pointers/), and [optimal substructure](/optimal-substructure/) for the dynamic program. The partitioning greedy uses a [binary heap](/binary-heap/), and the query structures build on the [interval tree](/interval-tree/) of Volume 3. The advanced entries use [graph coloring](/graph-coloring/), [chordal graphs](/chordal-graph/) and [minimum cost flow](/min-cost-flow-problem/) from Volume 7.
