## History

The maximum subarray problem came from pattern recognition. Ulf Grenander at Brown University posed the two-dimensional form, the rectangle of a digitized picture with the largest sum, as a maximum likelihood estimate of a pattern. The one-dimensional form was the simplification that he studied first, and he found a quadratic algorithm for it. In 1977 Grenander described the problem to Michael Shamos, who found the $n \log n$ divide-and-conquer algorithm. Shamos then described the problem at a seminar at Carnegie Mellon University, where the statistician Joseph Kadane designed the linear scan almost at once.

Jon Bentley told the story in his Programming Pearls column in the *Communications of the ACM* in September 1984. He gave all four algorithms, cubic, quadratic, $n \log n$ and linear, as a lesson in algorithm design, and the problem has been the standard example of that lesson since. David Gries had derived the same linear loop from its invariant in 1982, as an example of developing a program from its proof. The divide-and-conquer algorithm became the first worked example of the method in the third edition of Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest and Stein. In 2023 Kadane published his own account of the algorithm, with the version he had intended, which differs from the usual one on inputs with no positive item.

The two-dimensional problem stayed harder. Bentley's column gave cubic time as the best known, reached by running the scan on column sums. Tamaki and Tokuyama, and then Takaoka in 2002, reduced it to distance matrix multiplication and gained a factor smaller than any power of $n$. In 2016 Backurs, Dikkala and Tzamos showed that a polynomially faster algorithm would refute the APSP conjecture, so cubic time is essentially the best possible unless that conjecture fails.

Biological sequence analysis gave the problem a second life. Karlin and Altschul (1990) worked out the statistics of high-scoring segments that underlie BLAST, and Ruzzo and Tompa (1999) found all maximal scoring segments in linear time. Huang (1994) asked for regions of a DNA sequence with a high share of G and C, which is the maximum density segment with a minimum length. Lin, Jiang and Chao (2002) gave linear algorithms for the heaviest segment with length bounds, and Kim (2003), Goldwasser, Kao and Lu (2005) and Chung and Lu (2005) gave linear algorithms for the density problem. For $k$ runs, Bae and Takaoka (2004) studied the $k$ largest sums, and Brodal and Jørgensen (2007) reached the optimal $O(n + k)$ time.

The stack and window methods have a different past. The scan that finds the nearest smaller value on each side is old, and Berkman, Schieber and Vishkin (1993) gave a fast parallel algorithm for it. David Vandevoorde found the stack of open rectangles for the maximal rectangle in a binary matrix in 1992 and published it in *Dr. Dobb's Journal* in 1998. The sliding window with two pointers, the hash table of prefix sums and the deque of prefix sums have no known first paper; they grew in the folklore of programming contests and interviews, where most of the problems in the second half of this chapter now appear.

## Further reading

- Jon Bentley, *Programming Pearls*, second edition (2000). Column 8, *Algorithm Design Techniques*, develops the four algorithms for the maximum subarray problem and is still the best short lesson built on it.
- Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest and Clifford Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, third edition (2009). Section 4.1 gives the divide-and-conquer algorithm with a full proof and analysis.
- Antti Laaksonen, *Competitive Programmer's Handbook*. Short chapters on prefix sums, two pointers and the monotonic stack, with the problems of this chapter as examples.
- Joseph B. Kadane, *Two Kadane Algorithms for the Maximum Sum Subarray Problem*, Algorithms 16(11), 2023. The author's own account of where the algorithm came from.
- Kai-Min Chung and Hsueh-I Lu, *An Optimal Algorithm for the Maximum-Density Segment Problem*, SIAM Journal on Computing 34(2), 2005. The density problem with widths and both length bounds, with a survey of the earlier work.
