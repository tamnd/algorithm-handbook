## History

Belief revision began in philosophy and law. Carlos Alchourrón and David Makinson studied how a legal code should change when a norm is removed, and in 1982 they showed that maxichoice contraction gives revisions that decide every sentence. Peter Gärdenfors was studying epistemic states and conditionals at the same time. The three wrote "On the logic of theory change" in the *Journal of Symbolic Logic* in 1985, with the postulates for contraction and revision and the partial meet construction. Gärdenfors's book *Knowledge in Flux* (1988) gave the full theory. In 1988 Gärdenfors and Makinson added epistemic entrenchment, and Adam Grove gave the systems of spheres. Gärdenfors had already shown in 1986 that the Ramsey test for conditionals cannot live with preservation.

Computer science took the theory to databases and knowledge bases. In 1988 Mukesh Dalal defined revision by Hamming distance, and Marianne Winslett defined the possible models approach to reason about actions. In 1991 Hirofumi Katsuno and Alberto Mendelzon gave the propositional postulates R1 to R6 with faithful preorders, and in a second paper separated update from revision. Thomas Eiter and Georg Gottlob (1992) placed the main operators in the polynomial hierarchy. Wolfgang Spohn's ranking functions (1988) had already shown how to revise more than once. Adnan Darwiche and Judea Pearl gave postulates for iterated revision in 1994 and moved them to epistemic states in 1997, and Sébastien Konieczny and Ramón Pino Pérez stated the postulates for merging in 2002.

Argumentation as a formal model came from nonmonotonic reasoning. Phan Minh Dung's paper of 1995 showed that default logic, logic programming and n-person games share one structure: a set of arguments with an attack relation. He defined the admissible, complete, grounded, preferred and stable semantics. Andrei Bondarenko, Dung, Robert Kowalski and Francesca Toni built assumption-based argumentation on this in 1997. Guillermo Simari and Ronald Loui (1992) and later Alejandro García and Simari (2004) developed defeasible logic programming. Trevor Bench-Capon added values and audiences in 2003, and Martin Caminada gave labellings and semi-stable semantics in 2006. Henry Prakken defined ASPIC+ in 2010, and Sanjay Modgil and Prakken gave its general form in 2013, guided by the rationality postulates of Caminada and Leila Amgoud (2007).

The complexity picture came at the same time. Yannis Dimopoulos and Alberto Torres (1996) proved the NP and coNP results for stable and preferred semantics. Paul Dunne and Bench-Capon (2002) showed that sceptical preferred acceptance is $\Pi_2^P$-complete. Philippe Besnard and Sylvie Doutre (2004) gave the SAT encodings that most solvers now build on, and Samer Nofal, Katie Atkinson and Dunne (2021) analysed the linear-time grounded algorithm. Wolfgang Dvořák and Dunne collected the results in 2018.

## Further reading

- Peter Gärdenfors, *Knowledge in Flux: Modeling the Dynamics of Epistemic States*, MIT Press, 1988. The postulates, the constructions and the link to conditionals, by one of their authors.
- Peter Gärdenfors (ed.), *Belief Revision*, Cambridge University Press, 1992. The editor's introduction gives the representation theorems in a short and clear form.
- Hirofumi Katsuno and Alberto O. Mendelzon, "Propositional knowledge base revision and minimal change", *Artificial Intelligence* 52(3), 1991. The postulates on formulas and their reading as preorders on worlds.
- Pietro Baroni, Martin Caminada and Massimiliano Giacomin, "An introduction to argumentation semantics", *The Knowledge Engineering Review* 26(4), 2011. A survey of Dung's semantics and the later ones, with many examples.
- Pietro Baroni, Dov Gabbay, Massimiliano Giacomin and Leendert van der Torre (eds), *Handbook of Formal Argumentation*, volume 1, College Publications, 2018. The chapter of Dvořák and Dunne gives the complexity results in one table.
- Sanjay Modgil and Henry Prakken, "The ASPIC+ framework for structured argumentation: a tutorial", *Argument and Computation* 5(1), 2014. Structured arguments, attacks and preferences worked out on examples.
