A search system answers the query "sorted array search" with five documents: d1, d6, d8, d2 and d4. The [test collection](/test-collection/) says that d1 is highly relevant (grade 2), d2 and d8 are relevant (grade 1) and the others are not. How good is the ranking? [Precision at 5](/precision-at-k/) is $3/5$. [Average precision](/mean-average-precision/) is $29/36 \approx 0.806$. [nDCG at 5](/normalized-discounted-cumulative-gain/), with the grade as the gain, is 0.936. But the system gave d1 and d6 the same score, and d2 and d4 too. trec_eval breaks ties the other way and reads the ranking as d6, d1, d8, d4, d2. Then precision at 1 drops from 1 to 0, average precision to $53/90 \approx 0.589$ and nDCG at 5 to 0.686. A metric is a definition, and every detail of it changes the number: the tie rule, the cutoff, the gain of each grade, and what counts for a query with no relevant document.

A metric is also a claim about users, but only through a model. [Rank-biased precision](/rank-biased-precision/) assumes a user who reads on from each rank with a fixed probability $p$. [Expected reciprocal rank](/expected-reciprocal-rank/) assumes a cascade user who stops at the first document that satisfies them. The [C/W/L framework](/cwl-framework/) describes rank-biased precision, precision at k, average precision and many other metrics by one machine: the probability that the user goes on from each rank. ERR fits that machine only as an expected total gain, not as a rate. Whether real users behave like any of these models is a question for logged clicks, and the [click models](/click-model/) of chapter 35.4 fit such models to data.

Two more facts shape the chapter. First, one topic proves nothing. Systems are compared by the mean of a metric over many topics, and the difference needs a paired test, such as the [sign test](/sign-test/) or the [permutation test](/permutation-test/), before anyone can call it real. Second, nobody judges every document of a large collection. [Pooling](/pooling/) judges only the top documents of the runs that took part and counts the rest as not relevant. This favors those runs and biases the scores of new systems whose relevant documents were never judged. Half of the chapter deals with this gap, either offline with metrics that need fewer judgments or online with [interleaving](/interleaved-evaluation/) of two live rankings.

## What the chapter covers

1. **Test collections and the Cranfield method.** The [test collection](/test-collection/) of documents, topics and judgments, and the [Cranfield paradigm](/cranfield-paradigm/), which scores every system on the same fixed collection.
2. **Binary metrics at a cutoff.** [Precision at k](/precision-at-k/), [R-precision](/r-precision/), which cuts at the number $R$ of relevant documents, and [mean reciprocal rank](/mean-reciprocal-rank/), which looks only at the first relevant document.
3. **Average precision and the precision-recall curve.** [Average precision and MAP](/mean-average-precision/), and the [interpolated precision-recall curve](/interpolated-precision-recall-curve/) with its 11-point average.
4. **Graded relevance and user models.** [nDCG](/normalized-discounted-cumulative-gain/), [rank-biased precision](/rank-biased-precision/), [expected reciprocal rank](/expected-reciprocal-rank/), the [C/W/L framework](/cwl-framework/) and [alpha-nDCG](/alpha-ndcg/) for queries with several subtopics.
5. **Incomplete judgments.** [Pooling](/pooling/), [bpref](/bpref/), which ignores unjudged documents, [inferred average precision](/inferred-average-precision/), which estimates AP from a random sample of the pool, and [minimal test collections](/minimal-test-collection/), which judge only what decides a comparison.
6. **Online evaluation by interleaving.** [Interleaved evaluation](/interleaved-evaluation/) and its three main methods: [balanced](/balanced-interleaving/), [team-draft](/team-draft-interleaving/) and [probabilistic](/probabilistic-interleaving/) interleaving.

## A first reading

Read the test collection and the Cranfield paradigm first. Then read precision at k, mean reciprocal rank and mean average precision, which cover most of what papers report for binary judgments. Read nDCG for graded judgments, and then rank-biased precision and expected reciprocal rank to see two different user models behind a ranking metric. Read pooling to learn where the judgments come from and what that does to the scores. For online tests, read interleaved evaluation and team-draft interleaving. That is eleven entries.

On a first pass you can skip the interpolated curve beyond its definition, the C/W/L framework, alpha-nDCG, inferred AP, minimal test collections and probabilistic interleaving. Read balanced interleaving for its pitfalls rather than its method: it shows how a merge that looks fair can favor one ranking under random clicks.
