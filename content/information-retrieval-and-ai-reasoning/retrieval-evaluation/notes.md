## History

Cyril Cleverdon's experiments at Cranfield in the 1960s fixed the method that the chapter still uses. They compared indexing languages on one set of documents, one set of queries and complete relevance judgments, and they scored each method by recall and precision; his paper "The Cranfield tests on index language devices" appeared in Aslib Proceedings in 1967. The collections stayed small enough to judge every pair. In 1975 Karen Sparck Jones and C. J. van Rijsbergen proposed, for a larger "ideal" test collection that was never built, to judge only a pool of documents found by several search strategies.

The idea became practice when NIST started TREC in 1992. Each year the top documents of the submitted runs were pooled and judged, and Chris Buckley's program trec_eval computed average precision, R-precision and the interpolated averages in the form that most papers still report. Justin Zobel asked in 1998 how reliable such results are. He found the relative order of the systems stable, but estimated that the pools found at best 50% to 70% of the relevant documents. Cormack, Palmer and Clarke gave cheaper ways to build pools in the same year. Ellen Voorhees used reciprocal rank to score the TREC-8 question answering track in 1999. Buckley and Voorhees introduced bpref for incomplete judgments in 2004.

Graded relevance and user models came next. Kalervo Järvelin and Jaana Kekäläinen defined cumulated gain and its discounted and normalized forms in ACM Transactions on Information Systems in 2002, and Burges and coauthors used the gain $2^g - 1$ in their ICML paper of 2005. Alistair Moffat and Justin Zobel defined rank-biased precision in 2008, with an explicit user who reads on with a fixed probability. Chapelle, Metzler, Zhang and Grinspan defined expected reciprocal rank on the cascade model in 2009. Clarke and six coauthors defined alpha-nDCG for novelty and diversity in 2008. Moffat, Bailey, Scholer and Thomas set out the C/W/L framework in 2017, and Azzopardi, Mackenzie and Moffat examined in 2021 why ERR does not fit it as a rate.

Two lines of work reduced the cost of judging. Yilmaz and Aslam estimated average precision from a random sample of the pool in 2006 (inferred AP), and Carterette, Allan and Sitaraman judged only the documents that decide a comparison in the same year (minimal test collections). McSherry and Najork showed in 2008 how to compute the measures exactly when scores are tied.

Online comparison grew from clicks. Thorsten Joachims proposed balanced interleaving in "Evaluating retrieval performance using clickthrough data" (2003). Radlinski, Kurup and Joachims showed its bias and introduced team-draft interleaving at CIKM 2008. Hofmann, Whiteson and de Rijke gave probabilistic interleaving at CIKM 2011. Chapelle, Joachims, Radlinski and Yue compared interleaving with offline metrics at scale in 2012.

## Further reading

- Christopher D. Manning, Prabhakar Raghavan and Hinrich Schütze, *Introduction to Information Retrieval*, Cambridge University Press, 2008. Chapter 8 covers test collections, precision, recall, MAP, the interpolated curve and nDCG.
- Ellen M. Voorhees and Donna K. Harman (editors), *TREC: Experiment and Evaluation in Information Retrieval*, MIT Press, 2005. How the TREC collections were built, pooled and judged.
- Mark Sanderson, "Test collection based evaluation of information retrieval systems", *Foundations and Trends in Information Retrieval* 4(4), 2010. A survey of the Cranfield method, the metrics, pooling and significance testing.
- Stefan Büttcher, Charles L. A. Clarke and Gordon V. Cormack, *Information Retrieval: Implementing and Evaluating Search Engines*, MIT Press, 2010. Chapter 12 treats the metrics, pooling and statistical tests.
- Olivier Chapelle, Thorsten Joachims, Filip Radlinski and Yisong Yue, "Large-scale validation and analysis of interleaved search evaluation", *ACM Transactions on Information Systems* 30(1), 2012. The methods of online evaluation and how they agree with offline metrics.
