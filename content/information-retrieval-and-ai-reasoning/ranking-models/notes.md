## History

The first retrieval systems matched Boolean queries against manual index terms, and the [Boolean model](/boolean-retrieval-model/) stayed the norm of commercial search for decades. Graded scores came from two directions around 1960 and 1970. Maron and Kuhns (1960) proposed ranking documents by a probability of relevance. Gerard Salton's SMART system at Cornell built the [vector space model](/vector-space-model/), described by Salton, Wong and Yang (1975), with [tf-idf](/tf-idf/) weights whose idf part comes from Karen Sparck Jones (1972), and with the [Rocchio](/rocchio-algorithm/) feedback method of 1971. Salton, Fox and Wu (1983) gave an extended Boolean model that grades Boolean queries.

The probabilistic line was set out in the 1970s. Robertson and Sparck Jones (1976) derived term weights from relevance judgments, Robertson (1977) stated the [probability ranking principle](/probability-ranking-principle/), and Croft and Harper (1979) showed how to use the [binary independence model](/binary-independence-model/) with no judgments. Harter (1975) and Bookstein and Swanson (1974) modeled term counts with the [two-Poisson model](/two-poisson-model/). Its parameters were too hard to estimate, and Robertson and Walker (1994) replaced it with a simple saturating function, which became [BM25](/bm25/) in the Okapi runs at TREC-3. Measured comparisons at TREC also led Singhal, Buckley and Mitra (1996) to [pivoted length normalization](/pivoted-length-normalization/). Amati and van Rijsbergen (2002) derived another family of weights by [divergence from randomness](/divergence-from-randomness/), and Robertson, Zaragoza and Taylor (2004) extended BM25 to fields.

Ponte and Croft (1998) introduced the [query likelihood model](/query-likelihood-model/), which scores a document by the probability that its language model generates the query. Zhai and Lafferty studied its smoothing methods and showed by measurement how much the choice matters, and Lavrenko and Croft (2001) used the same framework for the [relevance model](/relevance-model-rm3/) of pseudo-relevance feedback.

Rank aggregation is older than retrieval. Borda proposed his count in 1781, and Kemeny (1959) defined the consensus ranking that minimizes the total number of disagreements. Fox and Shaw (1994) brought score fusion to TREC with [CombSUM and CombMNZ](/combmnz/). Dwork, Kumar, Naor and Sivakumar (2001) applied the theory to metasearch, proved that the Kemeny ranking is NP-hard to find for four lists, and proposed [Markov chain](/markov-chain-rank-aggregation/) methods. Cormack, Clarke and Büttcher (2009) proposed [reciprocal rank fusion](/reciprocal-rank-fusion/).

Learning to rank grew from the [Ranking SVM](/ranking-svm/) of Herbrich, Graepel and Obermayer (2000) and Joachims (2002) and [RankBoost](/rankboost/) of Freund, Iyer, Schapire and Singer (1998). Burges and colleagues developed [RankNet](/ranknet/) (2005), [LambdaRank](/lambdarank/) (2006) and, with Wu, Svore and Gao, [LambdaMART](/lambdamart/) (2010), and Cao et al. (2007) proposed the listwise [ListNet](/listnet/). Neural retrieval followed: DSSM by Huang et al. (2013), BERT reranking by Nogueira and Cho (2019), then [dense passage retrieval](/dense-passage-retrieval/), [ColBERT](/colbert/) and [retrieval-augmented generation](/retrieval-augmented-generation/) in 2020 and [SPLADE](/splade/) in 2021.

## Further reading

- Christopher D. Manning, Prabhakar Raghavan and Hinrich Schütze, *Introduction to Information Retrieval*, Cambridge University Press, 2008. The standard textbook for the Boolean, vector space, probabilistic and language models of this chapter.
- Stephen Robertson and Hugo Zaragoza, "The probabilistic relevance framework: BM25 and beyond", *Foundations and Trends in Information Retrieval* 3(4), 2009. The authors' own account of how the probability ranking principle, the binary independence model and the two-Poisson model lead to BM25 and BM25F.
- Chengxiang Zhai and John Lafferty, "A study of smoothing methods for language models applied to information retrieval", *ACM Transactions on Information Systems* 22(2), 2004. The measured comparison of Jelinek-Mercer, Dirichlet and absolute discounting that is the source of the usual default values.
- Cynthia Dwork, Ravi Kumar, Moni Naor and D. Sivakumar, "Rank aggregation methods for the web", WWW 2001. The paper that connected social choice with metasearch, with the hardness result, footrule aggregation and the Markov chain methods.
- Christopher J. C. Burges, "From RankNet to LambdaRank to LambdaMART: an overview", Microsoft Research Technical Report MSR-TR-2010-82, 2010. A short and clear derivation of the three methods and of the lambdas they share.
