## History

The array is as old as the stored-program computer: a program reached the next entry of a table by adding a constant to an address. FORTRAN, released by IBM in 1957, made arrays part of a high-level language. Linked memory came at about the same time. Allen Newell, Cliff Shaw and Herbert Simon used it around 1956 in their Information Processing Language, which they built for programs that searched for proofs, and John McCarthy's Lisp, described in 1960, made the list of two-field cells its central data structure. Knuth's first volume, which named the two methods *sequential allocation* and *linked allocation*, set out stacks, queues, deques, circular lists and doubly linked lists in the form most books still use.

The stack entered computing through the translation of formulas. Klaus Samelson and Friedrich Bauer described a "cellar" for translating and evaluating expressions, filed a patent on it in 1957, and published *Sequential formula translation* in the *Communications of the ACM* in 1960. Edsger Dijkstra's 1961 report on the ALGOL 60 translator for the X1 used a stack of operators to handle precedence, the method later called the shunting-yard algorithm. The parenthesis-free notation itself goes back to Jan Łukasiewicz's prefix notation for logic in the 1920s; Arthur Burks, Don Warren and Jesse Wright analysed a machine that reads such notation in 1954, and Charles Hamblin's 1962 paper in *The Computer Journal* treated translation to and from the prefix and postfix forms.

Several small ideas of the chapter have their own dates. The doubling array is the standard example of amortized analysis, and in 1999 Andrej Brodnik, Svante Carlsson, Erik Demaine, Ian Munro and Robert Sedgewick showed that a resizable array with constant-time access and append needs $\Omega(\sqrt{n})$ extra space and gave one that meets the bound. Knuth credits the tortoise-and-hare method of cycle detection to Robert Floyd; Richard Brent published his faster method in 1980, as part of his improvement of Pollard's rho factoring method. Knuth's paper *Dancing links* (2000) made popular the trick of putting a deleted node back into a doubly linked list with two writes, which he credits to Hiroshi Hitotumatu and Kohei Noshita (1979). In 1989 Michael Fredman and Michael Saks proved the cell-probe lower bound of $\Omega(\log n / \log \log n)$ for lists with access and update by position, and Paul Dietz gave a structure that meets it.

Self-organizing lists were studied first under random requests: McCabe analysed move-to-front in 1965, and Ronald Rivest compared it with transpose in 1976. James Hester and Daniel Hirschberg surveyed these rules in 1985. In the same year Daniel Sleator and Robert Tarjan compared move-to-front with every algorithm, online or offline, on every sequence, and showed that it is within a factor of 2. Their paper, *Amortized efficiency of list update and paging rules*, began competitive analysis as a field.

## Further reading

- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 1: Fundamental Algorithms*, third edition (1997). Section 2.2 is the classic treatment of sequential and linked allocation, stacks, queues, deques and circular and doubly linked lists, with many exercises.
- Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest and Clifford Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, fourth edition (2022). Chapter 10 gives arrays, stacks, queues and linked lists with sentinels, and the amortized analysis of dynamic tables is in Chapter 16.
- Robert Sedgewick and Kevin Wayne, *Algorithms*, fourth edition (2011). Section 1.3 builds bags, queues and stacks as code, with the resizing array and Dijkstra's two-stack evaluation; Section 3.1 has the elementary symbol tables.
- Pat Morin, *Open Data Structures*. A free textbook whose early chapters give array-based and linked lists in full detail, including the unrolled list it calls the SEList.
- Daniel D. Sleator and Robert E. Tarjan, *Amortized efficiency of list update and paging rules*, Communications of the ACM 28(2), 1985. The short paper behind the move-to-front bound and competitive analysis.
