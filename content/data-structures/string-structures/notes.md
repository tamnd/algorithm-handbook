## History

Searching by the letters of a key is as old as files on computers. René de la Briandais described it in 1959, and Edward Fredkin's paper *Trie memory*, in the *Communications of the ACM* in 1960, gave the structure its name, taken from the middle of the word "retrieval". Donald Morrison published PATRICIA in the *Journal of the ACM* in 1968, a binary trie that tests one bit at each node and skips the nodes with one child; the radix tree is its form over any alphabet, and Weiner's suffix tree of 1973 uses the same compaction for the suffixes of a text. Jon Bentley and Robert Sedgewick presented ternary search trees at SODA in 1997, in *Fast algorithms for sorting and searching strings*, and showed that a ternary search tree is built by the same partitions as multikey quicksort.

Later work made tries smaller or faster on real machines. Jun-ichi Aoe introduced the double-array trie in 1989, and it became the dictionary format of Japanese morphological analysers. Guy Jacobson's level-order unary degree sequence of 1989 stores the shape of a tree in about two bits per node, and it is the base of the LOUDS trie. Steffen Heinz, Justin Zobel and Hugh Williams introduced the burst trie in 2002 for the vocabularies of large text collections, and Nikolas Askitis and Ranjan Sinha made its containers array hash tables in the HAT-trie of 2007. Viktor Leis, Alfons Kemper and Thomas Neumann designed the adaptive radix tree in 2013 for the HyPer main-memory database. For strings on disk, Paolo Ferragina and Roberto Grossi presented the String B-tree at STOC in 1995, with the journal version in 1999.

The editing structures come from the editors of Xerox PARC. The piece table is usually traced to the Bravo editor of the 1970s, and Charles Crowley's survey of 1998 argued that it is the best general method for text sequences; Visual Studio Code moved to a piece table in a red-black tree in 2018. Ropes were part of the Cedar environment in the 1980s, and Hans Boehm, Russ Atkinson and Michael Plass described them in *Ropes: an alternative to strings*, in *Software: Practice and Experience*, in 1995.

The suffix entries of the chapter belong to the history told in Volume 8. Anselm Blumer and his coauthors gave the online construction of the suffix automaton in 1985. Juha Kärkkäinen and Peter Sanders gave the skew algorithm for suffix arrays at ICALP 2003, Toru Kasai and his coauthors gave the linear LCP algorithm in 2001, and Kärkkäinen, Giovanni Manzini and Simon Puglisi rearranged it as the PHI algorithm in 2009. S. Muthukrishnan reduced document listing to range minimum queries in 2002. Andrzej Ehrenfeucht, Ross McConnell, Nissa Osheim and Sung-Whan Woo introduced position heaps in 2011, and Gonzalo Navarro the LZ-index in 2004.

## Further reading

- Robert Sedgewick and Kevin Wayne, *Algorithms*, fourth edition (2011). Section 5.2 builds the string symbol table with R-way tries and ternary search trees, and Section 5.1 has the string sorts that go with them.
- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 3: Sorting and Searching*, second edition (1998). Section 6.3 treats digital searching, tries and PATRICIA, with their analysis.
- Jon L. Bentley and Robert Sedgewick, *Fast algorithms for sorting and searching strings*, SODA 1997. The short paper that joins ternary search trees and multikey quicksort.
- Viktor Leis, Alfons Kemper and Thomas Neumann, *The adaptive radix tree: ARTful indexing for main-memory databases*, ICDE 2013. A readable paper on node layouts and cache behaviour.
- Hans-J. Boehm, Russ Atkinson and Michael Plass, *Ropes: an alternative to strings*, Software: Practice and Experience 25(12), 1995. The design of ropes as they were used in practice.
- Gonzalo Navarro, *Compact Data Structures* (2016). The LOUDS encoding, succinct tries and compressed text indexes in one book.
