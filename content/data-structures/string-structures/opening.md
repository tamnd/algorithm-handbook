When you type the first letters of a word into a search box, the program must list the stored words that begin with them, and it must do so before the next key is pressed. A [hash table](/hash-table/) finds a whole word quickly but knows nothing about prefixes, and a search tree compares whole strings at every node. The structures of this chapter look at a key one symbol at a time. A [trie](/trie/) follows one edge per symbol, so a search costs time in proportion to the length of the key, whatever the number of keys, and all keys with a given prefix sit in one subtree. The same idea runs spelling checkers, the routing tables of the internet, the dictionaries of morphological analysers, and the in-memory indexes of databases.

The price of a plain trie is space, and most of the chapter is about paying less of it. The second theme is editing: a text editor holds one long string and changes it in the middle, which a flat array does badly. The last part of the chapter builds and uses tries and automata over all suffixes of one text, the full-text indexes of Volume 8, and ends with indexes that are smaller than the text they index.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in seven groups, read in order.

1. **String symbol tables and tries.** The [string symbol table](/string-symbol-table/), the [trie](/trie/) that implements it, the [prefix matching](/prefix-matching/) and [wildcard matching](/wildcard-matching-in-trie/) queries, and the [serialization](/trie-serialization/) of a code trie.
2. **Smaller tries.** The [radix tree](/radix-tree/), which merges chains of nodes; the [ternary search tree](/ternary-search-tree/) and its [hybrid](/hybrid-tst/) with a table for the first two symbols; the [adaptive radix tree](/adaptive-radix-tree/); the [double-array trie](/double-array-trie/); and the [LOUDS trie](/louds-trie/), which stores the shape in bits.
3. **Tries for caches and disks.** The [burst trie](/burst-trie/), the [HAT-trie](/hat-trie/) and the [String B-tree](/string-b-tree/).
4. **Editing long texts.** The [rope](/rope/) and the [piece table](/piece-table/).
5. **Building suffix structures.** [Suffix automaton construction](/suffix-automaton-construction/) and the online [algorithm of Blumer et al.](/blumer-algorithm/), the [DC3 algorithm](/dc3-algorithm/) for suffix arrays, the [PHI algorithm](/phi-algorithm/) for the LCP array, and the [suffix tree from the suffix array](/suffix-tree-from-suffix-array/).
6. **Queries on one text.** [Substring queries](/substring-queries/), the [position heap](/position-heap/), the [sparse suffix tree](/sparse-suffix-tree/), [counting distinct substrings](/distinct-substrings-counting/), the [longest common substring by a suffix tree](/lcs-via-suffix-tree/), and [Muthukrishnan's document listing](/muthukrishnan-document-listing/).
7. **Compressed indexes.** [Compressed indexing](/compressed-indexing/) and the [LZ-index](/lz-index/).

For a first reading, take these nine entries in order: [string symbol table](/string-symbol-table/), [trie](/trie/), [prefix matching](/prefix-matching/), [wildcard matching in a trie](/wildcard-matching-in-trie/), [radix tree](/radix-tree/), [ternary search tree](/ternary-search-tree/), [rope](/rope/), [piece table](/piece-table/) and [substring queries](/substring-queries/). Read the trie closely: every other structure of the first three groups is a trie with a different layout of its nodes, and its cost argument, one step per symbol of the key, comes back in each of them. The node layouts of group 2 beyond the ternary search tree, group 3, and the suffix algorithms of group 5 can wait. The position heap, the sparse suffix tree, document listing and the compressed indexes are research topics and are best left for a second reading, after the suffix structures of Volume 8.
