## History

Burton Bloom published the filter in 1970 in the *Communications of the ACM*, under the title *Space/time trade-offs in hash coding with allowable errors*. It was the second of two methods in the paper, and his example was a hyphenation program. In 1978 Larry Carter, Robert Floyd, John Gill, George Markowsky and Mark Wegman defined exact and approximate membership testers and proved the counting bound of $n \log_2(1/\varepsilon)$ bits, which made the gap of a factor $\log_2 e$ in the Bloom filter precise. The idea of storing only part of a key goes back to Cleary in 1984, whose hash table kept just the remainder of each key; the quotient filter of Bender and others (2012) is built on it.

Networking brought the filter into wide use. Li Fan, Pei Cao, Jussara Almeida and Andrei Broder added counters in 2000 so that Web proxies could delete entries from the summaries of their caches. The survey of Andrei Broder and Michael Mitzenmacher in 2004 collected the uses in networks, and Adam Kirsch and Mitzenmacher showed in 2008 that two hash functions are enough to generate all $k$. Cohen and Matias (2003) read the counters as estimates of multiplicities, Almeida, Baquero, Preguiça and Hutchison (2007) let the filter grow, and Deng and Rafiei (2006) let it forget. In 2014 Bin Fan, Dave Andersen, Michael Kaminsky and Mitzenmacher gave the cuckoo filter, which supports deletion and still uses less space than a Bloom filter at rates below about 3 percent.

The static filters came from a different line. Chazelle, Kilian, Rubinfeld and Tal (2004) stored a function on a set with the peeling of a random hypergraph, and Dietzfelbinger and Pagh (2008) recast the problem as a linear system over two elements and showed that it leads to filters close to the lower bound. Thomas Mueller Graf and Daniel Lemire made this practical with the xor filter (2020) and the binary fuse filter (2022), and Dillinger and Walzer gave the ribbon filter in 2021. Kraska, Beutel, Chi, Dean and Polyzotis (2018) proposed putting a learned model in front of a filter.

The counting sketches grew out of databases and streams. Philippe Flajolet and G. Nigel Martin presented probabilistic counting at FOCS 1983, to estimate the number of distinct values of an attribute in a database, and published the full analysis in 1985. Alon, Matias and Szegedy (1999) analysed a version with limited independence, and Flajolet's later LogLog (with Durand, 2003) and HyperLogLog (with Fusy, Gandouet and Meunier, 2007) cut the space further. For frequencies, Estan and Varghese (2002) used several hashed arrays of counters and their minimum to measure network traffic, and Graham Cormode and S. Muthukrishnan gave the count-min sketch and its simple analysis at LATIN 2004.

## Further reading

- Andrei Broder and Michael Mitzenmacher, *Network applications of Bloom filters: a survey*, Internet Mathematics 1(4), 2004. The standard introduction to Bloom filters, their variants and their analysis.
- Michael Mitzenmacher and Eli Upfal, *Probability and Computing*, second edition. Section 5.5 derives the false positive rate of a Bloom filter and the best number of hash functions, and Chapter 15 covers universal and pairwise independent hashing.
- Bin Fan, Dave G. Andersen, Michael Kaminsky and Michael Mitzenmacher, *Cuckoo filter: practically better than Bloom*, CoNEXT 2014. A clear account of a filter with deletion and its comparison with the Bloom filter.
- Thomas Mueller Graf and Daniel Lemire, *Xor filters: faster and smaller than Bloom and cuckoo filters*, ACM Journal of Experimental Algorithmics 25, 2020. Shows how peeling gives a practical static filter, with measured comparisons.
- Graham Cormode and S. Muthukrishnan, *An improved data stream summary: the count-min sketch and its applications*, Journal of Algorithms 55(1), 2005. The sketch, its error bound and its uses for range queries, quantiles and heavy hitters.
