## History

Hashing began at IBM in the early 1950s. Knuth credits H. P. Luhn with the idea of hashing with chaining, in an internal memorandum of January 1953. At about the same time, Gene Amdahl, Elaine Boehme (later McGraw), Nathaniel Rochester and Arthur Samuel used open addressing with linear probing in an assembler for the IBM 701. The first published description appears to be that of A. I. Dumey in 1956. W. W. Peterson's paper of 1957 on addressing for random-access storage introduced the term *open addressing*, and F. A. Williams described coalesced chaining in 1959. Robert Morris's survey *Scatter storage techniques*, in the *Communications of the ACM* in 1968, made the methods widely known.

The analysis came next. Knuth worked out the cost of linear probing in unpublished notes of 1963, and the formulas appeared in the third volume of *The Art of Computer Programming* in 1973. Quadratic probing was proposed by Maurer in 1968. Guibas and Szemerédi analysed double hashing in 1978 for loads up to about $0.319$, and Lueker and Molodowitch extended the result to every fixed load below 1 in 1993. Andrew Yao proved in 1985 that uniform hashing is optimal among open-addressing schemes of a natural kind, which settled a conjecture of Ullman from 1972. In 1985 Pedro Celis, Per-Åke Larson and Ian Munro introduced Robin Hood hashing.

J. Lawrence Carter and Mark Wegman removed the assumption of random keys. Their universal classes of hash functions, published in 1977 and 1979, give the expected bounds for every input once the function is drawn at random, and in 1981 they defined the stronger $k$-wise independent families. Michael Fredman, János Komlós and Endre Szemerédi showed in 1982 that a fixed set can be stored in linear space with constant worst-case access, and Dietzfelbinger, Karlin, Mehlhorn, Meyer auf der Heide, Rohnert and Tarjan made the scheme dynamic in 1988. Rasmus Pagh and Flemming Friche Rodler introduced cuckoo hashing at ESA 2001. For tables on disk, Fagin, Nievergelt, Pippenger and Strong published extendible hashing in 1979, and Witold Litwin linear hashing in 1980.

The threat that universal hashing answers became real in 2003, when Scott Crosby and Dan Wallach showed denial-of-service attacks on the hash tables of real programs; a second wave of attacks on web servers in 2011 led several languages to add fixes, most often a random seed for the hash of strings. In practice, the Swiss table, presented by Matt Kulukundis at CppCon 2017, put the probing of a whole group of slots into one vector instruction, and Rust and Go later adopted the design. The theory is still moving: Pătraşcu and Thorup showed in 2011 that simple tabulation, which is only 3-wise independent, gives expected constant time for linear probing and a high success probability for cuckoo hashing, and in 2024 Kuszmaul and Xi gave the first $O(1)$ bound for quadratic probing.

## Further reading

- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 3: Sorting and Searching*, second edition (1998). Section 6.4 has the classic analyses of chaining, linear probing, double hashing and coalesced hashing, and a detailed history.
- Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest and Clifford Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, fourth edition (2022). Chapter 11 gives chaining, open addressing and universal hashing with full proofs.
- Robert Sedgewick and Kevin Wayne, *Algorithms*, fourth edition (2011). Section 3.4 builds separate chaining and linear probing as code and measures them.
- Michael Mitzenmacher and Eli Upfal, *Probability and Computing*, second edition. Chapter 5 on balls and bins and Chapter 15 on universal hash functions give the probability behind the analyses.
- Rasmus Pagh and Flemming Friche Rodler, *Cuckoo hashing*, Journal of Algorithms 51(2), 2004. A short and readable paper on the simplest scheme with worst-case constant lookups.
