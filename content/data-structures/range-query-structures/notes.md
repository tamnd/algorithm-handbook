## History

The segment tree began in computational geometry. Jon Bentley described it in 1977, in an unpublished manuscript on Klee's rectangle problems, as a tree over the elementary intervals of a set of segments, and Herbert Edelsbrunner and Edward McCreight each introduced the interval tree in 1980. The form used in this chapter, a tree over the positions of an array with a monoid value at each node, is the form taught in competitive programming. The Fenwick tree came from data compression: Peter Fenwick published *A new data structure for cumulative frequency tables* in *Software: Practice and Experience* in 1994, to keep the counts of an adaptive arithmetic coder. Summed-area tables, the two-dimensional prefix sums, were given by Franklin Crow at SIGGRAPH in 1984 for texture mapping.

Lower bounds came early. Michael Fredman studied the cost of keeping an array and its partial sums in the *Journal of the ACM* in 1982, and Fredman and Michael Saks introduced the cell-probe method in 1989. Mihai Pătraşcu and Erik Demaine proved in 2004 and 2006 that $\Omega(\log n)$ time per operation is needed for dynamic partial sums, so the Fenwick tree and the segment tree are optimal in that model. For static queries over a semigroup, Andrew Yao (1982) studied how many stored values a query must combine against how much space is used, and Noga Alon and Baruch Schieber (1987) gave matching constructions. The disjoint sparse table and the sqrt tree are simple points on that trade-off.

Range minima were joined to lowest common ancestors in 1984. Harold Gabow, Jon Bentley and Robert Tarjan reduced range minimum queries to ancestor queries through the Cartesian tree, which Jean Vuillemin had defined in 1980, and Dov Harel and Tarjan gave the first ancestor structure with linear preprocessing and constant-time queries. These methods were thought too hard to implement until Michael Bender and Martin Farach-Colton published *The LCA Problem Revisited* at LATIN 2000. Their paper gave the sparse table under that name and the method with blocks of half-logarithmic size. Johannes Fischer and Volker Heun presented their $2n + o(n)$-bit structure at a conference in 2007 and in the *SIAM Journal on Computing* in 2011.

Many entries of the chapter come from programming contests and have no research paper. In a Codeforces discussion of 2015, Mo's algorithm is traced to Mo Tao, who used it for a problem of the 2009 China national training camp. Li Chao trees spread through Chinese contests in the early 2010s. Ji Ruyi introduced segment tree beats in his 2016 paper for the Chinese national team selection, and the Chtholly tree became known through Codeforces problem 896C in 2017. Research on queries that do not split is more recent: Danny Krizanc, Pat Morin and Michiel Smid studied range mode and range median queries in 2005.

## Further reading

- Antti Laaksonen, *Competitive Programmer's Handbook*. Free, and the best short source for the Fenwick tree, segment trees, sparse tables and Mo's algorithm as they are used in practice.
- Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest and Clifford Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, fourth edition (2022). The chapter on augmenting data structures builds the interval tree on a red-black tree.
- Mark de Berg, Otfried Cheong, Marc van Kreveld and Mark Overmars, *Computational Geometry: Algorithms and Applications*, third edition (2008). Chapter 10 covers interval trees and segment trees in their geometric form, and Chapter 5 covers range trees.
- Michael A. Bender and Martin Farach-Colton, *The LCA Problem Revisited*, LATIN 2000. A short paper that gives the sparse table, the Euler tour reduction and the block method in a few pages.
- Johannes Fischer and Volker Heun, *Space-efficient preprocessing schemes for range minimum queries on static arrays*, SIAM Journal on Computing 40(2), 2011. The space-optimal structure and its lower bound.
- Mihai Pătraşcu and Erik D. Demaine, *Logarithmic lower bounds in the cell-probe model*, SIAM Journal on Computing 35(4), 2006. The proof that partial sums need logarithmic time.
