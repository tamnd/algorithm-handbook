A range query asks a question about a contiguous piece of an array: the sum of the sales from day $l$ to day $r$, the lowest temperature in a week, the greatest common divisor of a block of numbers. If the array never changes, prefix sums answer a range sum in $O(1)$ time. The difficulty starts when the array changes between the queries, or when the operation has no inverse, as with the minimum. Then the work must be shared between the updates and the queries, and the structures of this chapter do this with trees and tables of partial answers. They are used in databases and spreadsheets, in the arithmetic coders that the [Fenwick tree](/fenwick-tree/) was first built for, in computational geometry, and in many algorithms of other volumes, such as the lowest common ancestor in a tree and the search in a suffix array.

The chapter keeps one question in view: what does the operation $\oplus$ allow? A structure that only uses associativity works for any [monoid](/monoid/). If $\oplus$ is idempotent, two answers may overlap, which gives the $O(1)$ query of the [sparse table](/sparse-table/). If $\oplus$ has an inverse, two prefix answers can be subtracted, which is all the Fenwick tree needs.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in nine groups, read in order.

1. **The problem and its operations.** The [range query problem](/range-query-problem/), the laws of the operation ([monoid](/monoid/), [idempotent](/idempotent-operation/), [invertible](/invertible-operation/)), and the standard cases: the [range minimum query](/range-minimum-query/), the [range gcd query](/range-gcd-query/) and the [range sum with point updates](/point-update-range-sum-query/).
2. **Blocks and tables.** [Square-root decomposition](/square-root-decomposition/), the general method with $O(\sqrt n)$ per operation, then the static tables: the [sparse table](/sparse-table/), the [disjoint sparse table](/disjoint-sparse-table/) and the [sqrt tree](/sqrt-tree/).
3. **Fenwick trees and segment trees.** The two workhorses with $O(\log n)$ per query and update: the [Fenwick tree](/fenwick-tree/) and the [segment tree](/segment-tree/), and segment trees for the [best subsegment](/segment-tree-maximum-subsegment-sum/), for [huge index ranges](/dynamic-segment-tree/) and over [sorted lists](/merge-sort-tree/).
4. **Updates on whole ranges.** [Range update and range query](/range-update-range-query/) with two Fenwick trees or with [lazy propagation](/lazy-propagation/), [range assignment](/range-assignment-query/), the [Chtholly tree](/chtholly-tree/) and [segment tree beats](/segment-tree-beats/).
5. **Two dimensions.** The [rectangle sum](/two-dimensional-range-sum-query/), with the [two-dimensional Fenwick tree](/two-dimensional-fenwick-tree/) and the [two-dimensional segment tree](/two-dimensional-segment-tree/).
6. **Answering queries offline.** [Offline query processing](/offline-query-processing/), [Mo's algorithm](/mo-algorithm/) and its [version with updates](/mo-algorithm-with-updates/), and [offline deletion](/offline-deletion/).
7. **Queries that do not split.** The number of [distinct values](/range-distinct-count-query/), the [$k$-th smallest value](/range-kth-smallest-query/) and the [mode](/range-mode-query/) of a range.
8. **Trees over other objects.** The [interval tree](/interval-tree/) and the [Li Chao tree](/li-chao-tree/) of lines.
9. **Optimal range minimum.** The [equivalence of RMQ and LCA](/rmq-lca-equivalence/), the [Bender-Farach-Colton](/bender-farach-colton-rmq/) structure with $O(n)$ preprocessing and $O(1)$ queries, and the succinct [Fischer-Heun](/fischer-heun-rmq/) structure.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these eleven entries in order: [range query problem](/range-query-problem/), [monoid](/monoid/), [idempotent operation](/idempotent-operation/), [invertible operation](/invertible-operation/), [range minimum query](/range-minimum-query/), [square-root decomposition](/square-root-decomposition/), [sparse table](/sparse-table/), [Fenwick tree](/fenwick-tree/), [segment tree](/segment-tree/), [lazy propagation](/lazy-propagation/) and [Mo's algorithm](/mo-algorithm/). Read the segment tree closely: most of the later entries change what a node stores or how an update reaches it, and the argument that a query visits $O(\log n)$ nodes comes back many times. The two-dimensional structures, the queries that do not split and the last group can wait. Segment tree beats, the sqrt tree and the Fischer-Heun structure are research topics and are best left for a second reading.
