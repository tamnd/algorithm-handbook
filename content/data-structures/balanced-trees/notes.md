## History

Georgy Adelson-Velsky and Evgenii Landis described the AVL tree, the first balanced search tree, in 1962, in a short paper in the Doklady of the Soviet Academy of Sciences, and showed through the smallest trees of each height that its height is $O(\log n)$. Knuth later gave the sharp bound, with the Fibonacci trees as the extreme case, as Theorem A of Section 6.2.3 of his third volume. In 1970 John Hopcroft introduced the 2-3 tree, which keeps all leaves at one depth and grows at the root; it was not published at the time, and it reached most readers through the 1974 book of Aho, Hopcroft and Ullman. Rudolf Bayer and Edward McCreight generalized it to the B-tree between 1970 and 1972.

Binary trees and multiway trees were joined in the 1970s. Rudolf Bayer described symmetric binary B-trees in 1972, binary trees with horizontal and vertical pointers that encode B-trees of order 4. Leo Guibas and Robert Sedgewick recast them in 1978, in "A dichromatic framework for balanced trees", with red and black colors, and showed that many balanced trees fit in one frame. In the same years Jürg Nievergelt and Edward Reingold balanced trees by size instead of height, with their trees of bounded balance (1972, journal version 1973). Norbert Blum and Kurt Mehlhorn corrected the range of the parameter in 1980, and Stephen Adams's functional version of 1992 and 1993 became the set library of several functional languages. In 2011 Yoichi Hirai and Kazuhiko Yamamoto proved with a proof assistant which parameters are valid.

Two other ideas came around 1990. Jean Vuillemin had described the Cartesian tree in 1980, ordered by one coordinate and heap-ordered by another; Cecilia Aragon and Raimund Seidel gave it random priorities in 1989 and called it a treap, with the full analysis in 1996. Rebuilding a whole subtree when it gets out of shape, used by Mark Overmars for geometric structures in the early 1980s, was applied to search trees by Arne Andersson in 1989 and, independently, by Igal Galperin and Ronald Rivest, who named the scapegoat tree in 1993. Andersson also gave the AA tree in 1993, a red-black tree with far fewer cases, and Sedgewick's left-leaning red-black tree of 2008 applies the same idea in mirror image.

The subject is still active. Bernhard Haeupler, Siddhartha Sen and Robert Tarjan introduced rank-balanced trees, among them the WAVL tree, at WADS 2009, and the FreeBSD kernel moved its tree macros from red-black trees to rank-balanced trees in 2020. Tarjan, Caleb Levy and Stephen Timmel posted zip trees in 2018. Guy Blelloch, Daniel Ferizovic and Yihan Sun showed in 2016 that one JOIN per tree type suffices for work-optimal parallel set operations, building on the merging algorithm of Mark Brown and Tarjan from 1979. On the side of pure combinatorics, Daniel Sleator, Tarjan and William Thurston proved in 1988 that $2n - 6$ rotations suffice and are sometimes needed to turn one tree into another, for all large $n$.

## Further reading

- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 3: Sorting and Searching*, second edition (1998). Section 6.2.3 treats balanced trees in depth, with the AVL height theorem and AVL deletion.
- Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest and Clifford Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, fourth edition (2022). Chapter 13 gives red-black trees with full proofs of insertion and deletion.
- Robert Sedgewick and Kevin Wayne, *Algorithms*, fourth edition (2011). Chapter 3 builds 2-3 trees and left-leaning red-black trees with short, tested code.
- Leo J. Guibas and Robert Sedgewick, *A dichromatic framework for balanced trees*, 19th Annual Symposium on Foundations of Computer Science (1978).
- Raimund Seidel and Cecilia R. Aragon, *Randomized search trees*, Algorithmica 16(4/5), 1996. The treap and its analysis.
- Bernhard Haeupler, Siddhartha Sen and Robert E. Tarjan, *Rank-balanced trees*, ACM Transactions on Algorithms 11(4), 2015.
