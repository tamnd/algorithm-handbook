Insert the keys 1, 2, 3, ..., 1000 into a plain [binary search tree](/binary-search-tree/), in that order, and the tree is a path of 1000 nodes. A search for 1000 then makes 1000 comparisons, as many as a scan of a linked list. Sorted input is not rare: log files arrive in time order, account numbers are handed out one by one, and a program often copies one sorted map into another. A library cannot hope that its keys come in random order. It needs a tree whose height stays $O(\log n)$ after every sequence of insertions and deletions. That tree is the [balanced binary search tree](/balanced-binary-search-tree/), and this chapter gives the main ways to build one.

Every scheme adds a rule on the shape of the tree and some data in the nodes, such as a height, a color, a size or a random priority. An update first works as in a plain tree. It can break the rule only on the path from the changed node to the root, and the tree repairs that path with [rotations](/tree-rotation/), which change the shape but keep the order of the keys, or by rebuilding a subtree. The schemes differ in the rule, in the number of rotations per update, and in the kind of bound they give: worst case, amortized or expected.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in seven groups, read in order.

1. **Balance and rotations.** The goal of [balance](/balanced-binary-search-tree/), the [rotation](/tree-rotation/), and the general method of [rebalancing](/tree-rebalancing/) along the search path.
2. **AVL trees.** [Height-balanced trees](/height-balanced-tree/), the [balance factor](/balance-factor/), the [AVL tree](/avl-tree/), and the [height theorem](/avl-height-theorem/), which bounds the height by $1.4405 \lg(n + 2)$ through the Fibonacci numbers.
3. **Multiway trees.** The [2-3 tree](/two-three-tree/) and the [2-3-4 tree](/two-three-four-tree/), whose leaves are all at one depth and which grow by splitting nodes at the root.
4. **Red-black trees.** The [black-height](/black-height/), the [red-black tree](/red-black-tree/), its [height bound](/red-black-height-theorem/) of $2 \lg(n + 1)$, its [correspondence with 2-3-4 trees](/red-black-correspondence-with-2-3-4-trees/), and the simpler [left-leaning red-black tree](/left-leaning-red-black-tree/), its [deletion](/red-black-tree-deletion/), and the [AA tree](/aa-tree/).
5. **Sizes in the nodes.** The [order-statistic tree](/order-statistic-tree/), which answers rank and select, the [weight-balanced tree](/weight-balanced-tree/), which balances by size, and the [scapegoat tree](/scapegoat-tree/), which stores nothing in its nodes and rebuilds subtrees that become lopsided.
6. **Treaps.** The [treap](/treap/), a search tree on the keys and a heap on random priorities, its [split](/treap-split/) and [merge](/treap-merge/), and the [implicit treap](/implicit-treap/), which stores a list indexed by position.
7. **Recent designs.** The [WAVL tree](/wavl-tree/), the [zip tree](/zip-tree/) and [join-based algorithms](/join-based-tree-algorithms/) for union, intersection and difference.

For a first reading, take these ten entries in order: [balanced binary search tree](/balanced-binary-search-tree/), [tree rotation](/tree-rotation/), [AVL tree](/avl-tree/), [AVL height theorem](/avl-height-theorem/), [2-3 tree](/two-three-tree/), [2-3-4 tree](/two-three-four-tree/), [red-black tree](/red-black-tree/), [the correspondence with 2-3-4 trees](/red-black-correspondence-with-2-3-4-trees/), [order-statistic tree](/order-statistic-tree/) and [treap](/treap/). Draw the trees by hand as you read; the cases of red-black insertion are easier to remember as splits of 2-3-4 nodes than as a table of colors. Deletion in red-black trees, the AA tree, the weight-balanced tree and the scapegoat tree can wait until you need them. The last group is research material, and it reads best after the rest of the chapter.
