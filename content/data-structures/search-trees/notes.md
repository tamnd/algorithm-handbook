## History

Binary tree search is as old as stored programs with pointers. Knuth reports that several people found it in the 1950s, and that P. F. Windley, A. D. Booth and A. J. T. Colin, and T. N. Hibbard published it independently around 1960. Hibbard's paper of 1962 gave the deletion that replaces a node with two children by its successor, and it analysed trees built from random insertions. Alan Perlis and Charles Thornton introduced threads in 1960, as a way to walk list structures without a stack. Jeffrey Eppinger showed by experiment in 1983 that long random sequences of inserts and deletes make the average search path longer than in a random tree.

The analysis of random trees came next and is still active. The exact average number of comparisons in a tree built from random insertions follows from the link with quicksort, and Knuth gives it in Volume 3. The height is harder: Luc Devroye found in 1986 that the expected height is about $4.311 \ln n$, and Bruce Reed showed in 2003 that its variance is bounded. On the side of known frequencies, Knuth gave the dynamic program for the optimal tree in 1971, together with the monotone roots that make it run in $O(n^2)$ time, and Kurt Mehlhorn showed in 1975 how to build nearly optimal trees faster.

Daniel Sleator and Robert Tarjan introduced the splay tree in 1985. Their paper proved the Access Lemma and, from it, the balance, static optimality, static finger and working set theorems, and it stated the dynamic optimality conjecture. Tarjan proved the sequential access theorem the same year, and Richard Cole, with Bud Mishra, Jeanette Schmidt and Alan Siegel, proved the dynamic finger theorem in two papers published in 2000. Robert Wilber gave two lower bounds on the cost of any binary search tree algorithm in 1989. Erik Demaine, Dion Harmon, John Iacono and Mihai Pătraşcu built the tango tree on the first of them in 2004 and showed that it is $O(\log \log n)$-competitive. The conjecture itself is still open.

Two other lines of work began in the 1970s. Leo Guibas, Edward McCreight, Michael Plass and Janet Roberts introduced finger search in 1977, and Scott Huddleston and Kurt Mehlhorn gave the level-linked (2,4)-tree with $O(1)$ amortized updates in 1982. Peter van Emde Boas showed in 1977 that integer keys allow predecessor search in $O(\log w)$ time, and Michael Fredman and Dan Willard presented the fusion tree in 1990, the first structure to beat comparison search for every word size. William Pugh introduced the skip list in 1990, as a simpler, randomized alternative to balanced trees.

## Further reading

- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 3: Sorting and Searching*, second edition (1998). Section 6.2.2 covers tree search, deletion and random trees, and the optimal trees, with the history.
- Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest and Clifford Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, fourth edition (2022). Chapter 12 gives binary search trees with careful proofs, Section 14.5 the optimal tree, and Chapter 17 augmentation.
- Robert Sedgewick and Kevin Wayne, *Algorithms*, fourth edition (2011). Chapter 3 builds ordered symbol tables, from arrays to search trees, as code with experiments.
- Daniel D. Sleator and Robert E. Tarjan, *Self-adjusting binary search trees*, Journal of the ACM 32(3), 1985. The splay tree paper; still the best place to learn the amortized analysis.
- William Pugh, *Skip lists: a probabilistic alternative to balanced trees*, Communications of the ACM 33(6), 1990. Short and readable, with the analysis and timing tests.
