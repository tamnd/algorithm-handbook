The structures of the earlier chapters assume that every cell of memory costs the same to reach. That stops being true when the data are larger than main memory. A disk, or an SSD, returns data in blocks of many items, and one block transfer takes far longer than thousands of steps on items already in memory. The [external memory model](/external-memory-model/) of Volume 1 keeps only what matters here: a memory of $M$ items, a disk read and written in blocks of $B$ items, and a cost equal to the number of block transfers, the I/Os. Computing inside memory is free. A structure for this model is judged by how many items each transfer serves.

The answer changes the shape of the structures. A [binary search tree](/binary-search-tree/) on disk reads one block per level and uses one key of it, so a search costs about $\lg N$ I/Os. A [B-tree](/b-tree/) puts a whole block of keys in each node, so the fan-out is about $B$ and a search costs $O(\log_B N)$ I/Os: three or four reads for a billion keys. That is the lower bound for searching by comparisons, and it is why B-trees and their variants are the indexes of file systems and databases. For other tasks the target is lower than one I/O per operation. A stack, a queue or a scan can serve $B$ operations per transfer, for $O(1/B)$ each. Sorting $N$ items costs $\mathrm{Sort}(N) = \Theta\big(\frac{N}{B}\log_{M/B}\frac{N}{B}\big)$ I/Os, which is $O(\frac{1}{B}\log_{M/B}\frac{N}{B})$ per item, far below one I/O. A structure whose operations reach that cost can sort, and the batched and write-optimized structures of this chapter do.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in six groups, read in order.

1. **Stacks and queues on disk.** The [external stack](/external-stack/) and the [external queue](/external-queue/) keep the blocks at their ends in memory, and cost $O(1/B)$ I/Os per operation, amortized.
2. **Searching on disk.** The problem of [external searching](/external-searching/), its lower bound of $\log_{B+1}(N+1)$ reads, and the trees that meet it: the [B-tree](/b-tree/), the [B+ tree](/b-plus-tree/) with all keys in linked leaves, and the [B*-tree](/b-star-tree/), whose nodes stay two-thirds full.
3. **Permuting.** The [permuting problem](/permuting-problem/) and the [permuting lower bound](/permuting-lower-bound/): moving records to known places costs $\Theta(\min\{N, \mathrm{Sort}(N)\})$ I/Os.
4. **Batched structures.** The [buffer tree](/buffer-tree/), which delays operations in large buffers, the [external memory priority queue](/external-memory-priority-queue/) built on it, and [time-forward processing](/time-forward-processing/), which uses that queue to evaluate a DAG on disk.
5. **Write-optimized dictionaries.** The idea of a [write-optimized structure](/write-optimized-structure/), the [B^ε-tree](/b-epsilon-tree/) and the [log-structured merge tree](/log-structured-merge-tree/), which make inserts much cheaper than in a B-tree and keep searches close to its cost.
6. **Cache-oblivious structures.** The [van Emde Boas layout](/van-emde-boas-layout/), the [packed memory array](/packed-memory-array/) and the [cache-oblivious B-tree](/cache-oblivious-b-tree/) built from them, which reach the B-tree bounds without knowing $B$.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take the external stack and queue, then [external searching](/external-searching/), the [B-tree](/b-tree/) and the [B+ tree](/b-plus-tree/). These are marked *Intro* or *Core*, and they are what most programs that touch a disk use. Work the B-tree example by hand: the split of a full node, and the rule that the height grows only at the root, come back in every tree of the chapter. The B*-tree can wait. The rest of the chapter is marked *Advanced* or *Research*, and it is best read after a first course in amortized analysis and external sorting. Of it, the [LSM tree](/log-structured-merge-tree/) is the one most used in practice, and the [van Emde Boas layout](/van-emde-boas-layout/) is the shortest way into the cache-oblivious ideas.
