## History

The B-tree came first. Rudolf Bayer and Edward McCreight designed it at the Boeing Scientific Research Laboratories and published it in *Acta Informatica* in 1972, under the title *Organization and maintenance of large ordered indexes*. Their analysis already counted disk accesses, not comparisons. Within a few years the B-tree and its variants had become the usual index of databases and file systems, and Douglas Comer's survey *The ubiquitous B-tree* (1979) records that. Comer describes the B+ tree, with IBM's VSAM access method as an example, and notes that the authors never explained the letter B. Knuth gave the name B*-tree, in the multiway-tree section of the third volume of *The Art of Computer Programming*, to the variant that shares keys with a sibling and splits two full nodes into three.

The theory came later. Robert Floyd studied permuting in a two-level storage in 1972. In 1988 Alok Aggarwal and Jeffrey Vitter defined the external memory model with general $M$ and $B$ in *The input/output complexity of sorting and related problems*, in the *Communications of the ACM*, and proved matching bounds for sorting, permuting, the FFT and matrix transposition. Their counting argument for permuting is the core of the sorting lower bound. In 1995 Yi-Jen Chiang, Michael Goodrich, Edward Grove, Roberto Tamassia, Darren Vengroff and Vitter used the permuting bound for lower bounds on graph problems and introduced time-forward processing. In the same year Lars Arge presented the buffer tree at WADS, with the journal version in *Algorithmica* in 2003. It turned internal-memory algorithms that use search trees and priority queues into batched algorithms with the sorting bound, and its priority queue made time-forward processing work for all parameters. Stacks and queues with buffered blocks need no single inventor; Rasmus Pagh's chapter in the 2003 volume *Algorithms for Memory Hierarchies* presents them with their $O(1/B)$ bounds.

Write optimization has two lines. Patrick O'Neil, Edward Cheng, Dieter Gawlick and Elizabeth O'Neil introduced the log-structured merge tree in *Acta Informatica* in 1996, for databases with heavy insert loads. Google's Bigtable (2006) used memtables and immutable sorted files, and LevelDB and RocksDB made the leveled LSM tree the common engine of key-value stores. In 2003 Gerth Stølting Brodal and Rolf Fagerberg proved the optimal trade-off between update and query costs for external dictionaries, with the B^ε-tree as the matching upper bound. Bender, Farach-Colton and Kuszmaul built it into the fractal tree index of TokuDB, and BetrFS (2015) used it in a file system.

The cache-oblivious line starts with Harald Prokop's master's thesis at MIT in 1999, written with the work of Frigo, Leiserson and Ramachandran that defined cache-oblivious algorithms. It described the van Emde Boas layout. At FOCS 2000, Michael Bender, Erik Demaine and Martin Farach-Colton combined that layout with a packed memory structure into the cache-oblivious B-tree, and the journal version appeared in the *SIAM Journal on Computing* in 2005. The packed array itself is older: Alon Itai, Alan Konheim and Michael Rodeh introduced sparse tables with density thresholds and the $O(\log^2 N)$ bound at ICALP 1981. Bulánek, Koucký and Saks proved at STOC 2012 that deterministic algorithms with linear space cannot do better.

## Further reading

- Jeffrey Scott Vitter, *Algorithms and Data Structures for External Memory*, Foundations and Trends in Theoretical Computer Science 2(4) (2008). The standard survey: the model, the bounds for sorting, permuting and searching, and the multiway, buffered and batched structures.
- Ulrich Meyer, Peter Sanders and Jop Sibeyn (editors), *Algorithms for Memory Hierarchies*, Lecture Notes in Computer Science 2625 (2003). Tutorial chapters on external and cache-oblivious structures, including the stack and the queue.
- Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest and Clifford Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, fourth edition (2022). Chapter 18 gives the B-tree with top-down insertion and deletion, as in this chapter.
- Douglas Comer, *The ubiquitous B-tree*, ACM Computing Surveys 11(2) (1979). A short and readable account of the B-tree, the B+ tree and the B*-tree.
- Chen Luo and Michael J. Carey, *LSM-based storage techniques: a survey*, The VLDB Journal 29(1) (2020). The many designs that grew out of the LSM tree.
