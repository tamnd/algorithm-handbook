## History

The correctness conditions came first. Leslie Lamport defined sequential consistency in 1979, for the memory of multiprocessors. Maurice Herlihy and Jeannette Wing defined linearizability in 1990, with the FIFO queue as their main example, and Herlihy showed in 1991 that a queue has consensus number 2, so that no wait-free queue can be built from reads and writes alone. Phillip Gibbons and Ephraim Korach later proved that testing whether a history of reads and writes is sequentially consistent is NP-complete. Quiescent consistency was named by Herlihy and Nir Shavit for the counters built from counting networks and diffracting trees.

Hot spots on shared counters were first attacked in hardware: the NYU Ultracomputer of Gottlieb and others combined fetch-and-add requests in its network in 1983. Yew, Tzeng and Lawrie moved combining into software trees in 1987, and Goodman, Vernon and Woest studied it for cache-coherent machines in 1989. James Aspnes, Herlihy and Shavit introduced counting networks in 1991, with the journal version in 1994. Shavit and Asaph Zemach added prisms to trees of balancers in the diffracting trees of 1996, and presented combining funnels at PODC 1998, with the journal version in 2000.

For queues and tables, Maged Michael and Michael Scott gave the two-lock queue and their lock-free queue at PODC 1996; Java's LinkedBlockingQueue and ConcurrentLinkedQueue follow them. In the same year Hunt, Michael, Parthasarathy and Scott gave the heap with a lock per slot and bit-reversed bottom slots. Doug Lea's ConcurrentHashMap in Java 5 used one lock per segment, and Java 8 moved to locks on single bins. Michael gave a lock-free hash table with a fixed number of buckets in 2002, and Ori Shalev and Shavit made it growable with split-ordered lists at PODC 2003, with the journal version in 2006. The B-link tree is older: Philip Lehman and S. Bing Yao added the high key and the right link to the B-tree in 1981, Yehoshua Sagiv reduced the locking further in 1986, and PostgreSQL builds its B-tree indexes on the design.

Later designs were shaped by real hardware. Boyd-Wickizer and others named sloppy counters in their 2010 study of Linux on 48 cores. The Disruptor was built at the LMAX exchange and described by Thompson, Farley, Barker, Gee and Stewart in May 2011. Prokopec, Bagwell and Odersky made Bagwell's hash array mapped trie lock-free as the Ctrie in 2011, and in 2012, with Bronson, added constant-time snapshots; the Ctrie became the TrieMap of Scala. Adam Morrison and Yehuda Afek replaced compare-and-swap loops by fetch-and-add in the LCRQ in 2013. Henzinger, Kirsch, Payer, Sezgin and Sokolova gave a theory of quantitative relaxation at POPL 2013, and in 2015 came the SprayList of Alistarh, Kopinsky, Li and Shavit and the MultiQueue of Rihani, Sanders and Dementiev; Alistarh, Kopinsky, Li and Nadiradze proved the rank bounds of the MultiQueue in 2017.

## Further reading

- Maurice Herlihy and Nir Shavit, *The Art of Multiprocessor Programming*, revised reprint. The standard textbook: correctness conditions, locks, queues, hash sets, counting networks, diffracting trees and priority queues.
- Maged M. Michael and Michael L. Scott, "Simple, fast, and practical non-blocking and blocking concurrent queue algorithms", PODC 1996. Short and clear, with the two-lock queue and the lock-free queue side by side.
- Philip L. Lehman and S. Bing Yao, "Efficient locking for concurrent operations on B-trees", ACM Transactions on Database Systems 6(4), 1981. The B-link tree and its proof.
- Sarita V. Adve and Kourosh Gharachorloo, "Shared memory consistency models: a tutorial", IEEE Computer 29(12), 1996. Sequential consistency and the weaker models of real processors.
- Remzi H. Arpaci-Dusseau and Andrea C. Arpaci-Dusseau, *Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces*, Chapter 29. Lock-based concurrent structures, from counters to hash tables, with measurements.
