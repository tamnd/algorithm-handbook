A sequential data structure is used by one thread at a time. On a machine with many cores, many threads use the same queue, table or counter at once, and their calls overlap in time. The structures of this chapter are built for this case. Each must settle two things: which results are correct when calls overlap, and how many calls can make progress at the same time.

The usual answer to the first question is linearizability: each call must act as if it took effect at one instant between its invocation and its response. A single lock around a sequential structure gives this at once, but then only one call runs at a time, and the lock itself becomes the point where all threads wait. The rest of the chapter is about doing better. Some structures split the data so that different calls take different locks. Some merge many calls into one step, or spread them over many places. Some use atomic instructions such as compare-and-swap and fetch-and-add in place of locks. And some change the specification: a counter that is correct only when no call is in progress, or a priority queue that may return an item near the minimum instead of the minimum.

Most entries come with a step-by-step schedule of two or three threads, a proof of correctness, and an account of where the design stops scaling. The notions of concurrency themselves, such as linearizability, mutual exclusion, lock-freedom and the atomic instructions, belong to Volume 22; this chapter uses them.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in six groups, read in order.

1. **Shared queues.** The [concurrent queue](/concurrent-queue/) with one lock and its proof, the [two-lock queue](/two-lock-queue/) that lets one enqueue and one dequeue run together, the [Disruptor](/disruptor-ring-buffer/) ring buffer with its sequence numbers and batches, and the [LCRQ](/lcrq/), a lock-free queue of rings driven by fetch-and-add.
2. **One lock per part.** [Lock striping](/lock-striping/) with its three rules, the striped [concurrent hash table](/concurrent-hash-table/) with a resize that takes all locks, and the [sloppy counter](/sloppy-counter/), which keeps one count per core.
3. **Weaker correctness conditions.** [Sequential consistency](/sequential-consistency/), which keeps only the order inside each thread, and [quiescent consistency](/quiescent-consistency/), which keeps real-time order only across moments of quiet.
4. **Counters that combine or spread.** The [combining tree](/combining-tree/) and the [combining funnel](/combining-funnel/) merge increments into batches; the [diffracting tree](/diffracting-tree/) sends tokens through balancers and prisms to many counters.
5. **Priority queues, exact and relaxed.** The [fine-grained concurrent heap](/fine-grained-concurrent-heap/) with a lock per slot, the idea of a [relaxed priority queue](/relaxed-priority-queue/), and two designs of it, the [MultiQueue](/multiqueue/) and the [SprayList](/spraylist/).
6. **Concurrent search structures.** The [B-link tree](/blink-tree/), whose searches take no locks, and the [Ctrie](/ctrie/), a lock-free hash trie with snapshots.
