Most structures in this volume are [ephemeral](/ephemeral-data-structure/): an update changes them in place, and the state before the update is gone. A [persistent data structure](/persistent-data-structure/) keeps every version. After a thousand insertions into a persistent set, a program can still search the set as it was after the 17th. This is what an editor needs for undo, what a version control system needs for history, what a backtracking search needs to return to an earlier state, and what a database needs to answer "as of last Tuesday". It is also the normal case in functional languages, where no value is ever changed, so that every data structure there is persistent whether the programmer asks for it or not.

Keeping a full copy of each version would solve the problem at a cost of $\Theta(n)$ per update. The chapter is about doing much better. The main idea is [structural sharing](/structural-sharing/): a new version is built from a few new nodes that point into the old version, so the two share everything the update did not touch. With [path copying](/path-copying/) in a balanced tree, an update makes $O(\log n)$ new nodes, and a search runs on any version as fast as on an ordinary tree.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups, read in order.

1. **Versions and degrees of persistence.** [Partial persistence](/partial-persistence/) lets every version be read and only the newest be updated; [full persistence](/full-persistence/) lets any version be updated; [confluent persistence](/confluent-persistence/) also lets two versions be combined. The [version tree](/version-tree/) records which version came from which.
2. **Immutable lists and the functional style.** The [persistent stack](/persistent-stack/), with $\Theta(1)$ push and pop, the idea of a [purely functional data structure](/purely-functional-data-structure/), and the [zipper](/zipper/).
3. **Path copying and persistent trees.** Path copying, and its use in the [persistent binary search tree](/persistent-binary-search-tree/), the [persistent red-black tree](/persistent-red-black-tree/), the persistent treap and trie, and the [persistent segment tree](/persistent-segment-tree/).
4. **General methods for linked structures.** The [fat node](/fat-node/) method, [node copying](/node-copying/), and the [theorem of Driscoll, Sarnak, Sleator and Tarjan](/dsst-persistence-theorem/): a structure of bounded in-degree becomes persistent with $O(1)$ amortized time and space per update step.
5. **Persistent arrays.** The [fully persistent array](/fully-persistent-array/), the 32-way [persistent vector](/persistent-vector/) of Clojure, [Baker's trick](/bakers-trick/) and its use in [persistent union-find](/persistent-union-find/).
6. **Asking about the past, and changing it.** [Historical queries](/historical-queries/), and [retroactive data structures](/retroactive-data-structure/), which change the past and recompute the present.
7. **Laziness and amortization under persistence.** The [banker's queue](/banker-queue/), [debit analysis](/debit-analysis/), lazy rebuilding and the [real-time queue](/real-time-queue/), whose operations take $O(1)$ time in the worst case on every version.
8. **Sequences built from number systems.** The [binary](/binary-random-access-list/) and [skew binary](/skew-binary-random-access-list/) random-access lists, [bootstrapping](/bootstrapping/), the [catenable deque](/catenable-deque/) and the [finger tree](/finger-tree/).

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these eleven entries in order: [ephemeral data structure](/ephemeral-data-structure/), [persistent data structure](/persistent-data-structure/), [partial persistence](/partial-persistence/), [full persistence](/full-persistence/), [structural sharing](/structural-sharing/), [persistent stack](/persistent-stack/), [purely functional data structure](/purely-functional-data-structure/), [path copying](/path-copying/), [persistent binary search tree](/persistent-binary-search-tree/), [persistent segment tree](/persistent-segment-tree/) and [banker's queue](/banker-queue/). They are marked *Intro* or *Core*, except the segment tree, which is marked *Advanced* but shows path copying at work on a structure you already know. The general methods of group 4 are the theory behind the chapter, and they can wait until the trees of group 3 are familiar. Groups 7 and 8 come from functional programming and assume that you are at ease with lazy evaluation; after the banker's queue, read the [real-time queue](/real-time-queue/) and then the random-access lists.
