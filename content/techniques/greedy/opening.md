A cashier who owes 68 cents in euro coins hands over 50, 10, 5, 2 and 1, always the largest coin that still fits, and no shorter list of coins exists. A lecture hall that takes the request that ends first, then the next request that starts after it, and so on, holds as many talks as any timetable could. Both are greedy algorithms: each step makes the choice that looks best now and never takes it back. With coins of 1, 3 and 4 cents the same rule pays 6 as $4 + 1 + 1$ and misses $3 + 3$. The chapter is about telling these cases apart and proving the right ones right.

A greedy algorithm is a dynamic program that needs to look at only one choice. That is why Volume 2 puts it after [optimal substructure](/optimal-substructure/): the proof that greedy works is a proof that the one choice it makes is part of some optimal solution, after which the rest is a smaller instance of the same problem. The chapter gives two patterns for such proofs, applies them to intervals, merges, coins and scheduling, and ends with the matroid, the structure on which the greedy rule is always right.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in six groups, read in order.

1. **The method.** The [greedy algorithm](/greedy-algorithm/) entry gives the template (candidates, a key to sort them by, a test that a candidate still fits) and a table of classic rules with their keys and their proofs. Its list of rules that fail matters as much. The [greedy-choice property](/greedy-choice-property/) is the condition every correct rule meets, shown with three non-examples: the 0-1 knapsack, the coins 1, 3, 4, and the shortest interval first.
2. **Proving a greedy algorithm.** An [exchange argument](/exchange-argument/) takes an optimal solution that differs from the greedy one and swaps two pieces without making it worse; for Smith's rule the change in cost of one swap is exactly $w_a p_b - w_b p_a$. [Greedy stays ahead](/greedy-stays-ahead/) proves by induction that after $k$ steps the greedy solution is at least as far along as any other.
3. **Intervals.** [Activity selection](/activity-selection/) asks for the most pairwise disjoint intervals, and [earliest finish time first](/earliest-finish-time-first/) solves it after one sort. [Interval partitioning](/interval-partitioning/) assigns every interval to a resource, and needs exactly as many resources as the depth, the largest number of intervals that share a point.
4. **Divisible items, merges and coins.** The [fractional knapsack](/fractional-knapsack/) is solved by value per unit of weight, the rule that fails for whole items. The [optimal merge pattern](/optimal-merge-pattern/) merges the two smallest files first, which is Huffman's algorithm. A [canonical coin system](/canonical-coin-system/) is one where paying largest coin first is always optimal; the entry gives tests for it and the bound on the smallest counterexample.
5. **Scheduling one machine.** [Optimal tape storage](/optimal-tape-storage/) orders files by length over frequency of use. This is the same problem as [minimizing weighted completion time](/minimizing-weighted-completion-time/), solved by [Smith's rule](/smith-rule/). [Minimizing maximum lateness](/minimizing-maximum-lateness/) is solved by earliest due date first, and [minimizing the number of late jobs](/minimizing-number-of-late-jobs/) by the Moore-Hodgson algorithm, which runs the same order and drops the longest job whenever one is late.
6. **Matroids.** A [matroid](/matroid/) is a family of independent sets closed under subsets in which a smaller independent set can always grow from a larger one. The [uniform](/uniform-matroid/), [partition](/partition-matroid/) and [linear](/linear-matroid/) matroids are the main examples, with the [graphic matroid](/graphic-matroid/) of forests from Volume 7. The [maximum-weight basis](/maximum-weight-matroid-basis/) problem is solved by the [matroid greedy algorithm](/matroid-greedy-algorithm/), and the [matroid greedy theorem](/matroid-greedy-theorem/) proves that matroids are the only systems where greedy is right for every weighting. [Job sequencing with deadlines](/job-sequencing-with-deadlines/) is a matroid in disguise.

Some entries treat one subject from several sides: activity selection and earliest finish time first; tape storage, weighted completion time and Smith's rule; and the maximum-weight basis, the matroid greedy algorithm and the matroid greedy theorem, which state one result as a problem, an algorithm and a theorem.

## A first reading

Read the [greedy algorithm](/greedy-algorithm/), then [activity selection](/activity-selection/) and [earliest finish time first](/earliest-finish-time-first/), and work the example of eight activities by hand. Read the [exchange argument](/exchange-argument/) and [greedy stays ahead](/greedy-stays-ahead/) next, and check each against the activity-selection proof. Then read the [fractional knapsack](/fractional-knapsack/), the [optimal merge pattern](/optimal-merge-pattern/), [interval partitioning](/interval-partitioning/), [Smith's rule](/smith-rule/) and [minimizing maximum lateness](/minimizing-maximum-lateness/). That is ten entries, enough for a first course.

The matroid group is a unit of its own. Read [matroid](/matroid/), the [matroid greedy algorithm](/matroid-greedy-algorithm/) and the [matroid greedy theorem](/matroid-greedy-theorem/) together, with [Kruskal's algorithm](/kruskal/) open beside them, since a minimum spanning tree is the first example of the theorem.
