## History

Reductions entered complexity theory in two forms at almost the same time. Cook, in his STOC paper of 1971, defined reducibility with a machine that may ask an oracle many questions, which is the Turing reduction of this chapter. Karp, in "Reducibility among combinatorial problems" (1972), used the stricter form with one question, the many-one reduction, and with it proved a long list of combinatorial problems NP-complete. Ladner, Lynch and Selman compared these and other polynomial-time reducibilities in 1975, and in the same year Ladner proved that if P differs from NP, then NP has problems that are neither in P nor NP-complete.

Many of the reductions used for algorithms come from the theory of flows. Menger proved his theorem on disjoint paths in 1927, in a paper on the theory of curves, and the max-flow min-cut theorem was published in 1956 by Ford and Fulkerson and, independently, by Elias, Feinstein and Shannon. Read through vertex splitting, Menger's theorem is the max-flow min-cut theorem with unit vertex capacities. Kőnig and Egerváry both published their theorems on bipartite matching in 1931. Aspvall, Plass and Tarjan reduced 2-SAT to strongly connected components in 1979, which gave a linear-time algorithm.

Scaling begins with Edmonds and Karp (1972), who scaled the numbers of a minimum-cost flow problem and ran successive shortest paths in each phase, to get a time polynomial in the bit length. Gabow (1985) made bit scaling a general method for network problems, shortest paths among them. On the side of linear programming, Karmarkar (1984) gave an interior-point method with $O(n^{3.5} L)$ arithmetic operations. Schrijver's book of 1986 collects the theory of linear and integer programs, including the conditions under which the optimum of a program is integral.

Canonical forms have a scattered history. Tseitin's encoding of formulas in clauses dates from 1968 and appeared in English in 1983. Aho, Hopcroft and Ullman gave the linear-time test for rooted tree isomorphism in their book of 1974. Booth found the least rotation of a string in linear time in 1980. Bryant (1986) proved that a reduced ordered binary decision diagram is canonical for a fixed order of the variables. McKay's program nauty for canonical labelling of graphs dates from his 1981 paper "Practical graph isomorphism", and McKay and Piperno described its successor Traces in 2014. For the bipartite double cover, Weichsel (1962) proved the connectivity theorem for direct products of graphs, and Brualdi, Harary and Miller (1980) used the same construction for directed graphs.

The plainest transform, sorting first, is too old to date. Graham's convex hull algorithm of 1972 is an early named case: sort the points by angle, then scan. Levitin's textbook uses the name transform-and-conquer and sorts its examples into the three kinds used in the opening of this chapter.

## Further reading

- Anany Levitin, *Introduction to the Design and Analysis of Algorithms*, third edition (2012). Chapter 6 is the source of the three kinds of transform, with presorting, Gaussian elimination, heaps, Horner's rule and problem reduction as examples.
- Jon Kleinberg and Éva Tardos, *Algorithm Design* (2006). The chapter on network flow reduces matching, disjoint paths and many other problems to maximum flow, and the chapter on NP reduces in the other direction.
- Ravindra K. Ahuja, Thomas L. Magnanti and James B. Orlin, *Network Flows: Theory, Algorithms, and Applications* (1993). Section 2.4 on network transformations covers node splitting, and later chapters treat scaling algorithms for flows and shortest paths.
- Richard M. Karp, "Reducibility among combinatorial problems" (1972). The paper that made many-one reductions the tool of NP-completeness.
- Alexander Schrijver, *Theory of Linear and Integer Programming* (1986). Chapter 19 proves that totally unimodular matrices, such as the incidence matrices of bipartite and directed graphs, give integral optima.
