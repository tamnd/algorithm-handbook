To test whether `lohel` is a rotation of `hello`, write `hello` twice and look for `lohel` in `hellohello`. It starts at position 3, so the answer is yes. The question was about rotations, and nothing on the shelf answers it directly. The doubled string turns it into a question about substrings, and [string matching](/string-matching/) answers that in linear time. The work is in the change of question, not in the search.

This chapter collects that move in its many forms. Levitin names three kinds. *Instance simplification* keeps the problem and makes the input nicer, as sorting does before a search for duplicates. *Representation change* keeps the input but stores it another way, as a [heap](/heap/) or the nested form of [Horner's rule](/horner-rule/) does. *Problem reduction* sends the instance to a different problem that already has an algorithm, as the rotation test does. In every case the cost is the cost of the change, plus the cost of the new problem, plus the cost of reading the answer back, and the change pays only when that sum beats a direct attack.

A reduction can be read in two directions. Forward, an algorithm for the target problem becomes an algorithm for the source. Backward, a lower bound or a hardness result for the source becomes one for the target. Mixing up the two directions is the most common mistake with reductions, and several exercises at the end of the chapter test it.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in five groups, read in order.

1. **Transform, then solve.** [Transform-and-conquer](/transform-and-conquer/) gives the template and sorts its examples by Levitin's three kinds. [Reduction](/reduction/) defines the move from a problem $A$ to a problem $B$, separates one-call reductions from reductions with many calls, and proves the rotation test above. [Reductions for algorithms](/reductions-for-algorithms/) uses it forward: bipartite matching becomes maximum flow, 2-SAT becomes [strongly connected components](/strongly-connected-components/), and longest increasing subsequence becomes longest common subsequence, with the cost rule that adds the size of the new instance to the bound. [Polynomial-time equivalence](/polynomial-time-equivalence/) is the coarse view, in which independent set, vertex cover and clique are one problem and the search version of a problem is no harder than its yes-or-no version.
2. **Sort first.** [Presorting](/presorting/) sorts once so that a later scan sees equal items side by side; for $n = 10^6$ items, a duplicate test drops from about $5 \times 10^{11}$ comparisons to about $2 \times 10^7$. [Reduction to sorting](/reduction-to-sorting/) applies it to merging intervals, [activity selection](/activity-selection/) and the convex hull, where the work is in the choice of sort key. [Coordinate compression](/coordinate-compression/) sorts the values instead of the items and replaces each value by its rank, so that a [Fenwick tree](/fenwick-tree/) of size $n$ can index numbers as large as $10^{18}$.
3. **One form for each meaning.** A [normal form](/normal-form-representation/) gives every object a fixed shape, such as conjunctive normal form for a SAT solver. The entry shows a formula whose every equivalent CNF has $2^n$ clauses and a Tseitin encoding of it with $3n + 1$. [Canonicalization](/canonicalization/) asks for more: one form for each meaning, so that a test of equivalence is a test of equality. Fractions in lowest terms, the least rotation of a string by [Booth's algorithm](/booth-algorithm/) and the [AHU](/ahu-algorithm/) code of a rooted tree are canonical.
4. **Numbers, exact and rounded.** [Reduction to linear programming](/reduction-to-linear-programming/) writes a problem as a linear program, and shows why shortest paths and flows come out whole while vertex cover can come out as halves. The [scaling technique](/scaling-technique/) solves a problem on the top bits of its numbers first and refines one bit at a time. [Capacity scaling](/capacity-scaling/) and [Gabow's shortest path algorithm](/gabow-scaling-sssp/) are its two worked cases, and both replace a factor of the largest number $C$ in the running time by $\log C$.
5. **Change the graph.** [Vertex splitting](/vertex-splitting/) turns each vertex into an in copy and an out copy joined by one edge, so that a flow algorithm with edge capacities can handle vertex capacities and find vertex-disjoint paths. The [bipartite double cover](/bipartite-double-cover/) keeps two copies of each vertex and lets each edge switch copies, so that [breadth-first search](/breadth-first-search/) finds shortest odd walks, tests bipartiteness and finds a shortest odd cycle.

## A first reading

Read [transform-and-conquer](/transform-and-conquer/) and [reduction](/reduction/) first, then [presorting](/presorting/) and [coordinate compression](/coordinate-compression/), which are short and used all the time. Then read [reductions for algorithms](/reductions-for-algorithms/) for its three worked reductions and [vertex splitting](/vertex-splitting/) for the one most often met in flow problems. That is six entries and enough for a first course. [Canonicalization](/canonicalization/) and the [bipartite double cover](/bipartite-double-cover/) come next. [Polynomial-time equivalence](/polynomial-time-equivalence/), [reduction to linear programming](/reduction-to-linear-programming/) and the [scaling technique](/scaling-technique/) can wait until you have met NP-completeness, linear programs and maximum flow.
