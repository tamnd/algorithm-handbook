An array of $n$ cells holds the letter $a$ in half of its cells and $b$ in the other half, and the task is to find an $a$. A program that probes the cells in a fixed order can be beaten. An opponent who knows the order puts the $b$s in the first $n/2$ places, and the program needs $n/2 + 1$ probes. Now let the program probe a random cell each time. Every probe finds an $a$ with probability $1/2$, whatever the input, so the expected number of probes is 2. The chance of more than 20 probes is $2^{-20}$, below one in a million. The opponent still picks the input, but cannot see the coins.

This is the point of randomization. The guarantee is a worst case over the inputs of an average over the program's own coin flips. No assumption is made about where the inputs come from, and no input is bad on average. Randomness is used in three ways in this chapter: to pick a pivot or a sample, to choose the order in which objects are processed, and to prove that an object exists without building it.

Two kinds of algorithms result. A Las Vegas algorithm is always right, and only its running time is random; [randomized quicksort](/randomized-quicksort/) is one. A Monte Carlo algorithm stops within a fixed bound and is wrong with a small probability; the [Miller-Rabin test](/miller-rabin/) and [Karger's minimum cut](/karger-min-cut/) are of this kind. Repetition makes the error as small as you like, and [Markov's inequality](/markov-inequality/) turns a Las Vegas bound into a Monte Carlo one.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in three groups, read in order.

1. **Coins inside the algorithm.** [Randomized algorithm](/randomized-algorithm/) gives the definitions: Las Vegas and Monte Carlo, one-sided and two-sided error, and the two facts that make the constants in the error bounds unimportant. [Expected running time](/expected-running-time/) defines the cost measure, separates it from the average over random inputs, and shows what an expectation does not tell you. [Randomized quickselect](/randomized-quickselect/) finds the item of rank $k$ with a random pivot and a three-way partition, in at most $4n$ comparisons in expectation on every input.
2. **Random order.** [Backwards analysis](/backwards-analysis/) bounds the cost of step $i$ of an insertion in random order by asking which of the first $i$ objects came last. [Randomized incremental construction](/randomized-incremental-construction/) is the design method behind it, with conflict lists or a history structure, and it gives expected $O(n \log n)$ algorithms for [trapezoidal maps](/trapezoidal-map/), convex hulls and Delaunay triangulations.
3. **Randomness as a proof.** The [probabilistic method](/probabilistic-method/) shows that an object exists by showing that a random one has the property with positive probability. Its tools are the [union bound](/union-bound/) and [linearity of expectation](/linearity-of-expectation/), and its classic results are Erdős's lower bound on Ramsey numbers and the cut with at least half of the edges.

## A first reading

Read [randomized algorithm](/randomized-algorithm/) and [expected running time](/expected-running-time/) together; they are short and share their examples. Then trace [randomized quickselect](/randomized-quickselect/) by hand on a small array and follow the proof of its $4n$ bound. This first group is enough for most uses of randomness in later volumes.

The other three entries are advanced. Start [backwards analysis](/backwards-analysis/) with its example of prefix minima, where the whole argument fits in one line, and only then read the geometric examples. [Randomized incremental construction](/randomized-incremental-construction/) reads best after a first look at the [trapezoidal map](/trapezoidal-map/) or the [convex hull](/convex-hull/). The [probabilistic method](/probabilistic-method/) can be read at any time after the first group.
