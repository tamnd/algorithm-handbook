Dynamic programming solves a problem by writing its optimum as a recurrence over smaller instances of the same problem and computing each smaller instance once. A rod of length $n$ is worth most when its first piece is chosen well and the rest is cut in the best way for its length, so the best values for lengths $0, 1, \ldots, n$ fill a table of $n + 1$ entries in $\Theta(n^2)$ time, where the plain recursion makes $2^n$ calls. The fewest coins that make an amount, the cheapest order in which to multiply a chain of matrices, and the shortest tour through 20 cities follow the same pattern with other tables: a list of amounts, a triangle of intervals, a table indexed by sets of cities.

The method differs from [divide and conquer](/divide-and-conquer-paradigm/) in one point. There the parts are disjoint and each is solved once anyway; here the parts share subproblems, and the work is in naming the subproblems so that there are few of them. Once the subproblems and the recurrence are right, the rest is routine: fill a table in a safe order, or let a recursion store its answers, then walk back through the choices to recover the solution. The chapter is the largest of the volume because the routine part is short and the design part has many patterns, each with its own kind of state.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups, read in order.

1. **When dynamic programming works.** [Optimal substructure](/optimal-substructure/) and [overlapping subproblems](/overlapping-subproblems/) are the two conditions. The [principle of optimality](/principle-of-optimality/) is Bellman's statement of the first, and [independent subproblems](/independent-subproblems/) explains why the longest simple path has no such recurrence.
2. **Filling the table.** [Memoization](/memoization/) works top down and [tabulation](/tabulation/) bottom up; both visit the [subproblem graph](/subproblem-graph/). [Traceback](/traceback/) recovers a solution, and [rolling arrays](/rolling-array-space-reduction/) cut the space to one or two rows.
3. **First problems.** [Rod cutting](/rod-cutting-problem/), [coin change](/coin-change-problem/), the [unbounded](/unbounded-knapsack/) and [bounded](/bounded-knapsack/) knapsack, [grid paths with obstacles](/grid-paths-with-obstacles/), [text segmentation](/text-segmentation/) and [egg dropping](/egg-dropping-problem/).
4. **Paths in a DAG.** [Dynamic programming as a DAG](/dag-dynamic-programming/) and [DP on a DAG](/dp-on-dag/) present the same idea from two sides; then [assembly lines](/assembly-line-scheduling/), [inventory planning](/inventory-planning/), [segmented least squares](/segmented-least-squares/), [printing neatly](/printing-neatly/) and the [bitonic tour](/bitonic-tsp/).
5. **Intervals.** [Parenthesization](/parenthesization/) counts the ways to split, [interval DP](/interval-dp/) gives the template, and [matrix chains](/matrix-chain-multiplication/), [polygon triangulation](/optimal-polygon-triangulation/) and [optimal binary search trees](/optimal-binary-search-tree/) apply it.
6. **Trees, subsets and digits.** [DP on trees](/tree-dynamic-programming/), [bitmask DP](/bitmask-dp/) with the [Held-Karp algorithm](/held-karp-algorithm/) and [sums over subsets](/sum-over-subsets-dp/), [broken profiles](/broken-profile-dp/) for grids, and [digit DP](/digit-dynamic-programming/) for counting numbers.
7. **Monge costs and line breaking.** The [Monge property](/monge-property/) behind [Knuth's optimization](/knuth-optimization/), the [divide and conquer optimization](/divide-and-conquer-optimization/) and the [SMAWK algorithm](/smawk-algorithm/) for [row minima](/row-minima-of-totally-monotone-matrices/); then [optimal text wrapping](/optimal-text-wrapping/) and the [Knuth-Plass algorithm](/knuth-plass-line-breaking/) of TeX.
8. **Other speed-ups.** The [convex hull trick](/convex-hull-trick/), the [slope trick](/slope-trick/) and the [Aliens trick](/aliens-trick/).

## A first reading

Read [optimal substructure](/optimal-substructure/) and [overlapping subproblems](/overlapping-subproblems/), then [memoization](/memoization/), [tabulation](/tabulation/) and [traceback](/traceback/). Work [rod cutting](/rod-cutting-problem/) and [coin change](/coin-change-problem/) by hand, then the [unbounded knapsack](/unbounded-knapsack/), where the loop direction separates the unbounded from the 0/1 case. Read [DP on a DAG](/dp-on-dag/) to see every table as a graph, then [interval DP](/interval-dp/) with [matrix chains](/matrix-chain-multiplication/), and [DP on trees](/tree-dynamic-programming/) and [bitmask DP](/bitmask-dp/). That is thirteen entries, enough for a first course.

The last two groups can wait. They need the first five, and their gains matter only when $n$ is in the thousands or more. The two DAG entries cover the same idea, so one of them is enough at first, and the same holds for [printing neatly](/printing-neatly/) and the two line-breaking entries of group 7.

## Before you start

You need [recursion](/recursion/) and proof by [induction](/induction/) from Volume 1, with the [cut-and-paste argument](/cut-and-paste-argument/) used there to prove optimal substructure, and enough [asymptotic notation](/asymptotic-notation/) to read a bound. The DAG view uses [topological sort](/topological-sort/) and [shortest paths in a DAG](/dag-shortest-paths/) from Volume 7, and the tree entries use [rooted trees](/rooted-tree/) and [postorder traversal](/postorder-traversal/). Memoization stores its answers in a [hash table](/hash-table/) when the keys are not small integers. The bitmask entries use the notation of [bit manipulation](/bit-manipulation/) and [submask enumeration](/submask-enumeration/) from Chapter 2.12, and the speed-ups use the [monotonic deque](/monotonic-deque/), the [priority queue](/priority-queue/) and [binary search](/binary-search/).
