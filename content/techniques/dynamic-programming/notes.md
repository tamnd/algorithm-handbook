## History

Richard Bellman developed dynamic programming in the early 1950s at the RAND Corporation. His survey *The theory of dynamic programming* appeared in the Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society in 1954, and his book *Dynamic Programming* in 1957; Chapter III of the book states the principle of optimality, which the chapter quotes. Bellman also gave some of the first uses that are now textbook examples: fitting a curve by line segments in 1961, the problem behind segmented least squares, and the subset recurrence for the travelling salesman problem in 1962. Michael Held and Richard Karp published the same recurrence two months later and on their own, with a count of the operations. Harvey Wagner and Thomson Whitin (1958) solved lot sizing by a recurrence over the months.

The tools for turning a recurrence into a program came next. Donald Michie coined *memo functions* in *Nature* in 1968, a function that remembers its earlier results. Daniel Hirschberg showed in 1975 that a longest common subsequence can be found in linear space, so that rolling the table does not lose the solution. For matrix chains, S. S. Godbole gave the $O(n^3)$ program in 1973, Francis Chin a linear-time approximation in 1978, and Hu and Shing an $O(n \log n)$ exact method in papers of 1982 and 1984, by way of triangulations of a convex polygon.

The speed-ups grew out of Donald Knuth's paper *Optimum binary search trees* in Acta Informatica (1971), which showed that the best roots are monotone and cut the time from $\Theta(n^3)$ to $\Theta(n^2)$. Frances Yao proved in 1980 that a quadrangle inequality on the cost gives the same speed-up for a whole class of interval programs, and extended the method in 1982. The inequality is far older: Gaspard Monge observed it in 1781 for the transport of earth, and Alan Hoffman returned to it in 1963 and credited it to Monge. In 1987 Alok Aggarwal, Maria Klawe, Shlomo Moran, Peter Shor and Robert Wilber gave the SMAWK algorithm for row minima of totally monotone matrices. In the same year Hirschberg and Lawrence Larmore defined the least weight subsequence problem, with line breaking as the main example; Wilber (1988) and Zvi Galil and Kunsoo Park (1990) brought it to linear time. Knuth and Michael Plass had described the line breaking of TeX in 1981. Aggarwal, Baruch Schieber and Takeshi Tokuyama (1994) used a penalty per edge for paths with exactly $k$ edges, the idea now called the Aliens trick.

The exponential methods have their own line. P. W. Kasteleyn counted the domino tilings of a rectangle in 1961, the problem that broken-profile programs count cell by cell. Andreas Björklund, Thore Husfeldt, Petteri Kaski and Mikko Koivisto gave fast subset convolution with the zeta and Möbius transforms in 2007, and Björklund, Husfeldt and Koivisto set partitioning by inclusion and exclusion in 2009. Among recent results, Günter Rote (2019) wrote up isotonic regression by dynamic programming, the problem behind the slope trick, and Mark de Berg, Kevin Buchin, Bart Jansen and Gerhard Woeginger (2021) beat the quadratic program for the bitonic tour.

## Further reading

- Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest and Clifford Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, fourth edition (2022). Chapter 14 covers rod cutting, matrix chains, the longest common subsequence and optimal binary search trees, with the two properties made explicit.
- Jon Kleinberg and Éva Tardos, *Algorithm Design* (2005). Chapter 6 builds each program from a careful definition of the subproblems, with weighted interval scheduling, segmented least squares and Hirschberg's linear-space alignment.
- Sanjoy Dasgupta, Christos Papadimitriou and Umesh Vazirani, *Algorithms* (2006). Chapter 6 presents every dynamic program as a path in an implicit DAG, the view of group 4.
- Jeff Erickson, *Algorithms* (2019). It derives each recurrence from a recursive backtracking search before adding the table.
- Richard Bellman, *Dynamic Programming* (1957). The source of the name, the principle of optimality and the functional equations.
- Rainer E. Burkard, Bettina Klinz and Rüdiger Rudolf, *Perspectives of Monge properties in optimization*, Discrete Applied Mathematics 70(2), 1996. A survey of the Monge property and of the fast algorithms that use it.
