On a match, count the run of equal values on each side, add the product of the two run lengths, and move both pointers past their runs. The matches are $0 + 9$, $3 + 6$ (twice), $4 + 5$ and $5 + 4$, so there are 5 subsets: $\{4, 5\}$, $\{3, 6\}$, $\{1, 2, 6\}$, $\{1, 3, 5\}$ and $\{2, 3, 4\}$.

The giant steps are $7$, then $7 \cdot 4 \equiv 11$, then $11 \cdot 4 \equiv 10$, which is in the table at $j = 3$ after $i = 2$ steps. So $x = 4 \cdot 2 + 3 = 11$, and indeed $3^{11} \equiv 7 \pmod{17}$.

11. Two disjoint subsets with equal sums give $c_i = 1$ on one, $-1$ on the other and 0 elsewhere, and a nonzero $c$ with sum zero gives back two such subsets. Each half has $3^{n/2}$ vectors; sort the values of one half and look up the negative of each value of the other. That is $O(n 3^{n/2})$ time, about $1.73^n$. Skip only the match of the all-zero left vector with the all-zero right vector; a zero-sum vector on one half paired with zero on the other is a real answer.

13. A position where $e$ is 1 must be split as $(1, 0)$ or $(0, 1)$, since $-1 + 2$ is not allowed. So both $-1$ entries sit where $e$ is 0, each opposite a 1 of the other vector. Choose which half of the support goes to $x$ in $\binom{w}{w/2}$ ways, the position of the $-1$ of $x$ in $n - w$ ways and that of $y$ in $n - w - 1$ ways. The count is $\binom{w}{w/2}(n - w)(n - w - 1)$; for $n = 40$, $w = 20$ it is $184{,}756 \cdot 20 \cdot 19 = 70{,}207{,}280$, which is 380 times the 0-1 count of $184{,}756$.

14. Store $E_{k_1}(P)$ for every $k_1$: $2^k$ calls and $2^k$ entries. For each $k_3$ compute $D_{k_3}(C)$ once, and for each $k_2$ decrypt again and look the result up. That is $2^{2k}$ calls in the inner loop, so about $2^{2k}$ in all. For DES this is about $2^{112}$ calls with $2^{56}$ entries.

15. Store with each half-sum the number of items it uses, and split each list into classes by that number. For each $c$ from 0 to $n/2$, scan class $c$ of the first list against class $w - c$ of the second with the two pointers. Each class takes part in one scan, so all scans together cost $O(2^{n/2})$ after the $O(n 2^{n/2})$ sort.

16. The first half gives $(0,0)$, $(2,3)$, $(3,4)$, $(4,5)$, $(5,7)$, $(6,8)$, $(7,9)$, $(9,12)$. The second gives $(0,0)$, $(1,1)$, $(5,8)$, $(6,7)$, $(6,9)$, $(7,8)$, $(11,15)$, $(12,16)$; the pairs $(6,7)$ and $(7,8)$ are dominated, by $(5,8)$ and by $(6,9)$. For each first-half pair, take the best second-half pair that fits in the remaining capacity. The best total is 15, from $(5,7) + (5,8)$: the items $(3,4)$, $(2,3)$ and $(5,8)$, of weight 10.

17. Sort $B$ and $D$. One heap holds, for each $a$, the next unused $b$, so it gives the sums $a + b$ in increasing order; the other gives the sums $c + d$ in decreasing order. Each pair enters and leaves its heap at most once, so there are at most $N^2$ pops on each side, and each scan step pops from one side. The heaps never hold more than $N$ entries, which gives $O(N^2 \log N)$ time and $O(N)$ memory.

18. Hash $B$ and $D$ by residue once. For a fixed $r$, each $a$ finds the $b$ with $b \equiv r - a$ among about $N/M$ candidates, so about $N^2/M$ pairs are built for each side, and the join costs the same. One residue costs $O(N + N^2/M)$ time and memory, and all $M$ residues cost $O(MN + N^2)$ time. A solution is found at $r = (a + b) \bmod M$. With $M$ about $N$ the time is $O(N^2)$ and the memory $O(N)$.
