## History

The idea of meeting in the middle appeared in several fields at about the same time. Ellis Horowitz and Sartaj Sahni published the split of subset sum into two halves in 1974, in the *Journal of the ACM*, in a paper on computing partitions with applications to the knapsack problem. Their paper also gave the extension to the 0/1 knapsack problem. In number theory, the [baby-step giant-step](/baby-step-giant-step/) method for discrete logarithms is credited to Daniel Shanks, who used it in a 1971 paper on class numbers. In search, Ira Pohl studied [bidirectional search](/bidirectional-search/) in a 1969 report and a 1971 paper. The three methods split an unknown into two halves and match a table of one half against the other.

Cryptography gave the method its name. In 1977 Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman analysed exhaustive search on the NBS Data Encryption Standard, and they pointed out that encrypting twice with two 56-bit keys does not square the cost of an attack if the attacker has a large memory. Ralph Merkle and Hellman returned to multiple encryption in 1981. They gave a chosen-plaintext attack on triple encryption with two keys, which is weaker than its 112-bit key suggests. These results are the reason triple DES has three stages. In 1999 Paul van Oorschot and Michael Wiener applied [parallel collision search](/parallel-collision-search/) to meet-in-the-middle attacks, to trade the large table for more computation.

The knapsack cryptosystem of Merkle and Hellman, from 1978, made subset sum a target for cryptanalysts. Richard Schroeppel and Adi Shamir published their four-list method in 1981, in the *SIAM Journal on Computing*, under the title "A $T = O(2^{n/2})$, $S = O(2^{n/4})$ algorithm for certain NP-complete problems". It kept the time of Horowitz and Sahni and cut the memory to the fourth root, and for almost thirty years it was the best generic method for hard knapsacks.

Nick Howgrave-Graham and Antoine Joux went below $2^{n/2}$ in 2010, at EUROCRYPT, with the first generic algorithm whose heuristic time beats Schroeppel and Shamir. The long version of their paper claimed an exponent of 0.3113; Anja Becker, Jean-Sébastien Coron and Joux restated the bounds as time $2^{0.337n}$ and memory $2^{0.256n}$ in 2011, when they extended the method to representations over $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and reached $2^{0.291n}$. The same counting of [representations](/representation-technique/) then moved to the decoding of random linear codes, in work of Alexander May, Alexander Meurer and Enrico Thomae (2011) and of Becker, Joux, May and Meurer (2012).

## Further reading

- Ellis Horowitz and Sartaj Sahni, *Computing partitions with applications to the knapsack problem*, Journal of the ACM 21(2), 1974. The two-list method for subset sums and the 0/1 knapsack problem.
- Richard Schroeppel and Adi Shamir, *A $T = O(2^{n/2})$, $S = O(2^{n/4})$ algorithm for certain NP-complete problems*, SIAM Journal on Computing 10(3), 1981. The four lists and the two heaps, with the time and space bounds.
- Whitfield Diffie and Martin E. Hellman, *Exhaustive cryptanalysis of the NBS Data Encryption Standard*, Computer 10(6), 1977. The cost of a key search on DES and the remark on double encryption.
- Alfred J. Menezes, Paul C. van Oorschot and Scott A. Vanstone, *Handbook of Applied Cryptography*. Chapter 7 treats multiple encryption and the attacks on two and three stages.
- Nick Howgrave-Graham and Antoine Joux, *New generic algorithms for hard knapsacks*, EUROCRYPT 2010. The first use of representations for subset sum.
- Xavier Bonnetain, Rémi Bricout, André Schrottenloher and Yixin Shen, *Improved classical and quantum algorithms for subset-sum*, ASIACRYPT 2020. A careful restatement of the merging tree of Howgrave-Graham and Joux and of the later improvements.
