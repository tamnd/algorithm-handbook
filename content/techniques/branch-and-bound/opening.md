Branch and bound is backtracking with an objective. Take a knapsack of capacity 16 and four items with values 40, 30, 50 and 10 and weights 2, 5, 10 and 5. A backtracking search that tries every subset that fits builds a tree of 26 nodes. Branch and bound keeps the best packing found so far, the [incumbent](/incumbent-solution/), and computes for each node an upper bound on every packing below it: here, the value of the fractional knapsack on the items not yet decided. At the root the bound is 115. Once a packing of value 90 is known, any node whose bound is 90 or less is dropped with its whole subtree. [Depth-first branch and bound](/depth-first-branch-and-bound/) expands 6 nodes on this instance and proves that 90 is optimal.

Every method in the chapter has the same four parts: a way to split a problem into smaller ones, a bound on the best value inside each part, a record of the best solution so far, and an order in which to take the open parts. The art lies in the bound. A loose bound prunes nothing and the search is plain enumeration; a bound that is not a true bound prunes the optimum away. Most good bounds come from a relaxation: drop a constraint that makes the problem hard, solve what is left, and use its value.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in six groups, read in order.

1. **The parts of the method.** The [incumbent](/incumbent-solution/) is the threshold for pruning. A [bounding function](/bounding-function/) must never be worse than the best solution below a node. A [branching rule](/branching-rule/) must split a node into children that cover all its solutions and are each smaller. A [fathoming rule](/fathoming-rule/) drops a node that is infeasible, whose bound cannot beat the incumbent, or whose relaxation is already solved by a feasible solution. A [node selection rule](/node-selection-rule/) picks the next live node.
2. **Three orders of search.** [Depth-first branch and bound](/depth-first-branch-and-bound/) uses a stack and memory linear in the depth, and finds feasible solutions early. [Best-first branch and bound](/best-first-branch-and-bound/) uses a [priority queue](/priority-queue/) on the bound and expands only nodes whose bound is at least the optimum; every order must expand the nodes whose bound is above it, so best-first is close to the least possible work, at the price of memory that can grow with the tree. [Breadth-first branch and bound](/breadth-first-branch-and-bound/) uses a [queue](/queue/) and works level by level.
3. **Combinatorial problems.** [Knapsack branch and bound](/knapsack-branch-and-bound/) sorts items by value per unit of weight and bounds with the [fractional knapsack](/fractional-knapsack/). [Little's algorithm](/little-algorithm/), the 1963 paper that named the method, bounds tours by reducing the rows and columns of the cost matrix and branches on one arc. The [Gilmore-Lawler bound](/gilmore-lawler-bound/) bounds the quadratic assignment problem by an [assignment problem](/assignment-problem/).
4. **Integer programs.** The [Land-Doig algorithm](/land-doig-algorithm/) solves a linear program at every node and splits on a fractional variable. The [Balas additive algorithm](/balas-additive-algorithm/) handles 0-1 programs with additions and comparisons only.
5. **Dominance and reduction.** [Dominance pruning](/dominance-pruning/) drops a partial solution that another partial solution beats for every completion. [Branch and reduce](/branch-and-reduce/) simplifies the instance at every node before it branches, and proves its running time by a recurrence on the size.
6. **Continuous variables.** [Interval branch and bound](/interval-branch-and-bound/) bisects boxes of real numbers and bounds a function on a box with [interval arithmetic](/interval-arithmetic/).

## A first reading

Start with the [incumbent](/incumbent-solution/), the [bounding function](/bounding-function/) and the [fathoming rule](/fathoming-rule/), which are short. Then read [depth-first branch and bound](/depth-first-branch-and-bound/) in full and work its knapsack trace by hand, followed by [knapsack branch and bound](/knapsack-branch-and-bound/), which runs the same idea with a bound computed in one pass over the sorted items. Read the [node selection rule](/node-selection-rule/) and [best-first branch and bound](/best-first-branch-and-bound/) next, with the proof that no order can expand fewer nodes, up to ties, and then the [branching rule](/branching-rule/). End with [Little's algorithm](/little-algorithm/) for a bound that needs real work. That is eight entries.

Skim [breadth-first branch and bound](/breadth-first-branch-and-bound/); its main lesson is why it is seldom used. The five advanced entries can wait. Read [Land-Doig](/land-doig-algorithm/) once you know linear programming, [branch and reduce](/branch-and-reduce/) when you study exact exponential algorithms, and [interval branch and bound](/interval-branch-and-bound/) after interval arithmetic.
