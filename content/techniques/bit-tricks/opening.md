A machine word is a row of $w$ bits, usually 32 or 64, and the processor applies AND, OR, exclusive-or and shifts to all of them in one step. Bit tricks are the methods that use this: they treat a word as a set, as a vector of small numbers, or as a binary numeral whose arithmetic has useful side effects. The expression $x \wedge (x - 1)$ clears the lowest 1-bit of $x$, so a loop over it counts the 1-bits in as many rounds as there are 1-bits. A [bit vector](/bit-set/) answers a set union over a universe of $n$ items in $\Theta(n / w)$ word operations instead of $n$. [Bit parallelism](/bit-parallelism/) runs the [Shift-Or](/shift-or/) string matcher with one word of state for a pattern of up to $w$ symbols.

These methods sit inside other algorithms more than they stand alone. The [Fenwick tree](/fenwick-tree/) walks its indices with $i \wedge (-i)$, [bitmask DP](/bitmask-dp/) indexes its table by subsets, the [iterative FFT](/iterative-fft/) reorders its input by [bit reversal](/bit-reversal/), and spatial indexes sort points by their [Morton codes](/morton-encoding/). The chapter collects the tricks once, with proofs, so that the entries that use them can point here.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups.

1. **Words and signs.** [Bitwise operations](/bitwise-operations/) and [two's complement](/twos-complement/), the encoding in which $-x = \overline{x} + 1$ and signed and unsigned words share one adder. [Sign extension](/sign-extension/) and [endianness conversion](/endianness-conversion/) widen and reorder words, and [bit manipulation](/bit-manipulation/) surveys the identities the rest of the chapter uses.
2. **Words as sets.** A [bitmask](/bitmask/) holds a subset of up to $w$ items, [bit packing](/bit-packing/) stores fields of $b$ bits with no gaps, and a [bit vector](/bit-set/) spreads a large set over $\lceil n / w \rceil$ words.
3. **Finding one bit.** The [lowest set bit](/lowest-set-bit/) gives the [power-of-two test](/power-of-two-test/) and the [trailing zero count](/trailing-zero-count/); the highest gives the [floor of $\lg x$](/floor-log2/), the [leading zero count](/leading-zero-count/) and the [next power of two](/next-power-of-two/). The [de Bruijn bit scan](/de-bruijn-bit-scan/) finds the index of a bit with one multiplication and a table.
4. **Counting bits.** The [population count](/population-count/) by [Kernighan's loop](/kernighan-popcount/), by [table lookup](/table-lookup/) and by the [SWAR method](/swar-popcount/), which is the standard case of [SIMD within a register](/simd-within-a-register/); [parity](/bit-parity/) is the count modulo 2.
5. **XOR identities and branch-free code.** The [single number](/single-number/) problem, [swapping](/swap-two-values/) and the [XOR swap](/xor-swap/), and [branchless programming](/branchless-programming/), which replaces a hard-to-predict jump by a mask.
6. **Moving bits.** [Bit interleaving](/bit-interleaving/) and [bit matrix transposition](/bit-matrix-transposition/) are solved, like bit reversal, in $\lg w$ rounds of masked block swaps.
7. **Subsets as numbers.** [Gosper's hack](/gospers-hack/) lists the subsets of one size, [submask enumeration](/submask-enumeration/) lists the submasks of a mask, and [SOS DP](/sos-dp/) solves the [sum over subsets](/sum-over-subsets/) problem in $n 2^{n-1}$ additions instead of $3^n$.
8. **Whole algorithms in words.** [Bit slicing](/bit-slicing/) runs a Boolean circuit on $w$ inputs at once, the [Four Russians method](/four-russians-method/) saves a factor $\log n$ by tabulating small blocks, and [Roaring bitmaps](/roaring-bitmap/) compress large sets of 32-bit integers.

## A first reading

Read [bitwise operations](/bitwise-operations/), [two's complement](/twos-complement/), [bitmask](/bitmask/) and [lowest set bit](/lowest-set-bit/) first: almost every other entry uses them. Then read [population count](/population-count/), [Kernighan's bit count](/kernighan-popcount/), the [power-of-two test](/power-of-two-test/) and the [bit vector](/bit-set/). Go on to [submask enumeration](/submask-enumeration/), [Gosper's hack](/gospers-hack/) and [branchless programming](/branchless-programming/), and finish with the [SWAR population count](/swar-popcount/), whose trace on one 16-bit word shows how masks keep the fields apart. That is twelve entries, and they are enough to read code that uses bit tricks.

The groups on moving bits and on whole algorithms can wait. Bit slicing, the Four Russians method and Roaring bitmaps each assume a field outside this chapter: Boolean circuits, matrix multiplication and set data structures.

## Before you start

You need binary numerals, as in the [positional number system](/positional-number-system/) of Volume 11, and from Volume 1 the [logarithms](/logarithms/) and the [word RAM](/word-ram/), the model in which one operation on a $w$-bit word costs one step. A few entries assume a data structure from Volume 3, such as the [dictionary problem](/dictionary-problem/), or [Boolean matrix multiplication](/boolean-matrix-multiplication/) from Volume 11. When an entry gives C code, read the pitfalls: shifts by $w$ or more and overflow of signed integers are undefined there, and many tricks are correct only on unsigned words.
