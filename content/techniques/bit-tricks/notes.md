## History

Bit tricks are as old as binary computers, and many have no known inventor. The earliest one in this chapter with a date is the population count loop that clears the lowest 1-bit: Peter Wegner described it in a short note in Communications of the ACM in 1960, for a machine with the IBM 704 instruction code. Kernighan and Ritchie later stated the fact behind it in an exercise of The C Programming Language, and the loop now carries Kernighan's name. In 1966 Guy M. Morton, in a technical report of IBM Ltd., Ottawa, proposed interleaving the bits of coordinates to order the records of a geodetic database, the order now called the Z-order or Morton order.

In February 1972 the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory issued HAKMEM, AI Memo 239, by Michael Beeler, Bill Gosper and Richard Schroeppel, a collection of short programs and facts. Its item 175, by Gosper, is the hack that steps to the next word with the same number of 1-bits. Two other strands date from the same years. In 1970 Arlazarov, Dinic, Kronrod and Faradzhev computed the transitive closure of a graph by tabulating blocks of about $\lg n$ bits, the method now called the Four Russians method, and in 1980 Masek and Paterson used the same idea for edit distance. In 1975 Leslie Lamport showed how to process several bytes at once with full-word instructions, the idea that Fisher and Dietz named SIMD within a register in a 1998 paper.

Word operations then became a tool of algorithm design. Allison and Dix gave a bit-vector algorithm for the longest common subsequence in 1986, Baeza-Yates and Gonnet the Shift-Or string search in 1992, and Myers a bit-vector algorithm for approximate matching in 1999. Biham wrote DES as a circuit and ran it bit-sliced in 1997, 64 encryptions at once on a 64-bit machine. Sarwate's table-driven CRC of 1988 and the de Bruijn bit scan, written up by Leiserson, Prokop and Randall in an MIT note of 1998, show the other direction, a small table in place of a loop. On the side of theory, Furst, Saxe and Sipser proved in 1984 that constant-depth circuits of polynomial size cannot compute parity.

Two books collected the field. Henry S. Warren's Hacker's Delight, whose second edition appeared in 2013, gathers the tricks for counting, finding and moving bits with proofs, and Knuth's Section 7.1.3 of The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 4A (2011), treats them as broadword computing, with the notation $\nu x$ and $\rho x$ used in this chapter. In exact algorithms, the fast zeta and Möbius transforms over subsets appear in the fast subset convolution of Björklund, Husfeldt, Kaski and Koivisto (2007); programming contests know the same loop as SOS DP. Roaring bitmaps, by Chambi, Lemire, Kaser and Godin (2016), with run containers added the same year by Lemire, Ssi-Yan-Kai and Kaser, carried bit vectors into compressed bitmap indexes and posting lists.

## Further reading

- Henry S. Warren, Jr., *Hacker's Delight*, second edition, Addison-Wesley (2013). The standard collection of bit tricks, with derivations; Chapters 2, 5 and 7 cover most of this chapter.
- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 4A, Combinatorial Algorithms, Part 1*, Addison-Wesley (2011). Section 7.1.3, Bitwise Tricks and Techniques, gives the theory of broadword computation with many exercises.
- Michael Beeler, R. William Gosper and Richard Schroeppel, *HAKMEM*, MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, AI Memo 239 (1972). The source of Gosper's hack and of many early tricks, short and dense.
- Jörg Arndt, *Matters Computational: Ideas, Algorithms, Source Code*, Springer (2011). Chapter 1, Bit wizardry, is a long, code-first catalogue of operations on words, from single bits to bit counts and base-2 logarithms.
- Fedor V. Fomin and Dieter Kratsch, *Exact Exponential Algorithms*, Springer (2010). Section 7.1 gives the fast zeta and Möbius transforms and their use in exponential algorithms over subsets.
