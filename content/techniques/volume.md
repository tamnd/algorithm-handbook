Most algorithms are not invented from nothing. They are built by one of a few general methods, applied to a new problem. Merge sort, the fast Fourier transform and Karatsuba multiplication are the same method, divide and conquer, applied to sorting, to polynomials and to integers. The shortest edit script behind `diff`, the line breaking of a typesetting program and the pricing of a cut rod are the same method again, dynamic programming. This volume is about the methods themselves: how each one works, when it applies, how to prove an algorithm built with it correct, and how to find its cost.

Many of the problems in this volume are small, chosen to teach the methods: cutting a rod, making change with coins, scheduling meetings in rooms, placing queens on a board, trapping rain water between bars, counting the 1 bits of a word. The same methods come back in every later volume, on graphs, strings, geometry and numbers.

## The ideas this volume teaches

Each chapter is one method, or a family of methods that share an idea. A few questions come up in every chapter.

- **How the problem splits.** [Divide and conquer](/divide-and-conquer-paradigm/) splits it into independent parts. [Dynamic programming](/optimal-substructure/) splits it into parts that overlap and stores each answer once. A [greedy algorithm](/greedy-algorithm/) makes one choice and is left with one smaller problem. [Backtracking](/generic-backtracking-algorithm/) tries each choice in turn and undoes it.
- **Why the result is right.** Each method has its own proof pattern. Divide and conquer is proved by strong induction on the size, dynamic programming by an argument that an optimal solution is made of optimal solutions to subproblems, a greedy algorithm by an [exchange argument](/exchange-argument/) or by showing that [greedy stays ahead](/greedy-stays-ahead/).
- **What it costs.** Divide and conquer is priced by a recurrence, dynamic programming by the number of subproblems times the work per subproblem, a scan with [two pointers](/two-pointers/) by the fact that each pointer only moves forward. A search is priced by the size of the tree it explores, and [branch and bound](/depth-first-branch-and-bound/) by how much of that tree a bound cuts away.
- **Trading time for memory, or memory for time.** [Meet in the middle](/horowitz-sahni-algorithm/) solves subset sum in $\Theta(2^{n/2})$ time instead of $2^n$ by storing $2^{n/2}$ sums. [Iterative deepening](/iterative-deepening-search/) does the opposite: it repeats work to keep memory linear in the depth.
- **Changing the problem.** A [reduction](/reduction/) solves a problem by turning it into one already solved, such as sorting, shortest paths or a linear program. [Coordinate compression](/coordinate-compression/) and [presorting](/presorting/) are small reductions used every day.
- **Using the machine.** A word of 64 bits is 64 small processors. The [bit tricks](/bitwise-operations/) of the last chapter count, find and enumerate bits in a few instructions, and [bit parallelism](/bit-parallelism/) runs many small computations in one word.

## The chapters

**Chapter 2.1, Divide and conquer**, gives the method in its general form, with the template, the recurrence $T(n) = aT(n/b) + f(n)$ and the induction that proves it correct. It adds the case with one subproblem, [decrease and conquer](/decrease-and-conquer/), and an advanced method that recurses over the time line of an offline sequence of operations.

**Chapter 2.2, Dynamic programming**, is the largest chapter. It starts from [optimal substructure](/optimal-substructure/) and [overlapping subproblems](/overlapping-subproblems/), solves them top down with [memoization](/memoization/) or bottom up with [tabulation](/tabulation/), and recovers the solution by [traceback](/traceback/). Then come the standard patterns: over intervals, over trees, over subsets as bitmasks, over digits. The chapter ends with the speed-ups that use the structure of the cost, such as [Knuth's optimization](/knuth-optimization/), the [convex hull trick](/convex-hull-trick/) and the [SMAWK algorithm](/smawk-algorithm/).

**Chapter 2.3, Greedy**, makes the locally best choice and never takes it back. It shows when this works, for [activity selection](/activity-selection/), the [fractional knapsack](/fractional-knapsack/) and several scheduling problems, and how to prove it. Its theory is the [matroid](/matroid/): the [matroid greedy theorem](/matroid-greedy-theorem/) says the greedy algorithm finds a maximum-weight independent set for every nonnegative weighting exactly when the system is a matroid.

**Chapter 2.4, Backtracking**, builds a solution one decision at a time, as a path in a [state space tree](/state-space-tree/), and backs up when a [partial solution](/partial-solution/) cannot be completed. [Pruning](/search-tree-pruning/) and the order in which variables are chosen decide how much of the tree is explored.

**Chapter 2.5, Branch and bound**, adds an objective. A [bounding function](/bounding-function/) and the best solution found so far, the [incumbent](/incumbent-solution/), cut off every subtree that cannot do better. The chapter covers the rules for branching, selecting and discarding nodes, and classic instances such as [knapsack branch and bound](/knapsack-branch-and-bound/) and the [Land-Doig algorithm](/land-doig-algorithm/) for integer programs.

**Chapter 2.6, Search strategies**, explores a space of states without a fixed tree. The order of the [search frontier](/search-frontier/) gives depth-first, [uniform-cost](/uniform-cost-search/), [best-first](/best-first-search/), [bidirectional](/bidirectional-search/) and [iterative deepening](/iterative-deepening-search/) search. The chapter defines when a search is [complete and optimal](/completeness-and-optimality-of-search/).

**Chapter 2.7, Two pointers and windows**, scans an array with two indices that only move forward, so the scan is linear. It covers pair search, the [Dutch national flag](/dutch-national-flag/), [fast and slow pointers](/fast-and-slow-pointers/), and the [sliding window](/sliding-window/) with its variants, up to the [sliding window maximum](/sliding-window-maximum-deque/) with a monotonic deque.

**Chapter 2.8, Sweep line**, moves a line across the plane and stops at [event points](/event-point/), keeping the objects the line crosses in a [status structure](/sweep-status-structure/). It finds the [area of a union of rectangles](/area-of-union-of-rectangles/) and the [skyline](/skyline-problem/), and it applies the same idea to time in [event-driven simulation](/event-driven-simulation/).

**Chapter 2.9, Meet in the middle**, splits a search over $2^n$ choices into two searches over $2^{n/2}$ and joins them. It covers the [Horowitz-Sahni algorithm](/horowitz-sahni-algorithm/) for subset sum, the [attack on double encryption](/meet-in-the-middle-attack/), and the later algorithms that save memory or time.

**Chapter 2.10, Randomization techniques**, defines the [randomized algorithm](/randomized-algorithm/) and its [expected running time](/expected-running-time/), and gives the tools to analyse one, such as [backwards analysis](/backwards-analysis/) and the [probabilistic method](/probabilistic-method/).

**Chapter 2.11, Reductions and transforms**, solves a problem by changing it: into another problem, as in a [reduction](/reduction/) or a [reduction to linear programming](/reduction-to-linear-programming/), into another form, as in [transform and conquer](/transform-and-conquer/) and [canonicalization](/canonicalization/), or into another graph, as in [vertex splitting](/vertex-splitting/).

**Chapter 2.12, Bit tricks**, works on the binary form of integers: [two's complement](/twos-complement/), the [lowest set bit](/lowest-set-bit/), [population count](/population-count/), [submask enumeration](/submask-enumeration/), and the methods that pack many values into one word, such as [SIMD within a register](/simd-within-a-register/) and the [Four Russians method](/four-russians-method/).

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course, read these entries in this order. From Chapter 2.1: [divide and conquer](/divide-and-conquer-paradigm/) and [decrease and conquer](/decrease-and-conquer/). From Chapter 2.2: [optimal substructure](/optimal-substructure/), [overlapping subproblems](/overlapping-subproblems/), [memoization](/memoization/), [tabulation](/tabulation/), [rod cutting](/rod-cutting-problem/) and the [coin change problem](/coin-change-problem/). From Chapter 2.3: the [greedy algorithm](/greedy-algorithm/), [activity selection](/activity-selection/) and the [exchange argument](/exchange-argument/). From Chapter 2.4: the [generic backtracking algorithm](/generic-backtracking-algorithm/). From Chapter 2.5: [depth-first branch and bound](/depth-first-branch-and-bound/). From Chapter 2.7: [two pointers](/two-pointers/) and the [sliding window](/sliding-window/). From Chapter 2.8: the [sweep line technique](/sweep-line-technique/). From Chapter 2.11: [reduction](/reduction/). From Chapter 2.12: [bitwise operations](/bitwise-operations/) and the [lowest set bit](/lowest-set-bit/). Do the exercises marked ◦ and • at the end of these chapters. That is a complete first unit on the methods of algorithm design.

The chapters can be read in almost any order. Chapter 2.2 should come before Chapter 2.3, since a greedy algorithm is best understood as a dynamic program that needs to look at only one choice, and Chapter 2.4 before Chapter 2.5, since branch and bound is backtracking with a bound. Chapter 2.12 is independent of the rest, though the bitmask dynamic programs of Chapter 2.2 use its notation. The opening page of each chapter names its groups and the order to read them, and the entries marked *Advanced* and *Research* are the place to go deeper.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained. Entries in other volumes are cited by number too: 1.3.1 is [recursion](/recursion/) in Volume 1, and 3.5.3 is the [priority queue](/priority-queue/) in Volume 3.

## What you need first

You should be able to read a short program and follow a proof by induction. From Volume 1 you need [recursion](/recursion/), [induction](/induction/), [loop invariants](/loop-invariant/), [asymptotic notation](/asymptotic-notation/), the [cut-and-paste argument](/cut-and-paste-argument/) for dynamic programming and, for Chapter 2.10, [linearity of expectation](/linearity-of-expectation/). From Volume 3 you need the [priority queue](/priority-queue/), the [hash table](/hash-table/) and the [balanced binary search tree](/balanced-binary-search-tree/). From the volumes after this one you need [sorting](/sorting-problem/), [binary search](/binary-search/), [prefix sums](/prefix-sums/), and [depth-first](/depth-first-search/) and [breadth-first search](/breadth-first-search/) on graphs; each can be read when an entry first asks for it.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
