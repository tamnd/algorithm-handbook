Eight queens go on a chessboard so that no two attack each other. Writing one column for each row gives $8^8$ placements, and the tree of all their prefixes has 19,173,961 nodes. Placing the queens row by row, and giving up on a row as soon as the new queen is attacked, visits only 2057 of those nodes and still finds all 92 solutions. That is backtracking: build a solution one decision at a time, test each partial solution, and back up as soon as the test says it can lead nowhere.

The method is exhaustive. It never misses a solution, and its worst case is exponential, because the problems it is used for, from puzzles to constraint satisfaction, are usually NP-hard. What decides its speed in practice is the size of the part of the tree it visits, and that depends on two things: how early the test rejects a dead partial solution, and in which order the decisions are made. The chapter is short and is built around these two questions.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in four groups, read in order.

1. **Partial solutions and the tree they form.** A [partial solution](/partial-solution/) is a prefix of decisions with a test that must be sound (it never rejects a prefix of a solution), closed under prefixes, and exact on complete solutions. The [state space tree](/state-space-tree/) has the partial solutions as its nodes; the entry counts it for queens and for subset sum and introduces Knuth's estimate of its size. [Search-tree pruning](/search-tree-pruning/) names five ways to cut it: by feasibility, by a bound, by symmetry, by dominance and by the alpha-beta rule of game search.
2. **The algorithm.** The [generic backtracking algorithm](/generic-backtracking-algorithm/) is a recursive procedure of eight lines, a [depth-first search](/depth-first-search/) of the state space tree that never builds the tree. The entry traces it on 4 queens, where it visits 17 prefixes and makes 60 tests, and proves that Knuth's random-path estimate is unbiased. [Chronological backtracking](/chronological-backtracking/) is the same procedure on a constraint satisfaction problem; when a variable has no consistent value, it returns to the variable assigned just before, even when that variable had nothing to do with the failure, and so may repeat the same failure many times.
3. **Two problems.** [Rat in a maze](/rat-in-a-maze/) looks for a path through a grid by trying the four moves in turn and unmarking a cell on the way back; finding one path is a graph search, but listing all simple paths is a true backtracking task. A [cryptarithmetic puzzle](/cryptarithmetic-puzzle/) such as SEND + MORE = MONEY assigns digits to letters; a column-by-column search with carries makes 7236 digit choices where brute force tries 1,814,400 maps.
4. **The order of the search.** [Variable-ordering heuristics](/variable-ordering-heuristics/) choose the next variable. The minimum-remaining-values rule, or fail first, picks the variable with the fewest values left; the degree rule breaks ties by the number of constraints; [DSATUR](/dsatur/) is the same idea for [graph coloring](/graph-coloring/). With forward checking, the rule solves 20 queens after 145 nodes against 145,151 for the row order.

## A first reading

Read the [partial solution](/partial-solution/) and the [state space tree](/state-space-tree/) first, and draw the tree for 4 queens. Then read the [generic backtracking algorithm](/generic-backtracking-algorithm/) and follow its trace by hand to the two solutions 2413 and 3142. Read [search-tree pruning](/search-tree-pruning/) next, and then the two problems, [rat in a maze](/rat-in-a-maze/) and the [cryptarithmetic puzzle](/cryptarithmetic-puzzle/), solving the 5 by 5 maze and SEND + MORE = MONEY yourself before you read the solutions. That is six entries, enough for a first course.

[Chronological backtracking](/chronological-backtracking/) and [variable-ordering heuristics](/variable-ordering-heuristics/) treat backtracking as a solver for constraint satisfaction problems and can be read with Volume 16. Chapter 2.5 continues where this chapter stops: [depth-first branch and bound](/depth-first-branch-and-bound/) is backtracking with a bound on the best value a subtree can still reach.
