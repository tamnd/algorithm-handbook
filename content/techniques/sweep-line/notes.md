## History

The plane sweep entered algorithm design with Michael Shamos and Dan Hoey's paper on geometric intersection problems at FOCS in 1976. To decide whether any two of $n$ segments cross, they swept a line over the endpoints, kept the crossed segments in a balanced tree in their order along the line, and tested only neighbours, in $O(n \log n)$ time. Jon Bentley and Thomas Ottmann (1979) turned the detection into reporting: the event queue now receives the crossing points as the sweep finds them, and the status swaps two segments at each crossing, for $O((n + k) \log n)$ time. Franco Preparata and Michael Shamos made the sweep a standard method in their book of 1985, and de Berg, Cheong, van Kreveld and Overmars give the version with a single event for all segments through a point. Edelsbrunner and Mücke (1990) proposed simulation of simplicity, which perturbs the input symbolically instead of writing a rule for each degenerate case.

Rectangles came next. Victor Klee asked in 1977 whether the length of a union of $n$ intervals can be found in fewer than $O(n \log n)$ steps, and Michael Fredman and Bruce Weide (1978) showed that it cannot with linear tests. In the same year as Klee's question, Bentley gave the sweep with a segment tree for the area of a union of rectangles in unpublished notes; applied one dimension at a time it takes $O(n^{d-1} \log n)$ time in $d$ dimensions, and Timothy Chan (2013) lowered the bound for $d \ge 3$ to $O(n^{d/2})$. Bentley and Derick Wood (1980) reported all $k$ intersecting pairs of rectangles in $O(n \log n + k)$ time, with the design of integrated circuits as their motive. Michael Ben-Or's lower bounds for algebraic computation trees (1983) give the $\Omega(n \log n)$ bounds for these problems through element distinctness.

The skyline is a teaching problem: Udi Manber used it in Section 5.6 of his book of 1989 to show divide and conquer by merging two skylines; the heap sweep is the general plane sweep applied to it. Its relative with sloped pieces, the lower envelope of segments, has a deeper theory. Sergiu Hart and Micha Sharir (1986) proved that Davenport-Schinzel sequences of order 3 have length $\Theta(n \alpha(n))$, Ady Wiernik and Sharir (1988) showed that segments reach this bound, and John Hershberger (1989) computed the envelope in $O(n \log n)$ time. Sharir and Pankaj Agarwal wrote the book on the subject in 1995.

The rotational sweep goes back to D. T. Lee's thesis of 1978, which built the visibility graph of $n$ segments by a sweep around each vertex, in $O(n^2 \log n)$ time. Emo Welzl (1985) and Asano, Asano, Guibas, Hershberger and Imai (1986) lowered this to $O(n^2)$ with the arrangement of the dual lines, and Chazelle and Lee (1986) gave an $O(n^2)$ algorithm for placing a circle of given radius to cover the most points.

The sweep over time is older than the sweep over the plane. Alder and Wainwright described event-driven molecular dynamics for hard spheres in 1959: compute the time of the next collision exactly, move all particles to it, and resolve it. Lubachevsky (1991) gave a scheme for many particles with one scheduled event per particle. Sedgewick and Wayne present the collision system with lazy deletion as an application of priority queues.

## Further reading

- Mark de Berg, Otfried Cheong, Marc van Kreveld and Mark Overmars, *Computational Geometry: Algorithms and Applications*, third edition (2008). Chapter 2 develops the segment intersection sweep with its event queue, status tree and degenerate cases.
- Franco P. Preparata and Michael Ian Shamos, *Computational Geometry: An Introduction* (1985). Chapter 7 treats the intersection sweeps and Chapter 8 the geometry of rectangles, including the measure of a union with the segment tree.
- Udi Manber, *Introduction to Algorithms: A Creative Approach* (1989). Section 5.6 solves the skyline problem by divide and conquer.
- Micha Sharir and Pankaj K. Agarwal, *Davenport-Schinzel Sequences and Their Geometric Applications* (1995). The reference for lower envelopes and their $\Theta(n \alpha(n))$ bound.
- Robert Sedgewick and Kevin Wayne, *Algorithms*, fourth edition (2011). Section 6.1 builds an event-driven simulation of colliding disks with a priority queue and lazy deletion.
