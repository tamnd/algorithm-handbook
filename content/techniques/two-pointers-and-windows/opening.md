Many problems on arrays and strings ask for the best pair of positions: two items with a given sum, two walls that hold the most water, the shortest stretch that contains every letter of a word. A search over all pairs costs $\Theta(n^2)$. The [two-pointer technique](/two-pointers/) keeps two indices that only move forward, and at each step it uses the order of the data to move one of them. Since neither index ever moves back, each crosses the input once, and the scan takes $O(n)$ steps.

The correctness of every scan in this chapter rests on one kind of [loop invariant](/loop-invariant/): the pairs that the scan has dropped cannot hold a better answer than the best one found so far. The cost rests on one kind of count: each index moves at most $n$ times. Most of the work in reading an entry is in seeing why a particular move is safe. The entries come in pairs: a problem states what must be computed, and an algorithm entry gives the scan, its proof and its pitfalls.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter has eight groups, read in order.

1. **The technique.** [Two pointers](/two-pointers/) gives the three shapes of the method: indices that move toward each other from the two ends, indices that move in the same direction, and the two ends of a window.
2. **Pairs from both ends.** [Two sum on a sorted array](/two-sum-sorted/) and the [pair search](/two-pointer-pair-search/) that solves it with at most $n - 1$ iterations. [3SUM](/three-sum-two-pointer-scan/) runs the pair search once for each item, in $\Theta(n^2)$ time. The [container with most water](/container-with-most-water/) and [trapping rain water](/trapping-rain-water/) use the same inward scan, each with its own reason for moving the shorter side.
3. **Reading and writing pointers.** [Removing duplicates](/remove-duplicates-from-sorted-array/) reads with one index and writes with another. The [intersection of sorted arrays](/sorted-array-intersection/) walks two arrays at once with at most $m + n - 1$ comparisons, and doubling search does better when one array is much shorter.
4. **Partition in one pass.** The [Dutch national flag problem](/dutch-national-flag/) and [Dijkstra's three-way partition](/dutch-national-flag-algorithm/), which uses three indices and is the partition step of [three-way quicksort](/three-way-quicksort/).
5. **Pointers at two speeds.** [Fast and slow pointers](/fast-and-slow-pointers/) find the start and the length of the cycle of a sequence $x_{i+1} = f(x_i)$ with constant memory, and the middle of a list in one pass.
6. **The sliding window.** The [sliding window](/sliding-window/) template, the [monotone window property](/monotone-window-property/) that makes it correct, and the [minimum size subarray sum](/minimum-size-subarray-sum/) as a first problem.
7. **Windows over strings.** The [longest substring without repeating characters](/longest-substring-without-repeating-characters/) with its [algorithm](/sliding-window-longest-substring/), the [limit of $k$ distinct characters](/longest-substring-with-k-distinct/), and the [minimum window substring](/minimum-window-substring/) with its [algorithm](/minimum-window-substring-algorithm/).
8. **The maximum of a window.** The [monotonic deque](/sliding-window-maximum-deque/) gives the maximum of every window of length $k$ in linear time.

## A first reading

Start with [two pointers](/two-pointers/) and the [pair search](/two-pointer-pair-search/), and trace the pair search by hand on a sorted array of eight items. Then read the [container with most water](/container-with-most-water/), whose proof of the move is the clearest small example of the method, and [Dijkstra's three-way partition](/dutch-national-flag-algorithm/), with its invariant of four regions. Read [fast and slow pointers](/fast-and-slow-pointers/) and work its small example of a sequence with a tail and a cycle. Then turn to windows: the [sliding window](/sliding-window/) template, the [monotone window property](/monotone-window-property/), and the [longest substring without repeating characters](/longest-substring-without-repeating-characters/) with its [algorithm](/sliding-window-longest-substring/). That is nine entries, and enough for a first course.

The [minimum window substring](/minimum-window-substring-algorithm/), the [window maximum](/sliding-window-maximum-deque/) and [3SUM](/three-sum-two-pointer-scan/) are core entries and can come next. The 3SUM entry also opens a door to fine-grained complexity, where the quadratic bound of the scan is a conjecture with many consequences.

## Before you start

You need [loop invariants](/loop-invariant/) and [induction](/induction/) from Volume 1, since each scan is proved correct by an invariant, and [binary search](/binary-search/) from Volume 5, which the pair search often replaces. The windows use [hash tables](/hash-table/) and the [deque](/deque/) from Volume 3, the fast and slow pointers walk a [linked list](/linked-list/) or a [functional graph](/functional-graph/), and the monotone window property is the window form of a [monotone predicate](/monotone-predicate/) from Volume 5. Volume 6 treats the same windows from the side of the sequence, in [subarrays](/subarray/) and [prefix sums](/prefix-sums/), and is a good companion.
