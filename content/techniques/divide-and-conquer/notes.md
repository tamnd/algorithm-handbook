## History

The oldest algorithm in this chapter is [Euclid's algorithm](/euclidean-algorithm/), given in Book VII of the *Elements* around 300 BC, where it works by repeated subtraction. It is a decrease-and-conquer method in today's terms, and Gabriel Lamé bounded its number of steps in 1844, which makes it one of the first algorithms to have its running time analysed. Binary search was described by John Mauchly in the Moore School Lectures of 1946, and Derrick Henry Lehmer gave a version for every $n$ in 1960.

Divide and conquer came with the first stored-program computers. John von Neumann wrote a merge sort for the EDVAC in 1945. C. A. R. Hoare published quicksort and its selection twin FIND, now called quickselect, in the *Communications of the ACM* in 1961. The method then changed the cost of arithmetic. Anatolii Karatsuba found his three-product multiplication in 1960, in answer to a conjecture of Kolmogorov that $n^2$ steps are needed, and it was published in 1962; Andrei Toom (1963) and Stephen Cook (1966) extended it to more pieces. Volker Strassen showed in 1969, in a paper titled *Gaussian elimination is not optimal*, that seven products of half size suffice to multiply matrices. James Cooley and John Tukey published the fast Fourier transform in 1965, though Gauss had used the same splitting about 1805. In 1973 Manuel Blum, Robert Floyd, Vaughan Pratt, Ronald Rivest and Robert Tarjan gave the median of medians and with it selection in linear worst-case time.

The theory of the recurrences followed the algorithms. Jon Bentley, Dorothea Haken and James Saxe published a general method for divide-and-conquer recurrences in 1980, which became the master theorem of the textbooks, and Mohamad Akra and Louay Bazzi solved recurrences with unequal parts in 1998. The name *decrease and conquer* and the split into decrease by a constant, by a constant factor and by a variable amount are Anany Levitin's; other authors file the same algorithms under divide and conquer.

The methods that recurse over time are recent. Jon Bentley's *Multidimensional divide-and-conquer* (1980) split point sets on one coordinate to solve dominance problems, the idea behind CDQ. Heikki Mannila and Esko Ukkonen posed union-find with backtracking in 1986, and Jeffery Westbrook and Robert Tarjan analysed it in 1989. David Eppstein (1994) recursed over blocks of an offline sequence of edge updates to maintain minimum spanning trees. In the Chinese olympiad community the split of an operation sequence into halves is named after Chen Danqi, whose national training team paper of 2008 used it on a dynamic program. Richard Peng, Bryce Sandlund and Daniel Sleator (2019) used divide and conquer over the operation sequence for offline 2- and 3-edge and vertex connectivity in $O(\log n)$ time per operation.

## Further reading

- Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest and Clifford Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, fourth edition (2022). Section 2.3 introduces the method with merge sort; Chapter 4 solves its recurrences and proves the master theorem; Chapter 26 runs it in parallel.
- Jon Kleinberg and Éva Tardos, *Algorithm Design* (2005). Chapter 5 builds each example (counting inversions, closest pair, integer multiplication, the FFT) from the recurrence it must satisfy.
- Sanjoy Dasgupta, Christos Papadimitriou and Umesh Vazirani, *Algorithms* (2006). Chapter 2 is a short, clear account centred on multiplication, medians and the FFT.
- Anany Levitin, *Introduction to the Design and Analysis of Algorithms*, third edition (2011). Chapters 4 and 5 separate decrease and conquer from divide and conquer, with many small examples.
- Jon Louis Bentley, *Multidimensional Divide-and-Conquer*, Communications of the ACM 23(4), 1980. The source of divide and conquer on one coordinate for dominance and closest-point problems.
