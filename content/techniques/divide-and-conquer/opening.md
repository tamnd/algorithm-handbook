Divide and conquer cuts a problem into smaller copies of itself, solves the copies by the same method, and puts their answers together. [Merge sort](/merge-sort/) sorts two halves and merges them. [Karatsuba multiplication](/karatsuba-multiplication/) multiplies two $n$-digit numbers with three products of half size instead of four, and so beats the schoolbook method. The [fast Fourier transform](/fast-fourier-transform/) evaluates a polynomial at $n$ points from two transforms of half size. In each case a direct method costs $\Theta(n^2)$ or more, and the split brings the cost down to $\Theta(n \log n)$ or to a smaller power of $n$.

The method is old and simple, and most of its uses sit in other volumes, next to the problems they solve: the sorts in Volume 4, the multiplication algorithms in Volume 11, the recurrences that price them in Volume 1. This chapter is short. It holds the method itself, its special case with one subproblem, and one advanced method that recurses over the time line of a sequence of operations instead of over the input.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in three groups, one entry each, read in order.

1. **The method.** [Divide and conquer](/divide-and-conquer-paradigm/) gives the template (base case, divide, conquer, combine), the recurrence $T(n) = aT(n/b) + f(n)$ for its cost, and the strong induction that proves an instance of it correct. Its list of classic examples runs from [binary search](/binary-search/) through [counting inversions](/counting-inversions/), [Strassen's algorithm](/strassen-algorithm/) and [median of medians](/median-of-medians/). Its pitfalls matter as much: when the parts overlap, as in the naive Fibonacci recursion, the method does exponential work, and the cure is [memoization](/memoization/), the first step of dynamic programming.
2. **One subproblem.** [Decrease and conquer](/decrease-and-conquer/) solves a single smaller instance and extends its answer. A decrease by one gives [insertion sort](/insertion-sort/) and topological sorting by source removal; a decrease by half gives binary search and [exponentiation by squaring](/binary-exponentiation/); a decrease that depends on the data gives [Euclid's algorithm](/euclidean-algorithm/) and [quickselect](/quickselect/). The entry proves the two recurrences behind the first two kinds: $T(n) = T(n-1) + f(n)$ is a sum, and $T(n) = T(\lfloor n/2 \rfloor) + d$ is $c + d \lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor$.
3. **Recursion over time.** [Divide and conquer on queries](/divide-and-conquer-on-queries/) handles an offline sequence of updates and queries. Divide and conquer over time hangs each update on $O(\log q)$ nodes of a [segment tree](/segment-tree/) over time and walks the tree with a structure that can undo its last change, such as [union-find with rollback](/union-find-with-rollback/); this gives deletions to a structure that only supports insertions. CDQ divide and conquer splits the operations in half and lets the left half act on the right half in one sweep, which turns a dynamic problem into a static one.

## A first reading

Read [divide and conquer](/divide-and-conquer-paradigm/) in full, including the correctness argument and the pitfalls, and then [decrease and conquer](/decrease-and-conquer/), with its two claims about recurrences. Then see the method at work in four entries from other volumes: [merge sort](/merge-sort/), [counting inversions](/counting-inversions/), [Karatsuba multiplication](/karatsuba-multiplication/) and [median of medians](/median-of-medians/). Work the trace of [binary search](/binary-search/) and of [exponentiation by squaring](/binary-exponentiation/) by hand. That is eight entries, and enough for a first course.

[Divide and conquer on queries](/divide-and-conquer-on-queries/) is advanced and can wait until you know the segment tree and union-find. When you come to it, work the two traces in the entry, one for connectivity with edge deletions and one for CDQ, before you read the correctness arguments.
