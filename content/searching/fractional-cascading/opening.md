Many search structures look up the same key in many sorted lists, one after the other. A walk down a segment tree with a sorted list at each node ranks the key at every node on the path; a range tree does the same in its secondary lists; a point location structure tests one point against many chains. Done separately, each search costs a binary search, and a walk through $k$ lists of total size $n$ costs $O(k \log n)$. Fractional cascading brings this down to $O(\log n + k)$ with linear space: one binary search in the first list, and then a constant number of steps for each list after it.

The idea is to copy every second element of each list into the list before it, with a pointer back to where it came from. After the key is found in one list, the nearest copy points to a place in the next list that is at most one or two steps from the answer. The copies are the "fraction" in the name, and the passing of copies from list to list is the "cascade". This chapter is short and most of its entries are advanced. It states the problem, builds the structure, and then asks how far the idea goes: to lists that change, and to maps of the plane in place of lists.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in three groups, read in order.

1. **One key in many lists.** [Multiple list search](/multiple-list-search/) asks for the successor of one key in each of $k$ sorted lists, and proves that $\Omega(\log n + k)$ steps are needed. A [catalog graph](/catalog-graph/) puts the lists at the vertices of a graph of bounded degree, and a query walks a connected part of it, often choosing the next vertex as it goes.
2. **Bridges.** A [fractional cascading bridge](/fractional-cascading-bridge/) is a copied element with its pointer. On a path, the augmented lists have total size at most $2n + k + 1$, and a query costs one binary search and one comparison per later list. On a graph of degree $d$, each step costs $O(\log d)$. The [hive graph](/hive-graph/) is an earlier planar structure of Chazelle that combs every other edge of a map in the same way, for reporting the segments that a vertical query crosses.
3. **Updates and limits.** [Dynamic fractional cascading](/dynamic-fractional-cascading/) supports insertions and deletions by splitting long gaps between bridges, as a B-tree splits full nodes, at an extra $O(\log \log n)$ per list. The [Chazelle–Liu lower bound](/fractional-cascading-lower-bound/) shows that for planar maps the idea fails: a fast query needs nearly quadratic space.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take [multiple list search](/multiple-list-search/), then the path case of the [fractional cascading bridge](/fractional-cascading-bridge/), and trace its example query by hand. Then read the definition of the [catalog graph](/catalog-graph/) and see the structure at work in the [layered range tree](/layered-range-tree/) and the [merge sort tree](/merge-sort-tree/), where it removes a factor of $\log n$ from the query. The [dynamic version](/dynamic-fractional-cascading/), the hive graph and the lower bound are research material and can wait. The one proof to understand fully is the correctness of the path query: why the answer in the next list is at the down pointer or one position before it.
