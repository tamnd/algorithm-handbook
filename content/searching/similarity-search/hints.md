1. $x \cdot y = 8$ and $\lVert x \rVert = \lVert y \rVert = 3$, so $\cos(x, y) = 8/9 \approx 0.8889$, the angle is about $27.27°$, and the squared distance of the unit vectors is $2 - 2 \cdot 8/9 = 2/9$. Since $z = 2x$, $\cos(x, z) = 1$ while $\lVert x - z \rVert = 3$: cosine similarity sees only direction, so two different points can be at similarity 1.

3. $1 - J(A, B) = 1 - 2/5 = 0.6$, $1 - J(B, C) = 1 - 2/5 = 0.6$ and $1 - J(A, C) = 1 - 1/7 = 6/7 \approx 0.857$. The largest distance, $6/7$, is below $0.6 + 0.6$, and each distance of $0.6$ is below $0.6 + 6/7$, so all three inequalities hold.

4. The squared distances in order are 8, 10, 2, 13, 1, 32, 8 and 4. The heap fills with 8, 10 and 2; then 1 replaces 10 and 4 replaces 8, and the second 8 is not strictly smaller than the top, so there are 2 replacements. The answer is $(2, 3)$, $(1, 1)$ and $(4, 2)$ at distances 1, $\sqrt 2$ and 2. The range query with radius 3 keeps the squared distances up to 9: these three points and $(0, 0)$ and $(0, 4)$.

5. The nearest distance is 3, so the 2-approximate answers are the points at 3 and 4.5. With $r = 4$, a point lies within $r$, so the output must be a point within $cr = 6$: the point at 3 or the one at 4.5. With $r = 2$, the nearest distance 3 lies in $(r, cr] = (2, 3]$, so "none" and the point at 3 are both correct. With $r = 1.5$, $cr = 2.25 < 3$, so the only correct output is "none".

6. The root is sort, with children port (key 1), heat (key 3), heap (key 4) and cheap (key 5). port has children sport (key 1) and sorts (key 2), and short hangs below sorts with key 2. The query computes $d(\text{spot}, \text{sort}) = 2$ and follows keys 1 to 3, skipping heap and cheap. At port the distance is 2 again, so it visits sport (distance 1, reported) and sorts (distance 3), and from sorts it follows key 2 to short (distance 2). heat, at distance 3, has no children. Six distances are computed out of eight, and the answer is sport.

9. Under the first order both minima are 3; under the second, $A$ gives 0 and $B$ gives 4; under the third both give 2. The signatures are $(3, 0, 2)$ and $(3, 4, 2)$, the estimate is $2/3$, and the true value is $|\{2, 3\}| / |\{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}| = 2/5$. Three functions give a standard deviation of about $0.28$, so the gap is not surprising.

11. The lower bound for a ball is $\delta(q, c) - \rho$: 3.5 for $A$, $2\sqrt 2 - 1 \approx 1.83$ for $B$ and 2.5 for $C$. Only $A$ is pruned at first, since its bound is at least 3. After the point at 2.1 is found in $B$, the bound 2.5 of $C$ is no smaller than 2.1, so $C$ is pruned as well.

13. A set of size 5 keeps $5 - 3 + 1 = 3$ elements and a set of size 4 keeps $4 - 3 + 1 = 2$. The prefixes are $acd$, $cde$, $ab$ and $ef$. The candidates are $(x_1, x_2)$, $(x_1, x_3)$ and $(x_2, x_4)$; their Jaccard values are $2/3$, $1/8$ and $1/2$, so only $(x_1, x_2)$ is output. Three pairs are never compared, among them $(x_1, x_4)$ with $J = 2/7$.

14. The size filter asks for $|y| \ge 0.7 \cdot 10 = 7$, and $8 \ge 7$. The overlap must be at least $\alpha = \lceil 0.7 \cdot 18 / 1.7 \rceil = 8$. The probing prefix of $x$ has $10 - 7 + 1 = 4$ elements and the indexing prefix of $y$ has $8 - \lceil 6.59 \rceil + 1 = 2$. At $(3, 1)$ the bound is $1 + \min(7, 7) = 8$, so the pair is kept; at $(4, 2)$ it is $1 + \min(6, 6) = 7 < 8$, so it is pruned.

17. The inner products with $q$ are 5, 4 and 1, so the answer is $a$; the distances are about 4.12, 1.41 and 1, so the nearest neighbor is $c$; the cosines are about 0.71, 1 and 0.71, so the most similar in direction is $b$. If every stored vector has length $R$, then $\lVert q - p \rVert^2 = \lVert q \rVert^2 + R^2 - 2\langle q, p \rangle$ and $\cos(q, p) = \langle q, p \rangle / (\lVert q \rVert R)$, and both are monotone in $\langle q, p \rangle$.

18. The nearest-neighbor distances of $0, 2, 3, 7, 12$ are 2, 1, 1, 4 and 5. For $q = 5$ only 7 has $|7 - 5| = 2 \le 4$, so the answer is $\{7\}$; for $q = 9.6$ the answer is $\{7, 12\}$. For the proof, take two points $p < p'$ on the same side of $q$, say to its right. Then $p' - p < p' - q$, so $p$ is closer to $p'$ than $q$ is, and $p'$ is not an answer. So each side gives at most one answer.

19. Every point links to the other two points of its group, so the graph has two parts, $\{0, 1, 2\}$ and $\{10, 11, 12\}$, with no edge between them. From 0 the search moves to 2 and stops there, at distance 9. An edge from 2 to 10 lets it continue to 10 and then 11. The [navigable small world](/navigable-small-world/) graph creates such long links, because the first points inserted link to points far apart.

22. Nesting and covering hold, but $C_0$ breaks separation: 3 and 4 are at distance $1$, which is not more than $2^0$. Remove 3 from $C_0$, so that $C_0 = \{0, 4, 8\}$; at level $-1$ the point 3 is covered by 4, and 1 by 0, within $2^0$. The point 8 first appears in $C_2$ with parent 0; 4 first appears in $C_1$ with parent 0 or 8, both at distance $4 = 2^2$; 1 and 3 first appear in $C_{-1}$ with parents 0 and 4.

24. If a hyperplane puts $x$ and $z$ on different sides, it puts $y$ on a different side from $x$ or from $z$. So $\Pr[x, z \text{ separated}] \le \Pr[x, y \text{ separated}] + \Pr[y, z \text{ separated}]$, and each probability is the angle divided by $\pi$. The function $1 - \cos$ is not a separation probability. It grows like $\theta^2/2$ for small angles, and the example $x = (1, 0)$, $y = (1, 1)$, $z = (0, 1)$ breaks the inequality.

25. For $\{1, \dots, 10\}$ the worst case is a radius just below 1: the ball holds only $p$, and the doubled ball holds $p - 1$, $p$ and $p + 1$, so $c = 3$; no larger radius gives a larger ratio. For the powers of two take $p = 2^m$ and $r$ just below $2^{m-1}$: the ball holds only $2^m$, and the doubled ball holds all $m + 1$ points, so $c = m + 1 = n$. The set lies on a line and has a small doubling dimension, yet its expansion constant is as large as it can be.

26. Three differing bits touch at most three blocks. With 4 blocks one block is untouched, and with 6 blocks three blocks are untouched, so some key of each design matches exactly. The first design keeps 4 copies, and each probe meets about $2^{34}/2^{16} = 2^{18}$ fingerprints, about a million in all. The second keeps $\binom{6}{3} = 20$ copies; each key has at least $10 + 10 + 11 = 31$ bits, so each probe meets at most about $2^{34}/2^{31} = 8$ fingerprints, at most 160 in all.

27. The bits agree when the minima agree, with probability $J$, or when they differ and the bits agree anyway, with probability $(1 - J)/2$. So $f = (1 + J)/2$ and $\hat J = 2\hat f - 1$; for 52 of 64, $\hat J = 0.625$. At $J = 0.6$, $f = 0.8$ and $\mathrm{Var}(\hat J) = 4 f(1 - f)/k = 0.64/k$, against $J(1 - J)/k = 0.24/k$ for plain MinHash. The one-bit scheme needs about $8/3$ times as many positions, but each takes 1 bit instead of 32, so it uses about 12 times less memory for the same variance.

28. At $k = 49$, $L \approx 694$ and the work is about $34{,}030$ hash evaluations plus about 520 expected far collisions, fewer than one per table, about $34{,}550$ in all. The minimum is at $k = 35$, with $L \approx 107$ and work about $8{,}290$: about $3{,}750$ hash evaluations and $4{,}540$ far collisions. The textbook choice drives the far collisions per table below one, which makes $L$ grow as $p_1^{-k}$; a shorter key accepts more collisions in return for far fewer tables.
