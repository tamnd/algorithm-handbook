## History

Exact similarity search began with files of words and records. Burkhard and Keller described trees keyed by integer distances for best-match file searching in 1973, the structure now called the BK-tree. Metric trees for general distances followed: Omohundro described ball trees in 1989, Uhlmann described metric trees split by the distance to one point or by the nearer of two points in 1991, and Yianilos named and analyzed vantage-point trees in 1993. Brin's GNAT (1995), the M-tree of Ciaccia, Patella and Zezula (1997) and the MVP-tree of Bozkaya and Özsoyoglu (1997) extended these ideas to several split points, to paged storage and to several vantage points. The survey of Chávez, Navarro, Baeza-Yates and Marroquín in 2001 put all of them in one framework of pivots and partitions.

The search for bounds in terms of the data rather than the space led to the expansion constant of Karger and Ruhl and the doubling dimension. Beygelzimer, Kakade and Langford introduced the cover tree in 2006 as a simple structure with linear space and query bounds that depend only on the expansion constant. Dasgupta and Freund showed in 2008 that random projection trees adapt to the intrinsic dimension of the data in the same spirit.

For high dimensions, Indyk and Motwani defined locality-sensitive hash families at STOC 1998 and solved approximate nearest neighbor search with them, and Gionis, Indyk and Motwani gave the practical version for Hamming space in 1999. Broder had already estimated the resemblance of Web documents from minima of random permutations of their shingles in 1997, for the AltaVista search engine. Charikar turned the random hyperplane rounding of Goemans and Williamson into a sketch for angles in 2002, and Manku, Jain and Das Sarma used 64-bit SimHash fingerprints to detect near duplicates in a Web crawler in 2007. Datar, Immorlica, Indyk and Mirrokni gave the family for Euclidean distance based on $p$-stable distributions in 2004. Bawa, Condie and Ganesan (2005) and Lv and coauthors (2007) reduced the number of tables with the LSH forest and with multi-probe queries, and Shrivastava and Li showed in 2014 that inner products need an asymmetric family.

Exact similarity joins came from databases. Chaudhuri, Ganti and Kaushik proposed a primitive operator for similarity joins in 2006 with the prefix filter at its core, Bayardo, Ma and Srikant scaled it to all pairs of sparse vectors with All-Pairs in 2007, and Xiao, Wang, Lin and Yu added the positional and suffix filters of PPJoin in 2008.

Vector search changed again in the 2010s. Sivic and Zisserman had indexed image descriptors with k-means cells and an inverted file in 2003, and Jégou, Douze and Schmid combined the inverted file with product quantization in 2011. Graphs took over in the same years: Dong, Charikar and Li built approximate k-nearest neighbor graphs with NN-Descent in 2011, Malkov and coauthors published navigable small world graphs in 2014, and Malkov and Yashunin's HNSW, a preprint of 2016 published in 2020, became the most used index of its kind. Subramanya and coauthors introduced DiskANN in 2019, for graphs that live on solid-state disks.

## Further reading

- Edgar Chávez, Gonzalo Navarro, Ricardo Baeza-Yates and José Luis Marroquín, *Searching in metric spaces*, ACM Computing Surveys 33(3) (2001). The unified view of metric indexes by pivots and partitions.
- Hanan Samet, *Foundations of Multidimensional and Metric Data Structures*, Morgan Kaufmann (2006). The reference for trees on points and on general metric spaces.
- Jure Leskovec, Anand Rajaraman and Jeffrey D. Ullman, *Mining of Massive Datasets*, second edition, Cambridge University Press (2014). The chapter on finding similar items covers shingling, MinHash, banding and LSH families with many exercises.
- Alexandr Andoni and Piotr Indyk, *Near-optimal hashing algorithms for approximate nearest neighbor in high dimensions*, Communications of the ACM 51(1) (2008). A short survey of locality-sensitive hashing and its exponents.
- Sariel Har-Peled, Piotr Indyk and Rajeev Motwani, *Approximate nearest neighbor: towards removing the curse of dimensionality*, Theory of Computing 8 (2012). The full journal version of the founding LSH results.
