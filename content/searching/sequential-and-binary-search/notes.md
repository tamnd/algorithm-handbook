## History

Binary search is older than the computer. People looked up words in dictionaries and names in phone books by opening near the middle long before anyone wrote a program. As a method for machines, John Mauchly described it in the Moore School Lectures of 1946. His version worked cleanly only for some sizes of table; Derrick Henry Lehmer gave a version for every $n$ in 1960. The details stayed hard for a long time. Jon Bentley reports in *Programming Pearls* that only about one in ten of the professional programmers in his courses wrote a correct binary search, and in 2006 Joshua Bloch showed that the midpoint $\lfloor (lo + hi)/2 \rfloor$ in the Java library could overflow, a bug that had been there for years.

The variants came soon after. W. W. Peterson proposed interpolation search in 1957, for records on random-access storage. David Ferguson described Fibonacci search in 1960, and Knuth later gave it, together with the uniform binary search and its table-driven form, in Volume 3 of *The Art of Computer Programming*, crediting L. E. Shar for the form with power-of-two steps. Ben Shneiderman studied jump searching in 1978, with the multi-level forms. The cost of interpolation search took longer to settle: Andrew Yao and Frances Yao proved in 1976 that it makes $\lg \lg n + O(1)$ probes on uniform keys, Yehoshua Perl and Edward Reingold gave a variant with a short proof in 1977, and Perl, Alon Itai and Haim Avni gave a simpler proof for the plain method in 1978.

Searching when the end is unknown has its own line. Jon Bentley and Andrew Yao studied unbounded search in 1976, gave the doubling method now called exponential search, and improved it to $\lg N + \lg \lg N + \cdots$ comparisons, close to their lower bound. The same problem had appeared a year earlier in coding theory, where Peter Elias designed prefix codes for the integers; a search strategy and a code are two views of the same tree. Doubling later returned as the galloping mode of Timsort and of adaptive merging.

Other lines came from other fields. Ronald Rivest analyzed self-organizing lists under independent requests in 1976, and Daniel Sleator and Robert Tarjan proved in 1985 that move to front is within a factor of two of any algorithm on any sequence, a founding result of competitive analysis; J. H. Hester and D. S. Hirschberg surveyed the area the same year. Nathan Linial and Michael Saks gave the lower bounds for searching ordered structures such as sorted matrices in 1985, and Richard Bird showed in 2006 how the saddleback walk can be improved for thin matrices. On modern hardware, the question turned from the number of comparisons to the cost of each: Paul-Virak Khuong and Pat Morin showed in 2017 that a branch-free loop over the Eytzinger layout, a numbering named after a sixteenth-century genealogist, beats the sorted order on large arrays.

## Further reading

- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 3: Sorting and Searching*, second edition (1998). Sections 6.1 and 6.2.1 treat sequential search, binary search and its variants, with exact counts and history.
- Jon Bentley, *Programming Pearls*, second edition (2000). Column 4 shows how to write a correct binary search from its invariant, and later columns tune it.
- Robert Sedgewick and Kevin Wayne, *Algorithms*, fourth edition (2011). Chapter 3 uses binary search in ordered symbol tables, with rank, floor and ceiling queries.
- Paul-Virak Khuong and Pat Morin, *Array Layouts for Comparison-Based Searching*, ACM Journal of Experimental Algorithmics 22 (2017). Experiments on sorted, Eytzinger, B-tree and van Emde Boas layouts, and why branches and prefetching matter.
- J. H. Hester and D. S. Hirschberg, *Self-Organizing Linear Search*, ACM Computing Surveys 17(3) (1985). A survey of move to front, transpose and their analysis.
