A learning algorithm takes examples and returns a rule. The examples may be emails marked spam or not spam, house sales with their prices, photos of handwritten digits, or the moves of a game together with who won. The rule predicts the label of the next email, the price of the next house, the digit in the next photo, the move to make next. Nobody writes the rule by hand; the algorithm fits it to the data. Such algorithms sort mail, rank search results, recommend films, read medical scans, translate text and control robots.

This volume collects the algorithms that learn a model from data, together with the theory that says when the model can be trusted. It starts with learning from labelled examples. Then come three ways to find structure in data with no labels: grouping points into clusters, mapping them to fewer dimensions, and training the neural networks that do much of both today. It ends with reasoning under uncertainty in probabilistic models, and with agents that learn to act from rewards.

## Why machine learning gets a volume of its own

A learning algorithm is judged on data it has not seen. That one fact sets the field apart from the rest of the handbook, where an algorithm is judged on its input. To say anything about unseen data you need a model of where the data comes from, so every entry in this volume names its model: the data distribution and the loss for learning, the graphical model for inference, the Markov decision process for control. Each guarantee then says whether it holds for every input, with high probability over the sample, or only in the limit. In this volume you will meet:

- **Generalization as a theorem.** In the [PAC model](/pac-learning/), examples are drawn independently from an unknown distribution. A finite class of $|H|$ rules needs about $\ln(|H|/\delta)/\varepsilon$ examples to reach error $\varepsilon$ with probability $1 - \delta$ when one rule in it is perfect. The [VC dimension](/vc-dimension/) replaces $\ln |H|$ for infinite classes, and the [no free lunch theorem](/no-free-lunch-theorem-learning/) shows why some restriction of the class is needed.
- **Optimization as a tool.** Training means minimizing a loss. [Ordinary least squares](/ordinary-least-squares/) has a closed form, [logistic regression](/logistic-regression/) and the [support vector machine](/support-vector-machine/) are convex problems, and a neural network is trained by gradient steps with no promise of a global optimum.
- **Hard problems and good heuristics.** Finding the best [k-means](/k-means/) clustering is [NP-hard](/k-means-np-hardness/). Lloyd's heuristic only reaches a local optimum, but [k-means++](/k-means-plus-plus/) seeding gives an expected cost within $8(\ln k + 2)$ of the optimum, as [its guarantee](/k-means-plus-plus-guarantee/) shows.
- **Randomness that keeps geometry.** The [Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma](/johnson-lindenstrauss-lemma/) says that any $n$ points map linearly to $O(\log n / \varepsilon^2)$ dimensions with every squared distance kept within a factor $1 \pm \varepsilon$. A random projection finds such a map with high probability.
- **Dynamic programming in new clothes.** [Backpropagation](/backpropagation/) computes every partial derivative of a network's loss on one example in time proportional to its number of weights. The [forward algorithm](/forward-algorithm/) of a hidden Markov model and [value iteration](/value-iteration/) for a Markov decision process are dynamic programs too.
- **Fixed points and contractions.** The [Bellman operator](/bellman-contraction/) is a contraction in the max norm, so value iteration converges geometrically to the optimal values.

## The chapters

**Chapter 25.1, Supervised learning**, learns a rule from labelled examples. It sets up the [learning problem](/empirical-risk-minimization/) and its losses, then the main families of methods: linear regression with [ridge](/ridge-regression/) and [lasso](/lasso-regression/) penalties, linear classifiers from the [perceptron](/perceptron/) to logistic regression, [nearest neighbors](/k-nearest-neighbors/), kernels and support vector machines, [decision trees](/decision-tree-learning/), and ensembles such as [random forests](/random-forest/) and [AdaBoost](/adaboost/). A group on PAC learning and the VC dimension gives the theory that ties them together.

**Chapter 25.2, Clustering**, groups unlabelled points so that points in the same group are similar. It covers [the clustering problem](/clustering-problem/), k-means and its seeding, [agglomerative clustering](/agglomerative-clustering/), whose single-linkage tree is the [minimum spanning tree](/minimum-spanning-tree/) built by Kruskal's algorithm, density-based methods such as [DBSCAN](/dbscan/), and [Gaussian mixture models](/gaussian-mixture-model/). [Kleinberg's impossibility theorem](/kleinberg-impossibility-theorem/) shows that no clustering function has three natural properties at once.

**Chapter 25.3, Dimensionality reduction**, maps points in many dimensions to few. [Principal component analysis](/principal-component-analysis/) keeps the directions of largest variance, found from the top eigenvectors of the [covariance matrix](/covariance-matrix/). [Random projection](/random-projection/) keeps distances with high probability. Nonlinear methods such as [t-SNE](/t-sne/) draw two-dimensional maps of the data, and the [curse of dimensionality](/curse-of-dimensionality/) explains why fewer dimensions help.

**Chapter 25.4, Neural network training**, covers the methods that fit the weights of a network. [Automatic differentiation](/automatic-differentiation/) and backpropagation compute the gradient. Then come [activation functions](/activation-function/), initialization, optimizers such as [AdamW](/adamw/), and the regularizers [dropout](/dropout/), [early stopping](/early-stopping/) and [batch normalization](/batch-normalization/). Many of these methods have no convergence guarantee, and the entries state what is proved, often for linear models, and test the rest on their update rules.

**Chapter 25.5, Probabilistic inference**, computes probabilities in a [graphical model](/graphical-model/), where missing edges stand for conditional independences. [Variable elimination](/variable-elimination/) and [belief propagation](/belief-propagation/) give exact marginals; belief propagation is exact on tree-shaped factor graphs. [Hidden Markov models](/hidden-markov-model/) get their own algorithms, among them the [Viterbi algorithm](/viterbi-algorithm/), and [expectation-maximization](/expectation-maximization/) fits models with hidden variables without ever lowering the likelihood.

**Chapter 25.6, Reinforcement learning**, learns to act from rewards. A [Markov decision process](/markov-decision-process/) is solved by value iteration or [policy iteration](/policy-iteration/) when the model is known. [Q-learning](/q-learning/) learns from samples alone and [converges with probability 1](/tabular-q-learning-convergence/) in the tabular case, under conditions on the step sizes and on how often each pair is visited. The [multi-armed bandit](/multi-armed-bandit/) isolates the trade between exploring and exploiting, and [UCB1](/ucb1/) has expected regret $O(K \log n / \Delta)$ after $n$ pulls of $K$ arms, where $\Delta$ is the smallest positive gap between the best mean and another.

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course, read these entries in this order. From Chapter 25.1: [loss functions](/loss-function/), [generalization error](/generalization-error/), [overfitting](/overfitting/), [cross-validation](/cross-validation/), ordinary least squares, ridge regression, the perceptron, logistic regression, k-nearest neighbors, decision tree learning, the support vector machine, PAC learning and AdaBoost. Then read k-means and k-means++ in Chapter 25.2, principal component analysis in Chapter 25.3, backpropagation in Chapter 25.4, the hidden Markov model and the forward algorithm in Chapter 25.5, and value iteration and Q-learning in Chapter 25.6. Do the exercises marked ◦ and • at the end of each of these chapters. That is a complete first unit on machine learning.

If you already know the basic methods, start with the opening page of each chapter. It names the groups of the chapter and the order to read them. The entries marked *Advanced* and *Research* are the place to go deeper; the theory groups of Chapter 25.1 come first among them, because the later chapters use their language.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained. Entries in other volumes are cited by number too, as [Hoeffding's inequality](/hoeffding-inequality/) in Volume 1 and [gradient descent](/gradient-descent/) in Volume 15 are; the number is printed with the link.

## What you need first

You should be able to read a short program, follow a proof by induction, and work with vectors and matrices. From Volume 1 you need [expected value](/expected-value/), [variance](/variance/), [conditional probability](/conditional-probability/) and Hoeffding's inequality. From Volume 12 you need the [least squares problem](/least-squares-problem/), the [spectral theorem](/spectral-theorem/) and the [singular value decomposition](/singular-value-decomposition/). From Volume 15 you need [convex functions](/convex-function/), gradient descent and [stochastic gradient descent](/stochastic-gradient-descent/). Chapter 25.5 uses [Markov chains](/markov-chain/) from Volume 17, and the trees of Chapter 25.1 use [Shannon entropy](/shannon-entropy/) from Volume 24.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
