## History

Supervised learning is older than the name. Adrien-Marie Legendre published the method of least squares in 1805 and Carl Friedrich Gauss in 1809, and Gauss later proved that it gives the linear unbiased estimator of least variance. Ronald Fisher's discriminant of 1936 classified by a linear rule fitted to data. Evelyn Fix and Joseph Hodges proposed the nearest neighbor rule in a report of 1951, and in 1967 Thomas Cover and Peter Hart proved that, with unlimited data, its error is at most twice the Bayes risk. Statistics supplied the models: Joseph Berkson named the logit in 1944, David Cox gave the regression analysis of binary data in 1958, and John Nelder and Robert Wedderburn joined linear, logistic and Poisson regression in the generalized linear model in 1972.

The other root is the study of learning machines. Frank Rosenblatt described the perceptron in 1958, and in 1962 Novikoff and Block proved that it makes at most $(R/\gamma)^2$ mistakes on separable data. Nick Littlestone's Winnow of 1988 showed that multiplicative updates make few mistakes when most features are irrelevant. Trees came from survey analysis: James Morgan and John Sonquist grew regression trees in 1963. Leo Breiman, Jerome Friedman, Richard Olshen and Charles Stone published *Classification and Regression Trees* in 1984, and J. Ross Quinlan published ID3 in 1986 and C4.5 in 1993. In 1976 Laurent Hyafil and Ronald Rivest had shown that finding a tree with the fewest expected tests is NP-complete, which is why all of these learners are greedy.

The theory of how much data a learner needs began with Vladimir Vapnik and Alexey Chervonenkis, whose paper of 1971 proved uniform convergence for classes of finite capacity, the quantity now called the VC dimension. Leslie Valiant's PAC model of 1984 added the computational side, and Anselm Blumer, Andrzej Ehrenfeucht, David Haussler and Manfred Warmuth tied learnability to the VC dimension in 1989. Agnostic learning (Haussler 1992; Kearns, Schapire and Sellie 1994), the statistical query model of Michael Kearns, and Rademacher complexity (Koltchinskii 2001; Bartlett and Mendelson 2002) refined the picture. Jorma Rissanen's minimum description length principle of 1978 gave a parallel account in terms of codes.

Regularization and kernels came together in the 1990s. Arthur Hoerl and Robert Kennard named ridge regression in 1970, after Andrey Tikhonov had used the same penalty for ill-posed problems; Robert Tibshirani proposed the lasso in 1996. Bernhard Boser, Isabelle Guyon and Vapnik introduced the kernel support vector machine in 1992, and Corinna Cortes and Vapnik gave the soft-margin form in 1995. The theorems behind kernels are older: Mercer's of 1909, and the representer theorem of George Kimeldorf and Grace Wahba of 1971. John Platt's sequential minimal optimization of 1998 made the training practical.

Ensembles grew out of a question in the PAC model. Kearns and Valiant asked whether a learner a little better than chance could be made accurate; Robert Schapire answered yes in 1990. Yoav Freund and Schapire presented AdaBoost in 1995. Breiman introduced bagging in 1996 and random forests in 2001, David Wolpert introduced stacking in 1992, and Friedman cast boosting as gradient descent in function space in 2001. Tianqi Chen and Carlos Guestrin added second-order steps and fast split finding in XGBoost (2016).

## Further reading

- Shai Shalev-Shwartz and Shai Ben-David, *Understanding Machine Learning: From Theory to Algorithms*, Cambridge University Press (2014). The PAC model, the VC dimension and the main learners, with complete proofs.
- Trevor Hastie, Robert Tibshirani and Jerome Friedman, *The Elements of Statistical Learning*, second edition, Springer (2009). The statistical view of linear models, regularization, trees, boosting and random forests.
- Christopher M. Bishop, *Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning*, Springer (2006). Probabilistic models, generative and discriminative classifiers, and kernel methods.
- Luc Devroye, László Györfi and Gábor Lugosi, *A Probabilistic Theory of Pattern Recognition*, Springer (1996). The careful treatment of the Bayes risk, consistency and nearest neighbor rules.
- Robert E. Schapire and Yoav Freund, *Boosting: Foundations and Algorithms*, MIT Press (2012). Boosting from the weak learning question to margins and the game-theoretic view.
- Tom M. Mitchell, *Machine Learning*, McGraw-Hill (1997). A short first textbook, good on decision trees, concept learning and the meaning of overfitting.
