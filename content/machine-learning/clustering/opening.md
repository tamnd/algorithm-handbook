Clustering splits a set of points into groups with no labels to learn from. Take the six numbers 0, 1, 2, 8, 9 and 20 and ask for two groups. The k-means cost, the sum of squared distances to the group means, is least for $\{0, 1, 2, 8, 9\}$ and $\{20\}$, with cost 70, against $272/3$ for $\{0, 1, 2\}$ and $\{8, 9, 20\}$. The k-median cost, the sum of plain distances to the group medians, prefers the second split, 14 against 16. The largest gap, 11, also cuts off 20, and a density method may call 20 noise and keep one group. Each answer is right for its own objective. Clustering is used to segment customers, group documents and genes, compress colors, and as a first look at any new data set.

The chapter keeps three things apart: the objective, the algorithm and the kind of guarantee. Most objectives are NP-hard. Optimal k-means is NP-hard already for two clusters in general dimension, and in a general metric no polynomial algorithm comes within $2 - \varepsilon$ of the best k-center radius unless P = NP. So most algorithms here are heuristics, and the entries describe them by what they do keep. Lloyd's algorithm never raises the k-means cost, but it stops at a local optimum that can be arbitrarily bad. The k-means++ seeding is within a factor $8(\ln k + 2)$ of the optimum in expectation, not on every run. Single linkage is exact for the spacing objective on every input. DBSCAN computes the clusters of its own definition, up to the choice for a border point near two clusters, and mean shift climbs a density estimate with no global guarantee. Kleinberg's theorem says that no clustering function has three natural properties at once, so every method gives one up.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these fourteen entries in order: the clustering problem, the k-means problem, the k-means algorithm, k-means++, the k-median problem, partitioning around medoids, the Gaussian mixture model, the dendrogram, agglomerative clustering, single linkage, Ward's method, DBSCAN, spectral clustering and the adjusted Rand index. They give the main objectives, the standard heuristic with its seeding, one method from each of the hierarchical, density and spectral families, and one honest way to score a result against known classes.

Skip at first the theorems of group 1 after the optimality of maximum spacing, the variants of group 2 after partitioning around medoids, and most of groups 8 and 9. Skip also the research entries: Dasgupta's cost, the pivot algorithm and streaming k-median. Group 4 matters as soon as you have to pick $k$ for real data, and group 9 when the data does not fit in memory.

## Before you start

You need the [Euclidean distance](/euclidean-distance/) and the axioms of a [metric space](/metric-space/), and the [partitions of a set](/set-partitions/), which are the outputs of every flat method. Single linkage is the [minimum spanning tree](/minimum-spanning-tree/) built by [Kruskal's algorithm](/kruskal/), the agglomerative methods use a [priority queue](/priority-queue/), and DBSCAN uses [fixed-radius near neighbor](/fixed-radius-near-neighbors/) queries and [connected components](/connected-components/). The soft and density methods use the [probability density function](/probability-density-function/), the [covariance matrix](/covariance-matrix/) and [maximum likelihood estimation](/maximum-likelihood-estimation/). Spectral methods need the [Laplacian matrix](/laplacian-matrix/) of a graph and its [eigenvectors](/eigenvector/). The guarantees use [expected value](/expected-value/), [linearity of expectation](/linearity-of-expectation/) and [weighted sampling](/weighted-sampling/), the hardness results use [NP-hardness](/np-hard/), and PAM is a [local search](/local-search/). Normalized mutual information is built from [Shannon entropy](/shannon-entropy/), and kernel k-means from the [kernel trick](/kernel-trick/).
