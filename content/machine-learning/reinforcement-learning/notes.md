## History

The planning half of the subject comes from operations research in the 1950s. Bellman's book *Dynamic Programming* (1957) set out the principle of optimality and the recursion that now carries his name, and with it value iteration. Shapley had already studied discounted stochastic games in 1953. Howard's *Dynamic Programming and Markov Processes* (1960) introduced policy iteration. Manne (1960) and d'Epenoux (1963) wrote the problem as a linear program, and Puterman and Shin (1978) proposed modified policy iteration, between the two classical methods. The complexity questions took longer. Papadimitriou and Tsitsiklis (1987) proved that planning in an MDP is P-complete, and Ye (2011) showed that policy iteration is strongly polynomial for a fixed discount.

Bandits have their own line. Thompson (1933) asked how to share patients between two treatments and proposed to give each with the probability that it is the better one. Robbins (1952) posed the sequential design problem in its modern form. Gittins and Jones (1974) found the index that solves the discounted Bayesian problem, and Lai and Robbins (1985) proved that every consistent policy has regret of order $\log n$ with a constant set by relative entropy. Auer, Cesa-Bianchi and Fischer (2002) gave UCB1 and the decaying ε-greedy rule with finite-time bounds, and Auer, Cesa-Bianchi, Freund and Schapire gave EXP3 for rewards chosen by an adversary. Thompson's rule had no regret proof until Agrawal and Goyal (2012) and Kaufmann, Korda and Munos (2012).

Learning from experience began in artificial intelligence. Samuel's checkers player of 1959 already adjusted an evaluation function toward later estimates. Barto, Sutton and Anderson (1983) built the first actor-critic to balance a pole on a cart, and Sutton (1988) defined temporal-difference learning and TD(λ). Watkins introduced Q-learning in his 1989 thesis, and Watkins and Dayan (1992) proved its convergence. Rummery and Niranjan (1994) proposed the on-policy method that Sutton later called SARSA. Williams (1992) gave REINFORCE, and Sutton (1990) joined learning and planning in Dyna. The theory of these methods came from stochastic approximation, which goes back to Robbins and Monro (1951): Jaakkola, Jordan and Singh (1994) and Tsitsiklis (1994) proved convergence for the tabular methods, and Tsitsiklis and Van Roy (1997) for linear TD on-policy. Baird's counterexample (1995) showed that off-policy TD with linear features can diverge, and gradient TD methods (Sutton, Szepesvári and Maei, from 2008) were the answer.

Neural networks changed the scale of the problems. Mnih and coauthors at DeepMind trained DQN on Atari games from pixels (2013, and in *Nature* in 2015), and van Hasselt's Double Q-learning (2010) led to Double DQN. Policy gradient methods followed: natural gradients (Kakade 2001), TRPO (Schulman et al. 2015), A3C (Mnih et al. 2016), PPO (Schulman et al. 2017), and the deterministic and maximum-entropy methods DDPG, TD3 and soft actor-critic. Off-policy corrections such as Retrace (Munos et al. 2016) and V-trace (Espeholt et al. 2018), offline methods such as conservative Q-learning (Kumar et al. 2020), and MuZero (Schrittwieser et al.), which plans with a learned model, are among the recent results.

## Further reading

- Richard S. Sutton and Andrew G. Barto, *Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction*, second edition, MIT Press, 2018. The standard text, from bandits and tabular methods to function approximation and policy gradients, with full history notes at the end of each chapter.
- Martin L. Puterman, *Markov Decision Processes: Discrete Stochastic Dynamic Programming*, Wiley, 1994. The reference for planning: discounted, total and average reward criteria, value and policy iteration, and linear programming, with proofs.
- Tor Lattimore and Csaba Szepesvári, *Bandit Algorithms*, Cambridge University Press, 2020. A complete account of stochastic and adversarial bandits, with the lower bounds.
- Dimitri P. Bertsekas and John N. Tsitsiklis, *Neuro-Dynamic Programming*, Athena Scientific, 1996. The convergence theory of TD and Q-learning through stochastic approximation, and of approximate dynamic programming.
- Csaba Szepesvári, *Algorithms for Reinforcement Learning*, Morgan and Claypool, 2010. A short book that states the main algorithms and their guarantees precisely.
