Take a network that reads a $28 \times 28$ image, with hidden layers of 512 and 256 units and 10 outputs. It has 535,040 weights, and one forward pass on one image costs about 535,040 multiply-adds. Training needs the derivative of the loss with respect to every weight. The direct way perturbs one weight at a time and runs the network again, so it needs 535,041 forward passes, about $2.9 \times 10^{11}$ multiply-adds per image. [Backpropagation](/backpropagation/) returns all 535,040 partial derivatives exactly, in at most $3P + O(N)$ multiply-adds for $P$ weights and $N$ units, here about 1.6 million. The ratio between the cost of the gradient and the cost of the function is a constant, whatever the size of the network, and that constant is what makes networks with billions of weights trainable. A training step is then a forward pass, a backward pass and an update, repeated millions of times.

The chapter keeps two kinds of result apart. Backpropagation, automatic differentiation and checkpointing are exact algorithms with cost bounds that hold for every network and every input. Most of the rest of the training loop, the initialization, the normalization layers, the adaptive optimizers and their schedules, the regularizers, are heuristics: they work well in practice, and for deep networks they have no general convergence or generalization guarantee. Where an entry does prove something, the proof names its model. Dropout on the inputs of a linear model is ridge regression with a data-dependent penalty, in expectation over the masks. AMSGrad has sublinear regret on online convex problems. At infinite width, gradient flow on squared loss is kernel regression. None of these carries over to a finite deep network as a theorem, and the pages here state each one with its model.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these fourteen entries in order: the activation function, the ReLU, the computational graph, automatic differentiation, backpropagation, gradient checking, the vanishing gradient problem, Xavier initialization, He initialization, batch normalization, the learning rate schedule, weight decay, AdamW and dropout. They give the model, the exact algorithm for its gradient and a test of that algorithm, the reason deep networks are hard to train, and the standard fixes that every training loop uses.

Skip at first the large-batch and curvature methods of group 6, the losses of group 8, and the other research entries: spectral normalization, the lottery ticket hypothesis, double descent, the neural tangent kernel and neural architecture search. Group 6 matters when one batch no longer fits on one device; group 9 matters when you ask why very large networks generalize at all.

## Before you start

You need the [gradient](/gradient/), [gradient descent](/gradient-descent/) and [stochastic gradient descent](/stochastic-gradient-descent/), and you should know the [momentum method](/momentum-method/), [RMSProp](/rmsprop/) and [Adam](/adam/), which the optimizers here modify. The model and the losses come from supervised learning: the [perceptron](/perceptron/), [softmax regression](/softmax-regression/), the [cross-entropy loss](/cross-entropy-loss/), [empirical risk minimization](/empirical-risk-minimization/), [overfitting](/overfitting/), [regularization](/regularization/), [ridge regression](/ridge-regression/) and [cross-validation](/cross-validation/). Backpropagation runs on a [directed acyclic graph](/directed-acyclic-graph/) in [topological order](/topological-sort/), and gradient checking uses [finite differences](/finite-differences/). The initialization and dropout entries use [expected value](/expected-value/), [variance](/variance/) and [linearity of expectation](/linearity-of-expectation/). The gradient problems use the [spectral radius](/spectral-radius/) and [matrix norms](/matrix-norms/); spectral normalization adds the [singular value decomposition](/singular-value-decomposition/) and [power iteration](/power-iteration/). Mixed precision needs [IEEE 754](/ieee-754/) arithmetic. The later entries draw on the [Fisher information matrix](/fisher-information-matrix/) and the [Kronecker product](/kronecker-product/) for K-FAC, [Gibbs sampling](/gibbs-sampling/) and [maximum likelihood estimation](/maximum-likelihood-estimation/) for contrastive divergence, [kernel ridge regression](/kernel-ridge-regression/) and the [pseudoinverse](/pseudoinverse/) for the theory of wide networks, and [black-box optimization](/black-box-optimization/) for the search over settings.
