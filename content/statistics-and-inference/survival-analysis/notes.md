## History

Actuaries estimated survival as a product of conditional survival fractions over age intervals long before there was a theory of censored data. Major Greenwood, in a 1926 report for the British Ministry of Health on the natural duration of cancer, gave the variance of such a life-table estimate by treating the interval fractions as independent binomial proportions. Edward Kaplan and Paul Meier wrote separate papers on estimation from incomplete observations and published them as one paper in the Journal of the American Statistical Association in 1958, with the product-limit estimate, its maximum likelihood property and the same variance formula. Bradley Efron (1967) described the estimate as redistributing the mass of each censored time to the right.

Testing came next. Gehan (1965) extended the Wilcoxon test to censored samples, and Nathan Mantel (1966) combined the $2 \times 2$ tables at the event times by the Mantel-Haenszel method. Richard and Julian Peto (1972) derived the same statistic as an asymptotically efficient rank invariant test and named it the logrank test. In the same year David Cox's paper "Regression models and life-tables" proposed the proportional hazards model and the likelihood that does not involve the baseline hazard, and Cox (1975) justified it as a partial likelihood. Breslow (1974) gave the estimate of the baseline hazard and his rule for ties, Efron (1977) a closer rule for ties, and Schoenfeld (1982) the residuals that Grambsch and Therneau (1994) turned into a test of proportional hazards.

Wayne Nelson proposed the cumulative hazard estimate for hazard plots of failure data in 1969 and 1972. Odd Aalen (1978) placed it in the theory of counting processes with a multiplicative intensity, where estimators are martingale transforms and martingale central limit theorems give their large-sample laws. Aalen and Søren Johansen (1978) used the same tools for the transition matrix of a Markov process, and Andersen and Gill (1982) gave the counting process proof for the Cox model. Andersen, Borgan, Gill and Keiding (1993) collected this work in one book.

Tsiatis (1975) showed that the dependence between latent failure times cannot be identified from the first failure and its cause, which moved attention to cause-specific hazards and cumulative incidence. Gray (1988) gave tests for cumulative incidence curves, and Fine and Gray (1999) a proportional hazards model for the subdistribution hazard. Vaupel, Manton and Stallard (1979) introduced frailty to explain how heterogeneity bends population mortality curves, and Clayton (1978) proposed the bivariate model now read as shared gamma frailty. For interval-censored data, Peto (1973) defined the nonparametric maximum likelihood estimate and Turnbull (1976) gave the innermost intervals and the self-consistency algorithm, one year before the EM algorithm was described in general.

## Further reading

- John D. Kalbfleisch and Ross L. Prentice, *The Statistical Analysis of Failure Time Data*, 2nd edition, Wiley, 2002. The likelihood theory of parametric models, the Cox model and competing risks.
- John P. Klein and Melvin L. Moeschberger, *Survival Analysis: Techniques for Censored and Truncated Data*, 2nd edition, Springer, 2003. Worked examples on real data for most methods of the chapter, including truncation and interval censoring.
- Per Kragh Andersen, Ørnulf Borgan, Richard D. Gill and Niels Keiding, *Statistical Models Based on Counting Processes*, Springer, 1993. The martingale proofs behind Nelson-Aalen, Kaplan-Meier, the log-rank test, the Cox model and Aalen-Johansen.
- Terry M. Therneau and Patricia M. Grambsch, *Modeling Survival Data: Extending the Cox Model*, Springer, 2000. Fitting and checking Cox models, with residuals, frailty and clustered data.
- H. Putter, M. Fiocco and R. B. Geskus, "Tutorial in biostatistics: competing risks and multi-state models", *Statistics in Medicine* 26, 2007. A short guide to cumulative incidence, the Fine-Gray model and multistate models.
