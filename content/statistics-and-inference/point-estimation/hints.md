1. $\tilde p$ has mean $(np + 1)/(n + 2)$, so its bias is $(1 - 2p)/(n + 2)$, which is $1/15$ at $p = 0.1$ and $0$ at $p = 1/2$. Its variance is $np(1 - p)/(n + 2)^2$. At $p = 0.1$ the mean squared errors are $9/1000 = 0.0090$ for $\hat p$ and $77/7200 \approx 0.0107$ for $\tilde p$, so $\hat p$ is better. At $p = 1/2$ they are $1/40 = 0.025$ and $5/288 \approx 0.0174$, so $\tilde p$ is better. Neither estimator is better at every $p$.

3. $P(\max_i X_i < 0.95) = 0.95^{50} \approx 0.0769$, and the maximum is never above $1$; the probability tends to $0$ as $n$ grows. $2X_1$ is uniform on $(0, 2)$, so $P(|2X_1 - 1| \le 0.05) = 0.05$ and the requested probability is $0.95$ for every $n$, since the estimator uses only one observation. $2X_1$ is unbiased and not consistent; the maximum is biased and consistent.

4. The triples are $(2, 3, 2)$ and $(3, 19/3, 62/3)$. With $\delta = 19/3 - 3 = 10/3$, the merged mean is $3 + \delta \cdot 3/5 = 5$ and $M = 2 + 62/3 + \delta^2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3/5 = 2 + 62/3 + 40/3 = 36$. The five values $(2, 4, 4, 5, 10)$ have deviations $-3, -1, -1, 0, 5$, with squares summing to $36$, so $s^2 = 9$. Adding the two values of $M$ gives only $68/3$; the missing $40/3$ is the spread between the two part means.

8. The complete-case mean is $(2 + 4 + 6 + 10)/4 = 5.5$. Imputing the stratum means $4$ and $10$ gives $(4 \cdot 4 + 4 \cdot 10)/8 = 7$. This is justified under MAR given $z$: within each value of $z$, the missing values have the same law as the observed ones. The complete-case mean underweights the stratum $z = 1$, which has more missing values. The three cases are (a) MCAR, (b) MAR given age and (c) MNAR.

10. The risk of $\tilde p$ is $(np(1 - p) + (1 - 2p)^2)/(n + 2)^2$, and that of $\hat p$ is $p(1 - p)/n$. With $q = p(1 - p)$ they are equal when $q = 5/42$, that is, at $p \approx 0.1381$ and $p \approx 0.8619$, and $\tilde p$ is better between these points. The maximum risks are $1/40$ and $5/288$, both at $p = 1/2$. The third estimator has risk $1/(4(\sqrt n + 1)^2) \approx 0.01443$ at every $p$, smaller than both maxima, so neither of the first two is minimax.

11. The information per observation is $1/\lambda^2$, so the bound is $\lambda^2/5$. $T$ has the Gamma$(5, \lambda)$ law, with $E[1/T] = \lambda/4$ and $E[1/T^2] = \lambda^2/12$. So $4/T$ is unbiased with variance $16\lambda^2/12 - \lambda^2 = \lambda^2/3$, which is $5/3$ times the bound; it is still the UMVUE, since $T$ is complete. For $c/T$ the mean squared error is $\lambda^2(c^2/12 - c/2 + 1)$: $7\lambda^2/12$ for $c = 5$, and the minimum $\lambda^2/4$ at $c = 3 = n - 2$.

12. $P(X_1 > 1 \mid T = t) = P(X_1/T > 1/t) = (1 - 1/t)^{n - 1}$ for $t > 1$, and $0$ for $t \le 1$. With $n = 5$ and $T = 6$ this is $(5/6)^4 \approx 0.4823$, against $e^{-5/6} \approx 0.4346$. The plug-in estimate is biased; the conditional one is the UMVUE, since $T$ is complete and sufficient.

20. The scoring step is $\theta \leftarrow \theta + 2U(\theta)/n$. 22. $X_1 - \bar X$ is normal with variance $\sigma^2(1 - 1/n)$ and independent of $\bar X$, so $P(X_1 \le c \mid \bar X = \bar x) = \Phi((c - \bar x)/(\sigma\sqrt{1 - 1/n}))$. Since $\bar X$ is complete and sufficient, this is the UMVUE. For the data it is $\Phi(0.5/\sqrt{0.75}) = \Phi(0.5774) \approx 0.7181$, against $\Phi(0.5) \approx 0.6915$ for the plug-in.

23. For the normal law, $S = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma^2 & \sigma\sqrt{2/\pi} \\ \sigma\sqrt{2/\pi} & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $G = -(1, \sqrt{2/\pi}/\sigma)^\top$, using $E|X - \mu| = \sigma\sqrt{2/\pi}$ and $2f(\mu)$ for the derivative of the sign condition. Then $G$ is $-1/\sigma^2$ times the first column of $S$, so $S^{-1}G = -e_1/\sigma^2$ and the variance is $\sigma^2/n$, that of the mean alone. For the Laplace law, $S = \begin{pmatrix} 2b^2 & b \\ b & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $G = -(1, 1/b)^\top$, which is $-1/b$ times the second column, so the variance is $b^2/n$, that of the median alone.

24. Each pair has $\hat\mu_i = (X_{i1} + X_{i2})/2$, and the MLE is $\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{4n}\sum D_i^2$. Since $E[D_i^2] = 2\sigma^2$, it converges to $\sigma^2/2$. The estimator $\tilde\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{2n}\sum D_i^2$ is unbiased and consistent, with $\sqrt n(\tilde\sigma^2 - \sigma^2) \to N(0, 2\sigma^4)$, since $D_i^2/(2\sigma^2)$ is $\chi^2_1$. The theorem assumes a fixed number of parameters; here the number grows with $n$, and each $\mu_i$ is estimated from only two observations.

25. Write the deviations of the part $a$ from the merged mean as $(x - \bar x_a) - n_b\delta/n$, and those of $b$ as $(x - \bar x_b) + n_a\delta/n$. Expanding the cubes, the linear terms vanish within each part, which gives $M_3 = M_{3a} + M_{3b} + \delta^3 n_a n_b (n_a - n_b)/n^2 + 3\delta(n_a M_{2b} - n_b M_{2a})/n$. The parts have $(n, \bar x, M_2, M_3) = (2, 5, 8, 0)$ and $(3, 10, 26, 36)$, with $\delta = 5$. The terms are $0 + 36 - 30 + 84 = 90$, and the union $(3, 7, 7, 9, 14)$ has deviations $-5, -1, -1, 1, 6$, whose cubes sum to $90$.

26. Stein's lemma gives $E[(X - \theta)^\top X/\|X\|^2] = (p - 2)E[1/\|X\|^2]$. So the risk is $p - (2c(p - 2) - c^2)\,E[1/\|X\|^2]$. It is below $p$ at every $\theta$ exactly when $0 < c < 2(p - 2)$, and it is smallest at $c = p - 2$, which is the James-Stein estimator. At $\theta = 0$, $\|X\|^2$ is $\chi^2_p$ and $E[1/\|X\|^2] = 1/(p - 2) = 1/3$. The risk is $5 - 5/3 = 10/3$ for $c = 1$ and $5 - 9/3 = 2$ for $c = 3$.
