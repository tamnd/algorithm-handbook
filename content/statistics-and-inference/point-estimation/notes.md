## History

Karl Pearson fitted distributions by matching moments in the 1890s. Fisher's paper of 1922, "On the mathematical foundations of theoretical statistics", set out the vocabulary still in use: consistency, efficiency, sufficiency and the method of maximum likelihood. In 1925 he defined the information in a sample and claimed that maximum likelihood is efficient in large samples. Neyman proved the factorization criterion for sufficiency in 1935, and Halmos and Savage gave it for every dominated family in 1949. Iterative fitting with expected weights, the method now called Fisher scoring, spread through bioassay after Bliss's probit analysis of 1935.

The theory of unbiased estimation took shape in the 1940s. Rao in 1945 and Cramér in his book of 1946 proved the information bound. Rao's paper of 1945 also contained the conditioning argument that Blackwell published independently in 1947, and Lehmann and Scheffé added completeness in 1950, which makes the improved estimator unique. Wald recast estimation as a choice of decision rule under a loss in *Statistical Decision Functions* (1950). Kullback and Leibler defined their divergence in 1951. The limits of the large-sample theory were found at the same time: Neyman and Scott showed in 1948 that the MLE can be inconsistent when the number of parameters grows, and Hodges's superefficient estimator, published by Le Cam in 1953, showed that the asymptotic variance of the MLE is not a lower bound at every point.

Stein showed in 1956 that the sample mean of a normal vector is inadmissible in three or more dimensions, and James and Stein gave the explicit shrinkage estimator in 1961. Efron and Morris interpreted it as an empirical Bayes rule in a series of papers in the 1970s. Huber introduced M-estimators for location in 1964, which led to the sandwich covariance for misspecified models. Hansen's generalized method of moments of 1982 came from econometrics, and Owen introduced empirical likelihood in 1988.

Horvitz and Thompson published their estimator for sampling without replacement in 1952, and Basu's circus example of 1971 showed how badly it can behave when the inclusion probabilities are unrelated to the values. Rubin defined the missing-data mechanisms in 1976 and gave multiple imputation in book form in 1987; the chained-equations version was described by van Buuren and coauthors in 1999. On the computing side, Welford published his one-pass update in 1962, Chan, Golub and LeVeque gave the formula for merging two samples in a Stanford report of 1979, and compared the known algorithms in 1983.

## Further reading

- George Casella and Roger L. Berger, *Statistical Inference*, 2nd edition, Duxbury, 2002. The standard first course: sufficiency, unbiased estimation, the Cramér-Rao bound and the large-sample properties of the MLE, with many worked examples.
- E. L. Lehmann and George Casella, *Theory of Point Estimation*, 2nd edition, Springer, 1998. The full theory of unbiasedness, equivariance, minimaxity, admissibility and shrinkage.
- A. W. van der Vaart, *Asymptotic Statistics*, Cambridge University Press, 1998. Rigorous proofs of the delta method, the asymptotics of M- and Z-estimators, and superefficiency.
- Art B. Owen, *Empirical Likelihood*, Chapman and Hall/CRC, 2001. The construction, the chi-squared calibration and its extensions to estimating equations.
- Roderick J. A. Little and Donald B. Rubin, *Statistical Analysis with Missing Data*, 3rd edition, Wiley, 2019. The mechanisms, likelihood methods under MAR, and multiple imputation with Rubin's rules.
- Tony F. Chan, Gene H. Golub and Randall J. LeVeque, "Algorithms for computing the sample variance: analysis and recommendations", *The American Statistician* 37 (1983), 242–247. Error bounds for the one-pass, two-pass and pairwise algorithms.
