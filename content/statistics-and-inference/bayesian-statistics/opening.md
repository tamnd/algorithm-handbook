A coin lands heads 15 times in 20 tosses. With a uniform prior on the chance of heads $p$, [Bayes' theorem](/bayes-theorem/) gives the posterior Beta$(16, 6)$. From that one distribution come several answers. The 95% [credible interval](/credible-interval/) for $p$ is $[0.528, 0.887]$, and the posterior probability that $p > 1/2$ is $0.987$. The probability that the next toss is a head is $16/22 = 8/11$. The question "is the coin fair?" needs a second model, one in which $p$ is exactly $1/2$. The [Bayes factor](/bayes-factor/) of the uniform model against the fair one is $65536/20349 \approx 3.22$, so with even prior odds the fair coin keeps posterior probability $0.237$. The two-sided [p-value](/p-value/) of the same data is $0.041$.

These numbers answer different questions, and the chapter keeps them apart. The posterior probabilities are statements about $p$ given the data and the prior. The p-value is a statement about repeated samples when $p = 1/2$, and it is not the probability that the coin is fair. A credible interval is not a [confidence interval](/confidence-interval/): averaged over the prior its coverage is exactly 95%, but at a fixed $p$ it can be far lower or higher.

Bayesian statistics is a short list of operations on one joint distribution of parameters and data: condition on the data, integrate out what is not of interest, and compare models by how well they predicted what was seen. The entries of this chapter say how to choose the prior, how to summarize and check the posterior, and how to compute it when no formula exists. Much of the computing rests on Markov chain Monte Carlo from Volume 17, which converges only in the limit, and whose diagnostics can show a failure but cannot prove success. The chapter therefore also has entries that check a computation and entries that check a model.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups, read in order.

1. **Summaries of the posterior.** The credible interval and the [highest posterior density region](/highest-posterior-density-interval/), the [Bayes estimator](/bayes-estimator/) that a loss function picks, and the [posterior predictive distribution](/posterior-predictive-distribution/) of new data.
2. **Priors, and when they stop mattering.** [Improper priors](/improper-prior/) and the checks they need, the [Jeffreys prior](/jeffreys-prior/) and the [reference prior](/reference-prior/), and the [Bernstein-von Mises theorem](/bernstein-von-mises-theorem/), under whose conditions the prior washes out.
3. **Models with structure.** [Bayesian linear regression](/bayesian-linear-regression/) in closed form, [de Finetti's theorem](/de-finetti-theorem/), the [hierarchical model](/hierarchical-bayesian-model/) and [empirical Bayes](/empirical-bayes/), where groups share strength.
4. **Testing and comparing models.** The Bayes factor, the [Savage-Dickey density ratio](/savage-dickey-density-ratio/), the [Lindley paradox](/lindley-paradox/) and [Bayesian model averaging](/bayesian-model-averaging/).
5. **Sparse priors.** The [spike-and-slab prior](/spike-and-slab-prior/) and the [horseshoe prior](/horseshoe-prior/).
6. **Computing marginal likelihoods and marginals.** The [Laplace approximation](/laplace-approximation/), [INLA](/integrated-nested-laplace-approximation/), [bridge sampling](/bridge-sampling/) and [nested sampling](/nested-sampling/).
7. **Inference without a likelihood.** [Approximate Bayesian computation](/approximate-bayesian-computation/), the [ABC rejection algorithm](/abc-rejection-sampling/) and [ABC sequential Monte Carlo](/abc-sequential-monte-carlo/).
8. **Checking models and computations.** The [posterior predictive check](/posterior-predictive-check/), [WAIC](/widely-applicable-information-criterion/), [PSIS-LOO](/pareto-smoothed-importance-sampling-loo/) and [simulation-based calibration](/simulation-based-calibration/).

## A first reading

Take these twelve entries in order: the credible interval, the Bayes estimator, the posterior predictive distribution, the improper prior, the Jeffreys prior, Bayesian linear regression, the hierarchical Bayesian model, empirical Bayes, the Bayes factor, the Lindley paradox, the Laplace approximation and the posterior predictive check. They give the main summaries of a posterior, the two standard ways to choose a prior, the models in closed form, the comparison of models and its conflict with significance tests, one way to compute, and one way to check.

Leave for later the reference prior, the sparse priors of group 5, and the samplers for marginal likelihoods and simulators in groups 6 and 7. They assume the first reading and need more of the computing background.
