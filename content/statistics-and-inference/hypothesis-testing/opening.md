A new treatment is given to 20 patients and 15 of them improve. If the treatment does nothing and each patient improves with probability $1/2$, how surprising is that? The exact [binomial test](/binomial-test/) answers with a two-sided p-value of $2 \cdot 5425/262144 = 0.0414$. The three large-sample tests of the same hypothesis give three other numbers: 0.0222 for the [likelihood ratio test](/likelihood-ratio-test/), 0.0098 for the [Wald test](/wald-test/) and 0.0253 for the [score test](/score-test/). All four are correct answers to slightly different questions, and only the first one has a size that is guaranteed for 20 patients. Much of this chapter is about knowing which guarantee a method gives: exact in finite samples, only in the limit, or only under an assumption that has to be checked.

The second example is a screen. A laboratory measures 10,000 genes and tests each one at level 0.05. If no gene is affected, 500 of them are still declared significant on average. The remedy is to choose an error rate for the whole family, such as the chance of any false rejection or the expected share of false rejections, and a procedure that controls it under the dependence that is really there.

Two warnings run through the chapter. A [p-value](/p-value/) is computed assuming the null is true, so it is not the probability that the null is true. A 95% [confidence interval](/confidence-interval/) covers the true value in 95% of repeated samples. That is a statement about the procedure. It does not say that the parameter lies in this particular interval with probability 0.95, which would be a Bayesian [credible interval](/credible-interval/).

## What the chapter covers

1. **Tests, errors and p-values.** The [hypothesis test](/statistical-hypothesis-test/), its [type I and type II errors](/type-i-and-type-ii-errors/), the p-value, [power](/statistical-power/) and the [effect size](/effect-size/).
2. **Confidence sets.** The confidence interval, the [pivotal quantity](/pivotal-quantity/) that gives exact intervals, and the [duality](/test-interval-duality/) between tests and confidence sets.

4. **Counts and proportions.** The binomial test, the [Clopper-Pearson](/clopper-pearson-interval/) and [Wilson](/wilson-score-interval/) intervals, [McNemar's test](/mcnemar-test/), [Fisher's exact test](/fisher-exact-test/) and the [chi-squared tests](/chi-squared-goodness-of-fit-test/) for many cells.
5. **Optimal tests.** The [Neyman-Pearson lemma](/neyman-pearson-lemma/), [uniformly most powerful tests](/uniformly-most-powerful-test/) and the [Karlin-Rubin theorem](/karlin-rubin-theorem/) for one-sided problems.
6. **Large-sample likelihood tests.** The likelihood ratio, Wald and score tests, and [Wilks' theorem](/wilks-theorem/), which gives their common $\chi^2_r$ limit.

## A first reading

Read the basic definitions in order: the hypothesis test, type I and type II errors, the p-value, power and the confidence interval. Then read the z-test, the t-test and the t interval, and test-interval duality to see that tests and intervals are one idea. For counts, read the binomial test and the Wilson interval. For many tests, read the multiple comparisons problem, the family-wise error rate, Holm's method, the false discovery rate and the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. That is fifteen entries.

On a first pass you can skip the optimality theory (Neyman-Pearson, UMP tests, Karlin-Rubin), the proofs behind the large-sample tests, Fieller's theorem, the Westfall-Young procedure, the local fdr, knockoffs and e-variables. Come back to the likelihood tests once you know [maximum likelihood estimation](/maximum-likelihood-estimation/).
