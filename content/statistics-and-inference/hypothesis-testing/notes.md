## History

The first significance test is usually traced to John Arbuthnot, who noted in 1710 that male christenings outnumbered female ones in London in each of 82 years and argued that chance would give this with probability $2^{-82}$. Karl Pearson gave the chi-squared test of goodness of fit in 1900. William Sealy Gosset, writing as Student, derived the law of the t statistic for small normal samples in "The probable error of a mean" in 1908. Fisher corrected the degrees of freedom of the chi-squared test for contingency tables in 1922, and his book Statistical Methods for Research Workers of 1925 made p-values and the level 0.05 part of everyday practice. Its fourth edition of 1932 added the method for combining independent p-values, and the exact test for $2 \times 2$ tables appeared in its 1934 edition and in The Design of Experiments of 1935.

Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson turned testing into a decision problem with two kinds of error. Their paper of 1928 introduced the likelihood ratio criterion, and their paper of 1933, "On the problem of the most efficient tests of statistical hypotheses", proved the lemma that bears their names. Neyman set out the theory of confidence intervals in 1937. Edwin Wilson had already given the score interval for a proportion in 1927, and Clopper and Pearson published the exact binomial interval in 1934. Karlin and Rubin showed in 1956 that families with a monotone likelihood ratio have a best one-sided test for every alternative at once.

The large-sample theory came next. Wilks proved in 1938 that twice the log likelihood ratio has a chi-squared limit under the null, Wald gave the test that bears his name in 1943, and Rao gave the score test in 1948. Several of the classical small-sample tests date from the same years: McNemar's test for paired proportions in 1947, Welch's test for unequal variances in 1947, Fieller's treatment of ratios in 1954 and the Shapiro-Wilk test of normality in 1965.

Multiple testing grew from Bonferroni's inequalities of 1936, which Dunn applied to confidence intervals in 1961; Šidák gave the product form in 1967. Marcus, Peritz and Gabriel stated the closed testing principle in 1976, and Holm published his step-down method in 1979. Simes gave his global test in 1986, and Hochberg built the step-up procedure on it in 1988. Westfall and Young's book of 1993 made resampling-based adjustment practical.

Benjamini and Hochberg defined the false discovery rate and proved that the step-up procedure controls it under independence in 1995. Benjamini and Yekutieli proved control under positive regression dependence and gave the correction for arbitrary dependence in 2001. Efron, Tibshirani, Storey and Tusher introduced the local false discovery rate in 2001, and Storey's paper of 2002 estimated the share of true nulls. Barber and Candès gave the knockoff filter in 2015, and Candès, Fan, Janson and Lv extended it to model-X knockoffs in 2018. Vovk and Wang studied e-values and calibrators in 2021, the year Shafer published "Testing by betting". The American Statistical Association's statement on p-values, by Wasserstein and Lazar in 2016, set out what a p-value does and does not measure.

## Further reading

- George Casella and Roger L. Berger, Statistical Inference, 2nd ed., Duxbury, 2002. Tests, intervals, pivots and the Neyman-Pearson theory at the level of a first graduate course.
- E. L. Lehmann and Joseph P. Romano, Testing Statistical Hypotheses, 3rd ed., Springer, 2005. The standard reference on optimal tests, with chapters on multiple testing.
- Alan Agresti, Categorical Data Analysis, 3rd ed., Wiley, 2013. Binomial and multinomial tests, contingency tables and exact methods.
- Lawrence D. Brown, T. Tony Cai and Anirban DasGupta, Interval estimation for a binomial proportion, Statistical Science 16(2), 2001. Why the Wald interval fails and which intervals to use instead.
- Sandrine Dudoit, Juliet Popper Shaffer and Jennifer C. Boldrick, Multiple hypothesis testing in microarray experiments, Statistical Science 18(1), 2003. A survey of FWER and FDR procedures, including the resampling methods.
- Bradley Efron, Large-Scale Inference, Cambridge University Press, 2010. Empirical Bayes views of the false discovery rate and the local fdr.
