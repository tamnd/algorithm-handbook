## History

Least squares with normal errors goes back to Gauss in 1809. His later Theoria combinationis (1821–1823) gave the distribution-free justification, that least squares has the smallest variance among linear unbiased estimators. Markov restated it in his textbook of 1900 and 1908, and Neyman attached Markov's name to it in 1934. Aitken wrote the result in matrix form in 1935 and extended it to correlated errors, which is generalized least squares. Fisher's Statistical Methods for Research Workers (1925) tabulated the variance ratio through $z = \frac12\log F$, and Snedecor named the F distribution in 1934. The numerical side came later. Householder's reflections (1958) and Golub's least squares method of 1965 made the QR decomposition the standard way to fit a regression and to compute nested F statistics.

Diagnostics grew in two waves. The first concerned the error assumptions. Durbin and Watson gave their test for serial correlation in 1950 and 1951, Box and Cox proposed their family of power transformations in 1964, and Wilk and Gnanadesikan surveyed probability plots in 1968. Unequal variances were handled first by Eicker in 1963 and 1967 and by Huber in 1967, and then for econometrics by White in 1980, after Breusch and Pagan had published their test in 1979. Koenker's studentized form of that test followed in 1981, and MacKinnon and White proposed the corrections HC1 to HC3 in 1985. The second wave concerned single observations. Cook defined his distance in 1977, Hoaglin and Welsch studied the hat matrix in 1978, and the books of Belsley, Kuh and Welsch (1980) and of Cook and Weisberg (1982) gave the subject its present shape.

Model choice began as computation. Efroymson's stepwise procedure of 1960 and the leaps and bounds algorithm of Furnival and Wilson (1974) searched subsets by their residual sums of squares. Akaike's criterion of 1973 and 1974 and Schwarz's of 1978 then gave the search a target. Hurvich and Tsai corrected AIC for small samples in 1989. Inference after such a search was long done as if no search had taken place, and Freedman showed its effect in 1983. Berk, Brown, Buja, Zhang and Zhao gave simultaneous PoSI intervals in 2013. Lee, Sun, Sun and Taylor gave the polyhedral lemma, which conditions on the selection event, in work published in 2016.

Mixed models came from animal breeding. Henderson's equations appeared in an abstract of 1950 and in a paper with Kempthorne, Searle and von Krosigk in 1959. Goldberger gave the best linear unbiased predictor for a regression with correlated errors in 1962, and Robinson reviewed its many derivations in 1991. Patterson and Thompson introduced restricted maximum likelihood in 1971, Harville wrote the restricted likelihood as a marginal likelihood in 1974 and reviewed ML and REML in 1977, and Laird and Ware gave the longitudinal form in 1982. Outside the normal linear model, Nelder and Wedderburn defined generalized linear models in 1972 and fitted them by iteratively reweighted least squares. Wedderburn's quasi-likelihood followed in 1974, and Liang and Zeger's generalized estimating equations in 1986. Errors in both variables go back at least to Deming's book of 1943, and Fuller's monograph of 1987 is the standard account.

## Further reading

- G. A. F. Seber and A. J. Lee, Linear Regression Analysis, 2nd ed., Wiley, 2003. The linear model and its F-tests in matrix form, with the proofs this chapter leaves out.
- R. D. Cook and S. Weisberg, Residuals and Influence in Regression, Chapman and Hall, 1982. Leverage, studentized residuals and Cook's distance, worked out with care.
- P. McCullagh and J. A. Nelder, Generalized Linear Models, 2nd ed., Chapman and Hall, 1989. Deviance, IRLS and quasi-likelihood from the people who built them.
- S. R. Searle, G. Casella and C. E. McCulloch, Variance Components, Wiley, 1992. ANOVA estimation, ML, REML and BLUP for random effects, with their history.
- G. Claeskens and N. L. Hjort, Model Selection and Model Averaging, Cambridge University Press, 2008. AIC, BIC and related criteria, with their assumptions stated.
- A. C. Cameron and D. L. Miller, A Practitioner's Guide to Cluster-Robust Inference, Journal of Human Resources, 2015. When cluster-robust standard errors work, and what to do when there are few clusters.
