A trial gives a new drug to 50 patients and an old one to 50 others, and 31 and 22 of them recover. The difference, $0.18$, is an estimate. Its standard error, about $0.10$, says how much it would move if the trial were run again. A test asks whether a difference this large is likely when the drugs are equally good, and an interval gives the values of the true difference that the data do not rule out. Each of these numbers needs a model of how the data arose, and each guarantee holds only under that model. If the patients chose their own drug, the same arithmetic estimates something else.

This volume is about the methods that turn data into estimates, tests and intervals, and about the theorems that say how far to trust them. Its eight chapters hold 276 entries: point estimation, hypothesis testing and intervals, nonparametric and robust methods, regression and diagnostics, Bayesian statistics, time series and sequential analysis, causal inference and the design of experiments, and survival analysis.

## Why statistics gets a volume of its own

The volume uses the probability of Volume 1, the models of Volume 25 and the sampling of Volume 17, but it asks a different question. Probability goes from a known law to the data; statistics goes from the data back to the law, and every answer carries a statement of how wrong it can be. A few ideas come back in every chapter:

- **A guarantee is exact, asymptotic or conditional on assumptions.** The [t interval](/t-confidence-interval/) covers at exactly its nominal rate for normal iid data, and the [Horvitz-Thompson estimator](/horvitz-thompson-estimator/) is exactly unbiased under its design. [Wilks's theorem](/wilks-theorem/), the [asymptotic normality of the MLE](/asymptotic-normality-of-mle/), sandwich standard errors and the [bootstrap](/bootstrap-method/) hold only as $n \to \infty$, and the bootstrap can fail, for the maximum or under heavy tails. Other results need conditions that the data cannot check: regularity, proportional hazards, unconfoundedness. A coverage or a power measured by simulation is a measurement, not a theorem.
- **Frequentist and Bayesian statements answer different questions.** A 95% [confidence interval](/confidence-interval/) is a procedure that covers the true value in 95% of repeated samples; a 95% [credible interval](/credible-interval/) holds 95% of the posterior probability. A [p-value](/p-value/) is the probability under the null of a result at least as extreme; it is not the probability that the null is true.
- **The statistic is also an algorithm.** The variance is computed by [Welford's update](/welford-algorithm/), a rank test by sorting, [Kaplan-Meier](/kaplan-meier-estimator/) in one pass over sorted times, a change point by dynamic programming, an [MCMC](/metropolis-hastings/) posterior by a Markov chain that converges only in the limit. Each entry gives the cost and the numerical issues.
- **Identification comes before estimation.** A causal effect, a missing-data mean or a survival curve under censoring is first a question of whether the observed law determines the target at all. Only then does the choice of estimator matter.

## The chapters

**Chapter 33.1, Point estimation**, defines an [estimator](/estimator/) and its [bias](/estimator-bias/), [standard error](/standard-error/) and [consistency](/consistent-estimator/), and computes the variance in one pass and in parallel. It covers the [likelihood](/likelihood-function/), the [score](/score-function/) and [Fisher scoring](/fisher-scoring/); [sufficiency](/sufficient-statistic/), the [Cramér-Rao bound](/cramer-rao-bound/) and the theorems of [Rao-Blackwell](/rao-blackwell-theorem/) and [Lehmann-Scheffé](/lehmann-scheffe-theorem/); the [delta method](/delta-method/) and the large-sample theory of the MLE with its counterexamples; [M-estimators](/m-estimator/), [GMM](/generalized-method-of-moments/) and [empirical likelihood](/empirical-likelihood/); the [James-Stein estimator](/james-stein-estimator/); and estimation under a sampling design or with [missing data](/missing-data-mechanism/).

**Chapter 33.2, Hypothesis testing and intervals**, turns estimates into decisions and sets. It covers [tests](/statistical-hypothesis-test/), [errors of the two types](/type-i-and-type-ii-errors/), [power](/statistical-power/) and [sample size](/sample-size-determination/); the exact tests, from the [binomial test](/binomial-test/) to [Fisher's exact test](/fisher-exact-test/), and the t, z and chi-squared tests; the [Neyman-Pearson lemma](/neyman-pearson-lemma/), the [likelihood ratio](/likelihood-ratio-test/), [Wald](/wald-test/) and [score](/score-test/) tests and the [duality of tests and intervals](/test-interval-duality/); binomial intervals such as [Clopper-Pearson](/clopper-pearson-interval/) and [Wilson](/wilson-score-interval/); and multiple testing, from [Bonferroni](/bonferroni-correction/) and [Holm](/holm-bonferroni-method/) to the [Benjamini-Hochberg procedure](/benjamini-hochberg-procedure/), which controls the false discovery rate under independence or positive dependence, the [Benjamini-Yekutieli](/benjamini-yekutieli-procedure/) correction for arbitrary dependence, [e-variables](/e-variable/) and the [knockoff filter](/knockoff-filter/).

**Chapter 33.3, Nonparametric, resampling and robust methods**, drops the parametric model. It covers the [empirical distribution function](/empirical-distribution-function/) with the [Glivenko-Cantelli theorem](/glivenko-cantelli-theorem/) and the [DKW inequality](/dkw-inequality/); rank and [permutation tests](/permutation-test/), exact under exchangeability; the bootstrap, the [jackknife](/jackknife-resampling/) and their intervals; [kernel density estimation](/kernel-density-estimation/) and local regression; the [influence function](/influence-function/), the [breakdown point](/breakdown-point/) and robust estimators of location and regression, from [Huber's](/huber-m-estimator/) to the [minimum covariance determinant](/minimum-covariance-determinant/); and [split conformal prediction](/split-conformal-prediction/), with finite-sample marginal coverage under exchangeability.

**Chapter 33.4, Regression and diagnostics**, checks and extends the linear model of Volume 25. It covers the [Gauss-Markov theorem](/gauss-markov-theorem/), the [F-test](/regression-f-test/), [AIC](/akaike-information-criterion/) and [BIC](/bayesian-information-criterion/); the [hat matrix](/hat-matrix/) and its [diagonal](/leverage-statistics/), [studentized residuals](/studentized-residual/) and [Cook's distance](/cooks-distance/); [heteroscedasticity](/heteroscedasticity/) and its [robust standard errors](/heteroscedasticity-consistent-standard-errors/); [weighted](/weighted-least-squares/) and [generalized least squares](/generalized-least-squares/), [IRLS](/iteratively-reweighted-least-squares/), [mixed models](/linear-mixed-model/) and [REML](/restricted-maximum-likelihood/); [GEE](/generalized-estimating-equations/); and [post-selection inference](/post-selection-inference/), which corrects the intervals of a model chosen from the same data.

**Chapter 33.5, Bayesian statistics**, treats the parameter as random. It covers [credible intervals](/credible-interval/), [Bayes estimators](/bayes-estimator/) and [Bayes factors](/bayes-factor/); priors, from [Jeffreys](/jeffreys-prior/) and [reference priors](/reference-prior/) to [spike-and-slab](/spike-and-slab-prior/) and the [horseshoe](/horseshoe-prior/); [hierarchical models](/hierarchical-bayesian-model/) and [empirical Bayes](/empirical-bayes/); the [Laplace approximation](/laplace-approximation/), [INLA](/integrated-nested-laplace-approximation/), [nested](/nested-sampling/) and [bridge sampling](/bridge-sampling/) and [ABC](/approximate-bayesian-computation/); model checking with [posterior predictive checks](/posterior-predictive-check/), [WAIC](/widely-applicable-information-criterion/) and [PSIS-LOO](/pareto-smoothed-importance-sampling-loo/); and the [Bernstein-von Mises theorem](/bernstein-von-mises-theorem/), which says when credible and confidence intervals agree in large samples.

**Chapter 33.6, Time series and sequential analysis**, drops independence. It covers [stationarity](/stationary-process/), [AR](/autoregressive-model/), [MA](/moving-average-model/), [ARMA](/arma-model/) and [ARIMA](/arima-model/) models with the [Box-Jenkins method](/box-jenkins-method/); unit-root tests, [cointegration](/cointegration/) and [GARCH](/garch-model/); [change-point detection](/change-point-detection/) by [CUSUM](/cusum/), [binary segmentation](/binary-segmentation/) and [PELT](/pelt-algorithm/); and tests that read the data as they arrive, from Wald's [SPRT](/sequential-probability-ratio-test/) and [group sequential designs](/group-sequential-design/) to [test martingales](/test-martingale/) and [confidence sequences](/confidence-sequence/), which stay valid at any stopping time.

**Chapter 33.7, Causal inference and design of experiments**, asks what would have happened under another treatment. It covers [potential outcomes](/potential-outcomes-framework/), [SUTVA](/sutva/) and [ignorability](/ignorability/); [randomized experiments](/randomized-experiment/), blocks, Latin squares, [factorial designs](/factorial-design/) and [optimal designs](/optimal-experimental-design/), with [analysis of variance](/one-way-anova/); [propensity scores](/propensity-score/), [inverse probability weighting](/inverse-probability-weighting/) and its [augmented](/augmented-inverse-probability-weighting/) form; [instrumental variables](/instrumental-variables-estimation/), [difference in differences](/difference-in-differences/), [regression discontinuity](/regression-discontinuity-design/) and [synthetic control](/synthetic-control-method/), each with the assumption that identifies it; and causal discovery by the [PC](/pc-algorithm/) and [FCI](/fci-algorithm/) algorithms, which need faithfulness.

**Chapter 33.8, Survival analysis**, handles times that are often not seen to the end. It covers the [survival](/survival-function/) and [hazard](/hazard-function/) functions and [censoring](/censoring/); the [Kaplan-Meier](/kaplan-meier-estimator/) and [Nelson-Aalen](/nelson-aalen-estimator/) estimators with [Greenwood's formula](/greenwood-formula/); the [log-rank test](/log-rank-test/); the [Cox model](/cox-proportional-hazards-model/) with its [partial likelihood](/cox-partial-likelihood/) and [Schoenfeld residuals](/schoenfeld-residuals/); and [competing risks](/competing-risks/), with the [Aalen-Johansen estimator](/aalen-johansen-estimator/) and the [Fine-Gray model](/fine-gray-model/). The estimators need censoring that is non-informative.

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course, read these entries in this order: estimator, sampling distribution, standard error, likelihood function, sufficient statistic, the asymptotic normality of the MLE, confidence interval, statistical hypothesis test, p-value, the t confidence interval, the likelihood ratio test, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, the bootstrap, permutation test, the Gauss-Markov theorem, credible interval, autoregressive model, potential outcomes framework, randomized experiment and the Kaplan-Meier estimator. Do the exercises marked ◦ at the end of each chapter you touch.

If you already know the basics, start with the opening page of each chapter. It names the groups in the chapter and the order to read them. Chapter 33.1 comes first, and Chapter 33.2 builds its tests and intervals on it; the other six chapters can be read in any order after these two. Chapter 33.5 compares its answers with the frequentist ones of the first two chapters, and Chapter 33.7 reuses the weighting of Chapter 33.1 and the regression of Chapter 33.4.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained. Entries in other volumes are cited by number too.
