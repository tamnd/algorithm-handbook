A doctor wants to know whether a drug helps. For each patient there are two outcomes: the one the patient would have with the drug, and the one the patient would have without it. The effect of the drug on that patient is the difference. But each patient either takes the drug or does not, so only one of the two outcomes is ever seen. Causal inference is the study of what can still be learned about these effects, and under which assumptions.

The answer depends on how treatment was given. If chance decided it, as in a randomized experiment, the treated and untreated groups differ only by chance, and a difference in means estimates the average effect with no further assumption. If people chose for themselves, or a doctor chose for them, the groups may differ in ways that also affect the outcome. Then every method needs an assumption that the data cannot fully check: that all common causes were measured, or that an instrument has no direct effect, or that two groups would have moved in parallel. Two questions run through the chapter. *Identification* asks whether the effect is a function of the distribution of the observed data at all. *Estimation* asks how to compute it well from a finite sample. A method can do well on the second and still fail the first.

The chapter also covers the design of experiments. It starts with the analysis of variance and goes on to blocks, factorial designs and their fractions, and designs that are optimal for a regression model. Its question is how to choose the runs so that a fixed budget answers as much as possible.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups, read in order.

1. **The causal question.** [Potential outcomes](/potential-outcomes-framework/), the [stable unit treatment value assumption](/sutva/), the [average treatment effect](/average-treatment-effect/) and its relatives, and why a [randomized experiment](/randomized-experiment/) identifies them. [Simpson's paradox](/simpsons-paradox/) shows that the data alone cannot say which comparison is causal.
2. **Analysing a comparative experiment.** The F test of [one-way ANOVA](/one-way-anova/), the simultaneous intervals of [Scheffé](/scheffe-method/) and [Tukey](/tukey-hsd/), [block designs](/randomized-block-design/), [Latin squares](/latin-square-design/), [balanced incomplete blocks](/balanced-incomplete-block-design/), [two-way ANOVA](/two-way-anova/), and [CUPED](/cuped/), which uses a covariate measured before the experiment to shrink the variance.
3. **Designs for many factors.** [Factorial](/factorial-design/) and [fractional factorial](/fractional-factorial-design/) designs, the [orthogonal arrays](/orthogonal-array/) behind them, [Plackett-Burman](/plackett-burman-design/) screening designs and [response surface methodology](/response-surface-methodology/).
4. **Optimal design.** The criteria of [optimal design](/optimal-experimental-design/), the [Kiefer-Wolfowitz theorem](/kiefer-wolfowitz-equivalence-theorem/) that certifies a D-optimal design, and the [Fedorov exchange algorithm](/fedorov-exchange-algorithm/) that searches for one.
5. **Adjusting for measured confounders.** [Ignorability](/ignorability/), the [propensity score](/propensity-score/), [weighting](/inverse-probability-weighting/) and [matching](/propensity-score-matching/), and [Rosenbaum's sensitivity analysis](/rosenbaum-sensitivity-analysis/) for bias from confounders that were not measured.
6. **Doubly robust and machine-learning estimators.** [Augmented weighting](/augmented-inverse-probability-weighting/), [double machine learning](/double-machine-learning/) and [targeted maximum likelihood](/targeted-maximum-likelihood-estimation/).
7. **Natural experiments.** [Difference in differences](/difference-in-differences/), the [synthetic control method](/synthetic-control-method/), [instrumental variables](/instrumental-variables-estimation/), [two-stage least squares](/two-stage-least-squares/), the [local average treatment effect](/local-average-treatment-effect/) and [regression discontinuity](/regression-discontinuity-design/).
8. **Mechanisms and causal graphs.** [Mediation analysis](/mediation-analysis/), the [front-door criterion](/front-door-criterion/), and four ways to learn a causal graph from data: the [PC](/pc-algorithm/), [FCI](/fci-algorithm/), [GES](/greedy-equivalence-search/) and [LiNGAM](/lingam/) algorithms.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these twelve entries in order: potential outcomes, the average treatment effect, the randomized experiment, Simpson's paradox, one-way ANOVA, the randomized block design, the factorial design, ignorability, the propensity score, inverse probability weighting, difference in differences and instrumental variables. They are marked *Intro* or *Core* in their headings. Together they cover the main idea of each half of the chapter: how chance assignment and blocking make comparisons fair, and what has to be assumed when there is no chance assignment. Read the worked examples with care. Most of them are small enough to check by hand, and several reuse the same data, so the methods can be compared on one table.
