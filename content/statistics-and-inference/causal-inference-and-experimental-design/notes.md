## History

The design of experiments began in agriculture. Jerzy Neyman wrote down potential yields for each plot in 1923 and analysed a completely randomized experiment over repeated assignments. Ronald Fisher made randomization the basis of inference: the analysis of variance appeared in 1925, and *The Design of Experiments* (1935) set out blocking, Latin squares and the randomization test. Frank Yates introduced balanced incomplete blocks in 1936 and the analysis of factorial experiments in 1937; Fisher proved in 1940 that such a design needs at least as many blocks as treatments. Fractional replication (Finney, 1945), the Plackett-Burman screening designs (1946) and Rao's orthogonal arrays (1947) followed. Box and Wilson started response surface methodology in 1951, and Box and Hunter named the resolution of a fraction in 1961.

Optimal design began when Kirstine Smith found designs for polynomial regression that minimize the largest prediction variance in 1918. Jack Kiefer built the general theory from 1959, and Kiefer and Jacob Wolfowitz proved in 1960 that D-optimal and G-optimal approximate designs are the same. Valerii Fedorov's exchange algorithm (1972) made it a computation. Euler conjectured in 1782 that orthogonal Latin squares of order 6 do not exist; Tarry confirmed this in 1900, and Bose, Shrikhande and Parker showed in 1960 that they exist for every order except 2 and 6. John Tukey and Henry Scheffé both gave methods for many comparisons among treatment means in 1953.

For observational data, Donald Rubin developed the potential-outcomes language from 1974; he stated the stable unit treatment value assumption in 1980, and Paul Holland's review of 1986 made the framework widely known. Paul Rosenbaum and Rubin defined the propensity score in 1983, and Rosenbaum developed sensitivity analysis for hidden bias from 1987, following Jerome Cornfield's 1959 argument about smoking. Inverse weighting goes back to Horvitz and Thompson's survey estimator of 1952. James Robins, Andrea Rotnitzky and Lue Ping Zhao gave the augmented weighting estimator in 1994. Van der Laan and Rubin introduced targeted maximum likelihood in 2006, and Chernozhukov and six coauthors published double machine learning in 2018. CUPED brought control variates to online experiments in 2013.

Economists turned to natural experiments. Philip Wright gave an instrumental-variables estimate in 1928, and Henri Theil gave two-stage least squares in 1953. Donald Thistlethwaite and Donald Campbell introduced the regression discontinuity design in 1960. Guido Imbens and Joshua Angrist showed in 1994 that under monotonicity an instrument identifies the effect on compliers. David Card and Alan Krueger's 1994 study of the minimum wage made difference-in-differences familiar, and Alberto Abadie and Javier Gardeazabal built the first synthetic control in 2003, which Abadie, Diamond and Hainmueller made general in 2010.

Graphs gave a second language. Reuben Baron and David Kenny set the practice of mediation analysis in 1986, and Robins and Sander Greenland defined direct and indirect effects in 1992. Judea Pearl gave the front-door criterion in 1995 and the mediation formula in 2001. Peter Spirtes and Clark Glymour published the PC algorithm in 1991, and *Causation, Prediction, and Search* (1993, with Richard Scheines) introduced FCI. David Maxwell Chickering made greedy equivalence search practical in 2002, and Shohei Shimizu and coauthors showed in 2006 that non-Gaussian errors identify the whole graph of a linear acyclic model without hidden confounders.

## Further reading

- George E. P. Box, J. Stuart Hunter and William G. Hunter, *Statistics for Experimenters*, second edition (2005). It teaches blocking, factorial designs and response surfaces through worked examples.
- A. C. Atkinson, A. N. Donev and R. D. Tobias, *Optimum Experimental Designs, with SAS* (2007). It covers optimality criteria, the equivalence theorem and exchange algorithms.
- Guido W. Imbens and Donald B. Rubin, *Causal Inference for Statistics, Social, and Biomedical Sciences* (2015). It develops potential outcomes from randomization inference to propensity scores and noncompliance.
- Paul R. Rosenbaum, *Observational Studies*, second edition (2002). It is the main source for matching and for sensitivity analysis to hidden bias.
- Judea Pearl, *Causality: Models, Reasoning, and Inference*, second edition (2009). It gives the graphical theory of identification, including the front-door criterion and mediation.
- Peter Spirtes, Clark Glymour and Richard Scheines, *Causation, Prediction, and Search*, second edition (2000). It presents the PC and FCI algorithms and the assumptions they need.
