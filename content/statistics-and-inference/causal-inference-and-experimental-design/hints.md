1. The treated outcomes are $5, 6, 4$ and the control outcomes are $5, 4, 6$, so the naive difference is $5 - 5 = 0$. The ATT is the mean of $\tau = 2, 4, 3$, which is 3. The selection bias is $(3 + 2 + 1)/3 - (5 + 4 + 6)/3 = 2 - 5 = -3$, and $0 = 3 + (-3)$. The ATC is the mean of $0, 3, 0$, which is 1, and $\tfrac12 \cdot 3 + \tfrac12 \cdot 1 = 2$ is the ATE. The units that would gain most were treated, but their outcomes without treatment were low, and the two effects cancel in the naive comparison.

2. There are $\binom{n}{n/2}$ assignments, and the observed one can be the single most extreme, so the smallest one-sided p-value is $1/\binom{n}{n/2}$. Swapping the treated and control halves changes the sign of the difference in means, so a two-sided p-value counts at least two assignments and is at least $2/\binom{n}{n/2}$. For $n = 6$ this is $2/20 = 0.1$; for $n = 8$ it is $2/70 \approx 0.029$. So $n = 8$.

3. A: 0.90 and 0.30; B: 0.80 and 0.20. Overall A has $42/100 = 0.42$ and B has $68/100 = 0.68$, because A was given mostly to severe cases. Standardized to half mild and half severe, A has $0.6$ and B has $0.5$. Severity is a common cause of treatment and recovery, so the comparison within severity, or the standardized one, is the causal one. A is better for a new patient of either kind.

4. The group means are 2, 4, 6 and the grand mean is 4. $SS_B = 3(4 + 0 + 4) = 24$ and $SS_W = 2 + 2 + 2 = 6$. $F = (24/2)/(6/6) = 12$ on 2 and 6 degrees of freedom. 11. The six estimates are $-3/2, -3/2, 3, 0, 9/2, 9/2$ (treating $\{1,2\}, \{1,3\}, \{1,4\}, \{2,3\}, \{2,4\}, \{3,4\}$), with mean $3/2$, the ATE. Their variance is $27/4$. 14. Greedy matches 0.50 to 0.45 and then must match 0.40 to 0.90, for a cost of $0.05 + 0.50 = 0.55$. The optimal matching pairs 0.40 with 0.45 and 0.50 with 0.90, for $0.05 + 0.40 = 0.45$. For the second part, take $t_1 < t_2$ matched to $c_1 > c_2$ and check the possible orders of the four points: in each, $\lvert t_1 - c_2 \rvert + \lvert t_2 - c_1 \rvert \le \lvert t_1 - c_1 \rvert + \lvert t_2 - c_2 \rvert$. Uncrossing never raises the cost and reduces the number of crossing pairs, so it ends at a non-crossing optimum. This is the fact behind the dynamic program on sorted scores.

16. The intention-to-treat difference is $0.6 \cdot 14 + 0.4 \cdot 9 - 10 = 2$, and the Wald ratio is $2/0.6 = 10/3$. By randomization and exclusion, the never-takers have mean $Y(0) = 9$ in both arms, and they are 40% of the control arm too. So $10 = 0.6\,\mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid C] + 0.4 \cdot 9$, which gives $\mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid C] = 32/3$. The takers in the treated arm are exactly the compliers, so $\mathbb{E}[Y(1) \mid C] = 14$, and $14 - 32/3 = 10/3$. Defiers would have to take the treatment when assigned to control, which nobody can do. Since the treated are exactly the compliers assigned to treatment, here the LATE is also the effect on the treated.

23. The bias is of order $n^{-(a + b)}$, so it is negligible when $a + b > 1/2$. For $a = b = 1/4$ the product is of order $n^{-1/2}$, the same order as the standard error, so the bias shifts the limiting normal law and the usual interval does not have its nominal coverage; each error must be $o(n^{-1/4})$, not just of that order. $a = b = 0.3$ and $a = 1/2$, $b = 0.01$ are fine; $a = 0.2$, $b = 0.25$ is not. A product condition lets one model be poor if the other is good: a propensity that is known, as in a randomized experiment, or fitted by a correct parametric model ($a = 1/2$), permits an outcome model that converges slowly.

24. $S_\tau^2 = S_1^2 + S_0^2 - 2S_{10}$, where $S_{10}$ is the covariance of $Y(1)$ and $Y(0)$, and $S_{10} \le S_1S_0$ by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. So the variance is at most $S_1^2/n_1 + S_0^2/n_0 - (S_1 - S_0)^2/n$. In Exercise 11 this is about 6.94, against the true $6.75$ and Neyman's $7.17$. For the six-unit table it is about 1.42, against $16/15 \approx 1.07$ and $23/15 \approx 1.53$. The bound depends only on the two marginal laws, so it can be estimated; it is exact when $Y(1)$ is an increasing linear function of $Y(0)$ across units.

25. For $x$ uniform on $[0, h]$, the slope is $\operatorname{Cov}(x, x^2)/\operatorname{Var}(x) = (h^3/12)/(h^2/12) = h$ and the intercept is $h^2/3 - h \cdot h/2 = -h^2/6$. On $[-h, 0]$ the same holds by symmetry. The fitted intercepts are $a_1 - \kappa_1h^2/6$ and $a_0 - \kappa_0h^2/6$, so the bias is $-(\kappa_1 - \kappa_0)h^2/6$. With the given numbers the estimate is $2 - 2/6 = 5/3$. Equal curvature on both sides moves both intercepts by the same amount. Since $\mu'' = 2\kappa$, the uniform kernel gives $-(h^2/12)(\mu_+'' - \mu_-'')$, against $-(h^2/20)(\mu_+'' - \mu_-'')$ for the triangular kernel: the triangular kernel puts less weight far from the cutoff.

26. With weight $1/2$ at $a$ and at $b$, $\det M = \tfrac14(ab^2 - ba^2)^2 = \tfrac14a^2b^2(b - a)^2$. This grows with $b$, so $b = 1$, and $a^2(1 - a)^2$ is largest at $a = 1/2$. Then $\det M = 1/64$ and $d(x) = 34x^2 - 72x^3 + 40x^4$. Factor $d(x) - 2 = 2(2x - 1)^2(5x + 1)(x - 1)$, which is at most 0 on $[0, 1]$ and is 0 at $x = 1/2$ and $x = 1$.

28. Write $r = (X - bE)/(1 + b^2)$, since the slope of $X$ on $Y$ is $b/(1 + b^2)$. Then $\operatorname{Cov}(r, Y) = (b - b)/(1 + b^2) = 0$. For the second claim, expand both squares and use independence: every term cancels against $\mathbb{E}[r^2]\,\mathbb{E}[Y^2]$ except those with $X^4$ and $E^4$, which leave $b^2(\kappa_X + \kappa_E)/(1 + b^2)^2$. (In the language of cumulants, $\operatorname{Cov}(r^2, Y^2) = 2\operatorname{Cov}(r, Y)^2 + \kappa_4(r, r, Y, Y)$.) Normal laws have $\kappa = 0$, so the covariance is 0, as it must be: $r$ and $Y$ are then jointly normal and uncorrelated, hence independent, and the backward model fits as well as the forward one. For $\pm 1$ laws, $\kappa = -2$ for each and the covariance is $-1$. If $X$ is $\pm 1$ ($\kappa_X = -2$) and $E$ takes $\pm\sqrt5$ with probability $1/10$ each and 0 otherwise ($\kappa_E = 2$), the covariance is 0 although the laws are not normal; $r$ and $Y$ are still dependent, and other moments show it.
