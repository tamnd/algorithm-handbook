## History

Keyframing comes from hand-drawn animation, where a lead animator drew the key poses and assistants drew the frames in between. Thomas and Johnston set down the studio's rules in *Disney Animation: The Illusion of Life* (1981), and Lasseter showed in 1987 how squash and stretch, timing and slow in and slow out carry over to 3D computer animation. Burtnyk and Wein (1976) had already built interactive key frame systems with skeletons. Kochanek and Bartels (1984) gave animators splines with tension, continuity and bias at each key, and Steketee and Badler (1985) separated the path from its timing, the idea behind ease curves and arc length control. Guenter and Parent (1990) worked out the arc length table with Gaussian quadrature and Newton's method for the inverse.

Rotations needed their own curves. Shoemake (1985) brought quaternions and slerp into animation and built spherical Bezier curves through keys, and gave squad in his SIGGRAPH 1987 course notes. Dam, Koch and Lillholm (1998) proved that squad is $C^1$. Kim, Kim and Shin (1995) wrote B-spline, Bezier and Hermite curves in cumulative form and turned them into curves of unit quaternions. For frames along a path, Bishop (1975) described the frame whose normals do not turn about the tangent, Klok (1986) named it the rotation minimizing frame and used it for sweeping, and Wang, Jüttler, Zheng and Liu (2008) gave the double reflection method with fourth-order accuracy.

Space deformation began with Barr (1984), who gave the taper, twist and bend with their Jacobians and the rule for moving normals. Sederberg and Parry (1986) replaced the closed-form maps by a Bezier lattice in free-form deformation, and Coquillart (1990) extended the lattice to cylinders and glued shapes. Singh and Fiume (1998) used space curves as handles in wires. On the image side, Beier and Neely published their line-pair morph at SIGGRAPH 92, after mesh warping had been used for the morphs in the film Willow.

Cages grew out of generalized barycentric coordinates. Floater (2003) defined mean value coordinates for star-shaped polygons, Ju, Schaefer and Warren (2005) extended them to closed triangle meshes, and Hormann and Floater (2006) to arbitrary polygons. Joshi, Meyer, DeRose, Green and Sanocki (2007) showed that mean value coordinates turn negative in concave cages and proposed harmonic coordinates, and Lipman, Levin and Cohen-Or (2008) added face normals to get Green coordinates, which keep angles in 2D. Rigidity came from morphing: Alexa, Cohen-Or and Levin (2000) interpolated rotation and stretch per triangle, and Igarashi, Moscovich and Hughes (2005) used rigid triangles to drag a 2D shape by handles in real time.

## Further reading

- Rick Parent, *Computer Animation: Algorithms and Techniques*, 3rd edition (2012). The standard textbook, with keyframing, speed control, orientation curves and deformation in one place.
- Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston, *Disney Animation: The Illusion of Life* (1981). The source of the principles of animation, written by two of the studio's animators.
- Michael S. Floater, "Generalized barycentric coordinates and applications", *Acta Numerica* 24 (2015). A survey of mean value, harmonic and other coordinates with their proofs.
- Jesús R. Nieto and Antonio Susín, "Cage based deformations: a survey", in *Deformation Models* (Springer, 2013). A comparison of cage coordinates for deformation.
- Mario Botsch and Olga Sorkine, "On linear variational surface deformation methods", *IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics* 14(1) (2008). The surface-based methods that extend the deformation ideas of this chapter.
