## History

Faces came first. Frederic Parke animated a polygon face in 1972 by interpolating between key shapes, the idea behind every blendshape rig. In 1978 Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen published the Facial Action Coding System, a psychologist's list of visible facial movements. Riggers later used it to choose and name their targets. Skinning came later and has no single inventor. Nadia Magnenat-Thalmann, Richard Laperrière and Daniel Thalmann deformed a hand mesh with joint-dependent local deformations in 1988. The matrix blend then spread in games and tools under many names. In 2000 J. P. Lewis, Matt Cordner and Nickson Fong described it exactly as skeleton subspace deformation and added pose space deformation on top.

Editing captured motion became a research subject in the 1990s. Andrew Witkin and Michael Kass had posed animation as optimization over a whole trajectory in 1988, with a jumping lamp. At SIGGRAPH 95, Witkin and Zoran Popović presented motion warping, and Armin Bruderlin and Lance Williams presented motion signal processing; both add smooth displacements to captured curves. In 1998 Michael Gleicher wrote retargeting as a kinematic spacetime problem, and Charles Rose, Michael Cohen and Bobby Bodenheimer interpolated many examples in verbs and adverbs. Lucas Kovar and Michael Gleicher then produced motion graphs (with Frederic Pighin, 2002), footskate cleanup (with John Schreiner, 2002) and registration curves (2003). In 2016 Simon Clavet's motion matching replaced the precomputed graph with a search at run time, in the game For Honor.

The faults of linear blending drove a decade of skinning work. Ladislav Kavan and Jiří Žára gave spherical blend skinning in 2005. Kavan, Steven Collins, Žára and Carol O'Sullivan gave dual quaternion skinning in 2007 and analyzed it in 2008. Implicit skinning (Vaillant and coworkers, 2013), delta mush (Mancewicz and coworkers at Rhythm and Hues, 2014) and optimized centers of rotation (Binh Huy Le and Jessica Hodgins, 2016) each fixed a remaining fault. Automatic weights followed a parallel line. It runs from the heat weights of Ilya Baran and Jovan Popović (2007) to the bounded biharmonic weights of Alec Jacobson and coworkers (2011) and the skinning decomposition of Le and Zhigang Deng (2012). Deformation transfer (Robert Sumner and Popović, 2004) and example-based facial rigging (Hao Li, Thibaut Weise and Mark Pauly, 2010) did the same for faces.

Physics and crowds form the last line. Dirk Helbing and Péter Molnár modeled pedestrians with social forces in 1995. Adrien Treuille, Seth Cooper and Popović planned whole crowds with one potential per group in 2006. KangKang Yin, Kevin Loken and Michiel van de Panne showed with SIMBICON in 2007 that a few rules make a simulated biped walk. In 2018 Xue Bin Peng and coworkers trained tracking controllers by deep reinforcement learning in DeepMimic.

## Further reading

- Rick Parent, *Computer Animation: Algorithms and Techniques*, third edition, Morgan Kaufmann (2012). A textbook covering kinematic linkages, motion capture processing, faces and crowds.
- Alec Jacobson, Zhigang Deng, Ladislav Kavan and J. P. Lewis, *Skinning: Real-time Shape Deformation*, SIGGRAPH 2014 course. Linear blending, dual quaternions, automatic weights and corrections, in one set of notes.
- Ladislav Kavan, Steven Collins, Jiří Žára and Carol O'Sullivan, *Geometric Skinning with Approximate Dual Quaternion Blending*, ACM Transactions on Graphics 27(4) (2008). The full analysis of dual quaternion skinning, with the comparison to linear blending and ScLERP.
- J. P. Lewis, Ken Anjyo, Taehyun Rhee, Mengjie Zhang, Fred Pighin and Zhigang Deng, *Practice and Theory of Blendshape Facial Models*, Eurographics 2014 State of the Art Reports. A survey of face rigs, from target design to weight solving.
- Lucas Kovar, Michael Gleicher and Frédéric Pighin, *Motion Graphs*, ACM Transactions on Graphics 21(3) (SIGGRAPH 2002). A short paper that set up the data-driven view of motion.
- Andrew Witkin and Michael Kass, *Spacetime Constraints*, Proceedings of SIGGRAPH 88. The paper that posed animation as optimization.
