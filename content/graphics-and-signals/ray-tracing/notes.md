## History

Arthur Appel cast rays from the eye in 1968 to shade solids and find their shadows. That is [ray casting](/ray-casting/). Turner Whitted made the method recursive in 1980, with reflected and refracted rays at each hit. Cook, Porter and Carpenter (1984) sampled the pixel, the lens, the light and time with many rays. Their [distributed ray tracing](/distributed-ray-tracing/) added soft shadows, depth of field and motion blur. In the same year, Heckbert and Hanrahan traced beams and Amanatides traced cones, to give a ray a width.

The cost of a query drove the work on structures. Clark (1976) proposed hierarchies of bounding volumes for visible surface algorithms. Kay and Kajiya (1986) bounded objects by slabs and walked the hierarchy nearest first. Goldsmith and Salmon (1987) estimated the cost of a hierarchy from the surface areas of its boxes. MacDonald and Booth (1990) turned this into the surface area heuristic for space subdivision. Amanatides and Woo (1987) gave the incremental walk through a grid. Möller and Trumbore (1997) published the ray-triangle test that most code still uses, and Woop, Benthin and Wald (2013) made it watertight. On the build side came binned SAH builds (Wald 2007), spatial splits (Stich, Friedrich and Dietrich 2009) and Morton-code builds (Lauterbach and others 2009, and Karras 2012), which suit parallel hardware.

Kajiya (1986) wrote the rendering equation and solved it with [path tracing](/path-tracing/). The same year, Immel, Cohen and Greenberg wrote a similar equation for radiosity. Goral, Torrance, Greenberg and Battaile (1984) had brought [radiosity](/radiosity/) from heat transfer to graphics. Ward, Rubinstein and Clear (1988) cached irradiance. Arvo and Kirk (1990) brought Russian roulette from particle transport to rendering. Lafortune and Willems (1993) traced paths from both ends, and Veach and Guibas did the same independently. Veach and Guibas then gave the [path integral](/path-integral-formulation/) form, multiple importance sampling (1995) and Metropolis light transport (1997). Veach's 1997 thesis gathered all of this and drew the line between unbiased and consistent estimators that the chapter follows.

Jensen (1996) stored photons in a kd-tree and estimated density, and so traded bias for low noise. Keller (1997) turned light paths into virtual point lights. Hachisuka, Ogaki and Jensen (2008) made photon mapping consistent with a shrinking radius. Georgiev, Křivánek, Davidovič and Slusallek (2012) combined vertex connection and merging with MIS. In participating media, delta tracking goes back to Woodcock and others (1965), in reactor physics. Bitterli and others (2020) reused samples across pixels and frames in ReSTIR, for real-time rendering with many lights.

## Further reading

- Matt Pharr, Wenzel Jakob and Greg Humphreys, *Physically Based Rendering: From Theory to Implementation*, third edition, Morgan Kaufmann, 2016. A full renderer explained line by line, from the ray tests and BVHs to path tracing, bidirectional methods and media.
- Eric Veach, *Robust Monte Carlo Methods for Light Transport Simulation*, PhD thesis, Stanford University, 1997. The source of the path integral, MIS, bidirectional path tracing and Metropolis light transport, with careful statements of bias and consistency.
- Philip Dutré, Kavita Bala and Philippe Bekaert, *Advanced Global Illumination*, second edition, A K Peters, 2006. A compact account of the rendering equation and of the Monte Carlo and finite element methods that solve it.
- Henrik Wann Jensen, *Realistic Image Synthesis Using Photon Mapping*, A K Peters, 2001. The photon map, its density estimates and the bias they bring, by the author of the method.
- Michael F. Cohen and John R. Wallace, *Radiosity and Realistic Image Synthesis*, Academic Press Professional, 1993. The finite element view of diffuse light transport, with form factors and iterative solvers.
