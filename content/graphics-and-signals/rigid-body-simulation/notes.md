## History

The laws of impact are older than computers. Newton's coefficient of restitution relates the speeds before and after a hit, Poisson's relates the impulses of compression and restitution, and Stronge (1990) related the work done in the two phases; with friction, the kinematic form can end a collision with more energy than it began with, and the energetic form cannot. Coulomb's friction law bounds the tangential force by a multiple of the normal force. With rigid bodies and Coulomb friction together, the contact forces at an instant may not exist or may not be unique, which is Painlevé's paradox, and Baraff (1991) proved that deciding whether consistent forces exist is NP-hard.

Simulation in graphics began in the late 1980s. Moore and Wilhelms (1988) and Hahn (1988) gave collision detection and response for animation, with penalty springs and impulses. Baraff (1989) wrote the contact forces as a quadratic program, and in 1994 he gave a pivoting method after Dantzig, fast enough for interactive use. Mirtich and Canny (1995) built impulse-based simulation, in which resting contact is a stream of microcollisions, and Mirtich's thesis (1996) added conservative advancement for the time of impact. Guendelman, Bridson and Fedkiw (2003) introduced shock propagation for stacks. Catto (2005) described the sequential impulse solver of game engines, projected Gauss-Seidel with warm starting, a fixed friction bound and a few iterations; Baumgarte's 1972 feedback is what such solvers use against drift.

Collision detection grew in parallel. Lin and Canny (1991) walked features of convex polyhedra to the closest pair with the help of coherence, and Mirtich (1998) removed its tolerances and its cycling in V-Clip. Cohen, Lin, Manocha and Ponamgi (1995) used sweep and prune for the broad phase in I-COLLIDE, after Baraff's thesis. Gottschalk, Lin and Manocha (1996) built OBB trees with the separating axis test in RAPID, Hubbard (1996) built sphere trees from the medial axis, and Klosowski and others (1998) used k-DOP trees for a flying object in a static scene. Dobkin, Hershberger, Kirkpatrick and Suri (1993) studied the intersection depth of polyhedra, and van den Bergen (2001) gave the expanding polytope algorithm as a sequel to GJK.

Continuous detection came from cloth. Provot (1997) solved the cubic for the coplanarity time of a moving point and triangle, Bridson, Fedkiw and Anderson (2002) made the tests part of robust cloth simulation, and Brochu, Edwards and Bridson (2012) made them exact with predicates. Wang and others (2021) compared the methods on a large benchmark. For concave bodies, Lien and Amato (2004 onward) split shapes into approximately convex pieces, and Mamou and Ghorbel (2009) gave a hierarchical method.

## Further reading

- Christer Ericson, *Real-Time Collision Detection* (2005). The practical reference on broad phase, bounding volume trees, GJK and robust tests.
- Gino van den Bergen, *Collision Detection in Interactive 3D Environments* (2003). GJK and the expanding polytope algorithm from their author, with the numerical care they need.
- Jan Bender, Kenny Erleben and Jeff Trinkle, "Interactive simulation of rigid body dynamics in computer graphics", *Computer Graphics Forum* 33(1) (2014). A survey of contact models and solvers, from impulses to complementarity.
- David Baraff, "Rigid Body Dynamics I and II", in Witkin and Baraff, *Physically Based Modeling: Principles and Practice*, SIGGRAPH 97 course notes. The equations of motion and contact forces, written for programmers.
- W. J. Stronge, *Impact Mechanics* (2000). The theory of collisions with restitution and friction.
- David E. Stewart, "Rigid-body dynamics with friction and impact", *SIAM Review* 42(1) (2000). A survey of the mathematics, including the paradoxes and time stepping.
