## History

The mathematics came before the machines. Gaspard de Prony fitted sums of exponentials to measurements of gases in 1795 by the roots of a polynomial, the first form of [Prony's method](/prony-method/). Arthur Schuster introduced the periodogram in 1898 to search meteorological records for hidden periods. Edmund Whittaker found the cardinal series of sinc functions in 1915, Harry Nyquist counted the pulses a telegraph channel can carry in 1928, Vladimir Kotelnikov stated the sampling theorem in 1933, and Claude Shannon gave it its standard form in 1949. George Udny Yule fitted autoregressive models to sunspot numbers in 1927.

The Second World War made filtering a theory of estimation. Norbert Wiener derived the optimal linear filter for stationary processes in a classified report of 1942, published as a book in 1949, and Andrey Kolmogorov had solved the discrete prediction problem in 1941. Norman Levinson gave the discrete FIR form and the recursion for its Toeplitz equations in 1947. Dwight North derived the matched filter for radar in 1943, and Van Vleck and Middleton found it independently. Dennis Gabor proposed in 1946 to analyse signals by Fourier transforms of short windowed pieces, the idea of the short-time Fourier transform, and the sound spectrograph of Bell Laboratories made the spectrogram an instrument the same year. In 1948 Maurice Bartlett showed that averaging the periodograms of segments lowers the variance of a spectrum estimate.

The digital era began around 1960. Rudolf Kalman published his filter in 1960, and Kalman and Bucy the continuous form in 1961. Bernard Widrow and Marcian Hoff found the LMS rule in 1959 and published it in 1960. The fast Fourier transform of Cooley and Tukey in 1965 changed the costs: Thomas Stockham described sectioned convolution by FFT, the basis of overlap-add and overlap-save, in 1966, and Peter Welch returned to averaged periodograms in 1967. Charles Rader and Bernard Gold gave a systematic account of recursive digital filters through the z-transform in 1967. In 1972 Thomas Parks and James McClellan cast optimal linear-phase FIR design as Chebyshev approximation and solved it with the Remez exchange, and their program with Lawrence Rabiner became the standard design tool. Abraham Savitzky and Marcel Golay published their smoothing filters in 1964, and John Tukey proposed running medians in 1974.

Models of speech and spectra followed. Itakura and Saito, and Atal and Schroeder, applied linear prediction to speech in the late 1960s, and John Makhoul's tutorial of 1975 set out its methods. John Parker Burg introduced maximum entropy spectral analysis in 1967. Pisarenko found frequencies from the smallest eigenvector of a covariance in 1973; Ralph Schmidt generalized this to MUSIC around 1979, and Roy, Paulraj and Kailath introduced ESPRIT in 1986. Multirate processing grew from the work of Schafer and Rabiner on interpolation in 1973 and the polyphase network of Bellanger, Bonnerot and Coudreuse in 1976. Ingrid Daubechies built compactly supported orthonormal wavelets in 1988, Stéphane Mallat showed in 1989 that the wavelet transform is a cascade of filters with downsampling, and Wim Sweldens introduced the lifting scheme in 1996, which JPEG 2000 later used for its lossless transform.

The newest group of the chapter is sparse recovery. Chen, Donoho and Saunders named basis pursuit in 1998, and Pati, Rezaiifar and Krishnaprasad added the orthogonal projection to matching pursuit in 1993. In 2005 and 2006 Candès, Romberg and Tao, and Donoho, showed that $\ell_1$ minimization recovers sparse vectors from few random measurements, which started compressed sensing. Daubechies, Defrise and De Mol proved convergence of the thresholded iteration in 2004, and Beck and Teboulle proved its $O(1/k)$ rate and gave the accelerated form in 2009. Griffin and Lim gave their iteration for signals from STFT magnitudes in 1984, and Norden Huang and coworkers published empirical mode decomposition in 1998.

## Further reading

- Alan V. Oppenheim and Ronald W. Schafer, *Discrete-Time Signal Processing*, third edition, Pearson, 2010. The standard text for sampling, the z-transform, filter structures, filter design and the DFT; most entries of the chapter cite it.
- John G. Proakis and Dimitris G. Manolakis, *Digital Signal Processing: Principles, Algorithms, and Applications*, fourth edition, Pearson Prentice Hall, 2007. A second account of the same core, with more on multirate processing, linear prediction and spectral estimation.
- Petre Stoica and Randolph L. Moses, *Spectral Analysis of Signals*, Pearson Prentice Hall, 2005. Periodogram methods, parametric models, and the subspace methods MUSIC and ESPRIT, with their statistics.
- Simon Haykin, *Adaptive Filter Theory*, fifth edition, Pearson, 2014. Wiener filters, LMS, RLS and the Kalman filter as one family, with convergence analyses.
- Stéphane Mallat, *A Wavelet Tour of Signal Processing: The Sparse Way*, third edition, Academic Press, 2009. Time-frequency analysis, wavelet bases and filter banks, and sparse representations.
- Simon Foucart and Holger Rauhut, *A Mathematical Introduction to Compressive Sensing*, Birkhäuser, 2013. Sparse recovery, the restricted isometry property and the algorithms of the last group, with full proofs.
