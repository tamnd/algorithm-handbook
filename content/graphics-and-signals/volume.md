A screen shows a few million pixels, and a game redraws all of them sixty times a second. Each frame starts as a list of triangles and ends as a grid of colors: each triangle is clipped, projected and turned into pixels, the hidden pixels are dropped, and the rest are shaded. A film renderer makes the same picture by following random paths of light. An image editor finds the edges in a photograph, a phone codes each 20 ms of speech in a few dozen numbers, and a solver moves the bricks of a falling wall and the smoke above it. All of these are algorithms on samples of a continuous world.

This volume is about those algorithms. Of its twelve chapters, nine are written and hold 467 entries: rasterization, curves and surfaces, visibility, ray tracing, image processing, signal processing, keyframing and deformation, rigid body simulation and fluid animation. Real-time rendering, character animation and deformable body simulation are being written.

## Why graphics and signals get a volume of their own

A graphics or signal algorithm turns a continuous object into samples, in space or in time, or samples back into a continuous object, and it is judged at the samples. A few ideas come back in every chapter:

- **A sample is a point.** A [pixel](/pixel/) is a point sample at its center, not a little square. The rules that decide which samples a shape covers, such as the [top-left fill rule](/top-left-fill-rule/), are stated for points.
- **Aliasing is the price of sampling.** Detail finer than the sample spacing comes back as false patterns. The [sampling theorem](/sampling-theorem/) says when it can be avoided, and [antialiasing](/antialiasing/), the [mipmap](/mipmap/) and the [reconstruction filter](/reconstruction-filter/) fight it when it cannot.
- **Exact answers need exact arithmetic.** With integer coordinates, [Bresenham's algorithm](/bresenham-line-algorithm/) and the [edge function](/edge-function/) give exactly the pixels their definitions name, and the [watertight ray-triangle test](/watertight-ray-triangle-intersection/) lets no ray slip between triangles.
- **The cost is per pixel, per ray or per sample.** Bounds count covered pixels, rays, samples, particles or grid cells, like the $O(\log M)$ operations per output of fast convolution.
- **Not every guarantee is a theorem.** Some results are exact, some hold in expectation, like the unbiased estimate of [path tracing](/path-tracing/), and some are heuristics judged by measurement or by eye, like [temporal antialiasing](/temporal-antialiasing/). A simulation for animation may be stable for any step and still far from accurate. The entries say which kind each result is.

## The chapters

**Chapter 28.1, Rasterization**, turns lines, polygons and triangles into pixels. It starts with the pixel and the [graphics pipeline](/graphics-pipeline/), clips with [Cohen-Sutherland](/cohen-sutherland-line-clipping/) and [Liang-Barsky](/liang-barsky-line-clipping/), and draws lines and circles with Bresenham's algorithm and the [midpoint circle algorithm](/midpoint-circle-algorithm/). It fills polygons with the [scanline fill](/scanline-polygon-fill/) and triangles by edge functions and [Pineda's loop](/pineda-rasterization/). It ends with [perspective-correct interpolation](/perspective-correct-interpolation/), [textures](/texture-mapping/) and antialiasing from [supersampling](/supersampling-antialiasing/) to [multisampling](/multisample-antialiasing/).

**Chapter 28.2, Curves and surfaces**, turns a few control points into smooth shapes. A [Bézier curve](/bezier-curve/) is a weighted average of its points with [Bernstein polynomials](/bernstein-polynomials/) as weights, which gives the [convex hull property](/convex-hull-property/) and evaluation by the [de Casteljau algorithm](/de-casteljau-algorithm/). The chapter goes on to the [Catmull-Rom spline](/catmull-rom-spline/), [B-splines](/b-spline/) with the [de Boor algorithm](/de-boor-algorithm/), and [NURBS](/nurbs/). On surfaces it treats subdivision by [Catmull-Clark](/catmull-clark-subdivision/) and [Loop](/loop-subdivision/), meshes from data by [marching cubes](/marching-cubes/) and [Poisson reconstruction](/poisson-surface-reconstruction/), and simplification by [quadric error metrics](/quadric-error-metrics/).

**Chapter 28.3, Visibility**, decides what the viewer sees. It defines [hidden surface removal](/hidden-surface-removal/) and solves it in image space with the [z-buffer](/z-buffer/) and the [hierarchical z-buffer](/hierarchical-z-buffer/), and in object order with the [painter's algorithm](/painters-algorithm/) and [BSP tree visibility](/bsp-tree-visibility/). It covers culling of [back faces](/back-face-culling/), the [view frustum](/frustum-culling/) and [occluders](/occlusion-culling/), shadows by [shadow mapping](/shadow-mapping/) and [shadow volumes](/shadow-volume-algorithm/), and the geometry of seeing, with [Chvátal's theorem](/chvatal-art-gallery-theorem/) that $\lfloor n/3 \rfloor$ guards suffice.

**Chapter 28.4, Ray tracing**, follows rays from the [pinhole camera](/pinhole-camera-model/) into the scene. It intersects rays with [spheres](/ray-sphere-intersection/), triangles by [Möller-Trumbore](/moller-trumbore/) and boxes by the [slab method](/slab-method/). It speeds the search with the [bounding volume hierarchy](/bounding-volume-hierarchy/), built by the [surface area heuristic](/surface-area-heuristic/). For light it gives the [BRDF](/brdf/) from [Lambert](/lambertian-reflectance/) to [microfacets](/microfacet-brdf/), the [rendering equation](/rendering-equation/), path tracing with [Russian roulette](/russian-roulette/) and [multiple importance sampling](/multiple-importance-sampling/), then [bidirectional path tracing](/bidirectional-path-tracing/), [photon mapping](/photon-mapping/), [radiosity](/radiosity/) and [ReSTIR](/restir/).

**Chapter 28.5, Image processing**, works on the [digital image](/digital-image/) itself. It starts with [color spaces](/color-spaces/), [histograms](/image-histogram/) and [equalization](/histogram-equalization/) and [Otsu's threshold](/otsu-thresholding/). It filters with [linear filters](/linear-filtering/), the [Gaussian](/gaussian-filter/) and the [integral image](/integral-image/), and finds edges with [Sobel](/sobel-operator/), [Canny](/canny-edge-detector/) and the [Hough transform](/hough-transform/). It covers [dilation](/morphological-dilation/) and [erosion](/morphological-erosion/), the [distance transform](/distance-transform/), [watershed segmentation](/watershed-segmentation/), [pyramids](/laplacian-pyramid/), [SIFT](/sift/), and restoration and editing with the [bilateral filter](/bilateral-filter/), [non-local means](/non-local-means/) and [seam carving](/seam-carving/), measured by [PSNR](/psnr/) and SSIM.

**Chapter 28.6, Signal processing**, treats one-dimensional sampled signals. It starts with [convolution](/discrete-convolution/), the [z-transform](/z-transform/), [aliasing](/aliasing/) and the sampling theorem. It builds [FIR](/fir-filter/) and [IIR](/iir-filter/) filters, runs long ones by [overlap-add](/overlap-add-method/), and designs them by [Parks-McClellan](/parks-mcclellan-algorithm/). It changes rates by [polyphase resampling](/polyphase-resampling/), splits signals by the [discrete wavelet transform](/discrete-wavelet-transform/), and estimates spectra from the [periodogram](/periodogram/) to [MUSIC](/music-algorithm/). It ends with [linear prediction](/linear-prediction/), the [Wiener](/wiener-filter/), [LMS](/lms-algorithm/) and [Kalman](/kalman-filter/) filters, and sparse recovery by [basis pursuit](/basis-pursuit/) and [compressed sensing](/compressed-sensing/).

**Chapter 28.7, Real-time rendering**, is being written. It will cover what a game engine draws in a few milliseconds: physically based shading, normal and parallax mapping, filtered and cascaded shadows, deferred and clustered shading, image-based lighting, ambient occlusion, transparency, level of detail, texture compression and tone mapping. Meanwhile, read the [microfacet BRDF](/microfacet-brdf/) of Chapter 28.4, and [shadow mapping](/shadow-mapping/) and the [hierarchical z-buffer](/hierarchical-z-buffer/) of Chapter 28.3.

**Chapter 28.8, Keyframing and deformation**, makes motion from a few keys and shapes from a few handles. It starts from [keyframe interpolation](/keyframe-interpolation/), [ease-in and ease-out](/ease-in-ease-out/) and the [arc length table](/arc-length-table/). Rotations are interpolated by [squad](/squad/). Shapes deform by [free-form deformation](/free-form-deformation/), [cage-based deformation](/cage-based-deformation/) with [harmonic coordinates](/harmonic-coordinates/), and [as-rigid-as-possible shape manipulation](/as-rigid-as-possible-shape-manipulation/).

**Chapter 28.9, Character animation**, is being written. It will cover skinning, blendshapes and faces, inverse kinematics, motion capture editing, physics-based control and crowds. Meanwhile, read [inverse kinematics](/inverse-kinematics/) and [FABRIK](/fabrik/) in Volume 37, and squad and keyframe interpolation in Chapter 28.8.

**Chapter 28.10, Rigid body simulation**, moves bodies that never bend. The [problem entry](/rigid-body-simulation-problem/) sets the laws, with [restitution](/coefficient-of-restitution/) and [Coulomb friction](/coulomb-friction-law/). Collisions are found by [sweep and prune](/sweep-and-prune/), [OBB trees](/obb-tree/), the [expanding polytope algorithm](/expanding-polytope-algorithm/) and [continuous collision detection](/continuous-collision-detection/), and resolved by [collision impulses](/collision-impulse/), [projected Gauss-Seidel](/projected-gauss-seidel/) and [Baraff's contact force algorithm](/baraff-contact-force-algorithm/).

**Chapter 28.11, Deformable body simulation**, is being written. It will cover mass-spring systems, finite element elasticity, cloth and rods, position based and projective dynamics, and contact for cloth. Meanwhile, read the contact entries of Chapter 28.10, such as the [elementary continuous collision tests](/elementary-ccd-tests/), and the [finite element method](/finite-element-method/) and [backward Euler method](/backward-euler-method/) of Volume 12.

**Chapter 28.12, Fluid animation**, simulates smoke, fire and water. It builds on the [MAC grid](/mac-grid/) and [stable fluids](/stable-fluids/), stable for any time step but dissipative, adds [vorticity confinement](/vorticity-confinement/) for smoke, and moves liquids with [PIC](/particle-in-cell/), [FLIP](/fluid-implicit-particle/) and [APIC](/affine-particle-in-cell/), which trade dissipation against noise. The SPH solvers, from [weakly compressible SPH](/weakly-compressible-sph/) to [DFSPH](/divergence-free-sph/), keep the density only near its rest value, and [FFT ocean synthesis](/fft-ocean-synthesis/) animates open water.

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course, read these entries in this order: the pixel, the graphics pipeline, the edge function, [triangle rasterization](/triangle-rasterization/), the z-buffer, the Bézier curve, the B-spline, ray-sphere intersection, the bounding volume hierarchy, path tracing, linear filtering, the Canny edge detector, the sampling theorem, the FIR filter, the periodogram, keyframe interpolation, the rigid body simulation problem and stable fluids. Do the exercises marked ◦ at the end of each chapter you touch.

If you already know the basic algorithms, start with the opening page of each chapter, which names its groups and the order to read them. Chapters 28.1 and 28.3 belong together, as do 28.5 and 28.6, and the animation chapters 28.8, 28.10 and 28.12 can be read in any order.

Each entry stands on its own; its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of each prerequisite.

## What you need first

Some linear algebra is needed throughout: vectors, dot and cross products, and $4 \times 4$ matrices on homogeneous coordinates. From Volume 1 you need the [word RAM](/word-ram/) and the [real RAM](/real-ram/), the two cost models of the entries. Volume 3 has the spatial trees behind visibility, ray tracing and [collision detection](/collision-detection/), such as the [kd-tree](/kd-tree/) and the [BSP tree](/bsp-tree/). Volume 11 has the [fast Fourier transform](/fast-fourier-transform/) and [convolution](/convolution/), which this volume uses and does not repeat. Volume 12 has the [floating-point model](/floating-point-model/), [bilinear interpolation](/bilinear-interpolation/), [Monte Carlo integration](/monte-carlo-integration/), and the numerics of the simulation chapters: the [Navier-Stokes equations](/navier-stokes-equations/), the [backward Euler method](/backward-euler-method/) and the [conjugate gradient method](/conjugate-gradient-method/). Volume 13 has the [orientation test](/orientation-test/) and [GJK](/gjk-algorithm/). Ray tracing rests on [importance sampling](/importance-sampling/) of Volume 17, the Kalman filter on the [state-space model](/state-space-model/) of Volume 25, rigid bodies on the [Newton-Euler equations](/newton-euler-equations/) of Volume 37, and particle fluids on [smoothed particle hydrodynamics](/smoothed-particle-hydrodynamics/) of Volume 43.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
