Smoke curling around a building, fire licking up a wall, a wave breaking over a ship's deck: in films and games these are simulated, not drawn. A fluid solver for animation has a different goal from one for engineering. Nobody measures the drag on the ship. What matters is that the motion looks right, that an artist can steer it, and that the solver survives the large time steps of a production schedule, often two or three grid cells of motion per frame. The algorithms of this chapter are built for that goal, and their entries say plainly what each one gives up for it.

Two choices run through the chapter. The first is where the fluid lives: on a fixed grid, on particles that move with the flow, or on both. The second is how the solver keeps the fluid from compressing. A grid solver projects the velocity onto a field with no divergence by solving a Poisson equation for the pressure each step. A particle solver keeps the density near its rest value, only approximately, by a stiff equation of state or by a few iterations. Neither is exact, and the speed, the noise and the blur of each method follow from these two choices.

## What the chapter covers

1. **The problem and the grid.** The [Eulerian and Lagrangian viewpoints](/eulerian-and-lagrangian-viewpoints/) and the material derivative that links them, the [problem of fluid simulation for animation](/fluid-simulation-for-animation/), and the [MAC grid](/mac-grid/), with pressure at cell centers and each velocity component on the faces normal to it.
2. **Grid solvers.** [Foster and Metaxas](/foster-metaxas-fluids/) animated liquids by explicit finite differences and a cell-by-cell pressure iteration. [Stable fluids](/stable-fluids/) advects by tracing back along the flow, diffuses implicitly and projects. It is stable for any time step, but first order and dissipative: stable is not accurate.
3. **Sharper advection.** [BFECC](/bfecc/) and the [unconditionally stable MacCormack method](/unconditionally-stable-maccormack/) estimate the error of a plain advection step by a round trip and correct it. They are second order for smooth fields and need a limiter at sharp edges.
4. **Smoke and fire.** [Boussinesq buoyancy](/boussinesq-buoyancy/) makes hot gas rise and soot sink, [vorticity confinement](/vorticity-confinement/) puts back the small swirls that dissipation removes, and the [Nguyen-Fedkiw-Jensen fire](/fire-simulation/) joins a fuel flow and a product flow at a thin core.
5. **Particles on a grid.** The [particle-in-cell method](/particle-in-cell/) carries velocity on particles and projects on the grid. PIC is dissipative, [FLIP](/fluid-implicit-particle/) keeps much more energy but is noisy, and [APIC](/affine-particle-in-cell/) stores an affine velocity on each particle and keeps angular momentum under the hypotheses of its paper.
6. **Liquid surfaces and solids.** The [particle level set](/particle-level-set/) repairs the volume loss of a level set with marker particles, [surface reconstruction from particles](/particle-fluid-surfacing/) builds a mesh to render, and [variational solid-fluid coupling](/variational-solid-fluid-coupling/) lets walls and bodies cut the grid.
7. **Particle fluids.** [Weakly compressible SPH](/weakly-compressible-sph/) lets the fluid compress by about 1% and takes small steps. [PCISPH](/pcisph/), [IISPH](/implicit-incompressible-sph/) and [DFSPH](/divergence-free-sph/) iterate on the pressure to allow larger steps, and [position based fluids](/position-based-fluids/) treat density as a constraint on positions. All of them enforce incompressibility only approximately.
8. **Water surfaces.** [Gerstner waves](/gerstner-waves/), [FFT ocean synthesis](/fft-ocean-synthesis/) and [height field water](/height-field-water/) animate a surface with no volume solver at all.

## A first reading

Read the Eulerian and Lagrangian viewpoints, the problem entry, the MAC grid and stable fluids, in that order; these four carry the rest of the chapter. Then read Boussinesq buoyancy and vorticity confinement for smoke, PIC and FLIP for liquids, and weakly compressible SPH and IISPH for particle fluids. Gerstner waves stand alone and make a good short read at any point.

Foster and Metaxas, the fire model, the advection schemes, the particle level set, surfacing, solid coupling, APIC, PCISPH, DFSPH and position based fluids can wait for a second pass. Each refines one step of a method you will already know.

## Before you start

The flow obeys the [Navier-Stokes equations](/navier-stokes-equations/) of Volume 12, and the pressure step solves a [Poisson equation](/poisson-equation/), usually by the [conjugate gradient method](/conjugate-gradient-method/) or by [Jacobi](/jacobi-method/) and [Gauss-Seidel](/gauss-seidel-method/) sweeps. Advection rests on the [advection equation](/advection-equation/), the [method of characteristics](/method-of-characteristics/) and [bilinear interpolation](/bilinear-interpolation/); the time steps use the [backward Euler](/backward-euler-method/) and [symplectic Euler](/symplectic-euler-method/) methods and the [CFL condition](/courant-friedrichs-lewy-condition/). Liquid surfaces use the [level set method](/level-set-method/). All of these are in Volume 12.

The particle solvers are built on [smoothed particle hydrodynamics](/smoothed-particle-hydrodynamics/) of Volume 43, which explains the kernel sums that they share; Volume 43 also has the [lattice Boltzmann method](/lattice-boltzmann-method/), which evolves particle populations on a lattice and recovers the Navier-Stokes equations at low Mach number. Ocean synthesis needs the [fast Fourier transform](/fast-fourier-transform/) of Volume 11, and surfacing uses [implicit surfaces](/implicit-surface/) and [marching cubes](/marching-cubes/) from Chapter 28.2.
