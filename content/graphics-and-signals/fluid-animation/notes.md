## History

The grid and the particle methods of this chapter both began at Los Alamos. Francis Harlow developed the particle-in-cell method there in the 1950s and early 1960s, and in 1965 Harlow and Welch published the marker-and-cell method, which put pressure at cell centers and velocities on cell faces and moved marker particles to show where the liquid was. Brackbill and Ruppel introduced FLIP in 1986. These were tools of computational physics, aimed at accuracy, and computer graphics took two more decades to adopt them.

Graphics first animated water as a surface. Fournier and Reeves, and Peachey, brought Gerstner's trochoidal wave of 1802 to SIGGRAPH 86, and Mastin, Watterberg and Mareda synthesized ocean scenes by filtering noise with an ocean spectrum and inverting it by FFT in 1987. Kass and Miller (1990) solved a linearized shallow water equation with implicit steps on a height field. Tessendorf's course notes *Simulating Ocean Water* (2001) gave the form of FFT ocean synthesis used today, with the time evolution by the dispersion relation.

Foster and Metaxas (1996) brought the marker-and-cell method into animation, with explicit steps and a pressure iteration cell by cell. Stam's *Stable Fluids* (1999) removed the step limit with semi-Lagrangian advection, implicit diffusion and a projection, and Fedkiw, Stam and Jensen (2001) moved the method to the MAC grid and added vorticity confinement, a force that Steinhoff and Underhill (1994) had proposed for vortex computations. In 2002 Nguyen, Fedkiw and Jensen animated fire with two flows joined at a thin core, and Enright, Fedkiw, Ferziger and Mitchell published the particle level set. Sharper advection followed: BFECC from Dupont and Liu (2003), used for fluids by Kim, Liu, Llamas and Rossignac (2007), and the unconditionally stable MacCormack method of Selle and colleagues (2008). Batty, Bertails and Bridson (2007) wrote the projection as an energy minimization to couple fluids with solids. Zhu and Bridson (2005) brought PIC and FLIP to liquids and sand, and Jiang, Schroeder, Selle, Teran and Stomakhin (2015) added the affine velocity of APIC.

Particle fluids came from astrophysics through Monaghan, who used the Tait equation for free surface flows in 1994. Becker and Teschner (2007) brought weakly compressible SPH to graphics. The search for larger steps then gave PCISPH (Solenthaler and Pajarola, 2009), position based fluids (Macklin and Müller, 2013), IISPH (Ihmsen and coauthors, 2014) and DFSPH (Bender and Koschier, 2015). Each keeps the density near the rest density by a cheaper or better iteration, and none enforces it exactly.

## Further reading

- Robert Bridson, *Fluid Simulation for Computer Graphics*, second edition (2015). The standard text on grid and hybrid solvers: the MAC grid, projection, advection, level sets, FLIP and solid coupling.
- Jos Stam, *Stable Fluids*, Proceedings of SIGGRAPH 99. A short paper that is still the clearest statement of the method.
- Jerry Tessendorf, *Simulating Ocean Water*, SIGGRAPH course notes (2001). The derivation and the practice of FFT ocean synthesis, with Gerstner waves and choppy displacement.
- Dan Koschier, Jan Bender, Barbara Solenthaler and Matthias Teschner, *Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics Techniques for the Physics Based Simulation of Fluids and Solids*, Eurographics tutorial (2019). A survey of WCSPH, PCISPH, IISPH, DFSPH and their boundary handling, in one notation.
- Ronald Fedkiw, Jos Stam and Henrik Wann Jensen, *Visual Simulation of Smoke*, Proceedings of SIGGRAPH 2001. The step from stable fluids to production smoke, with buoyancy and vorticity confinement.
