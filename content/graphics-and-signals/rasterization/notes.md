## History

Raster graphics began with pen plotters and scanned displays. Jack Bresenham, at IBM, published his line algorithm in 1965 in the IBM Systems Journal, to drive a digital plotter whose pen moves in one of eight directions per step. Michael Pitteway extended the idea to conic sections in 1967, and Bresenham gave an algorithm for circular arcs in 1977. Jerry Van Aken treated ellipses in 1984, and Douglas McIlroy's 1992 paper "Getting raster ellipses right" settled the details of the integer method. Robert Sproull showed in 1982 that Bresenham's algorithm follows from the plain rounding algorithm by program transformations, and in 1987 Bresenham wrote about the ties and the reversal of lines. The word pixel first appeared in print in 1965, and Alvy Ray Smith's 1995 memo "A pixel is not a little square" gave the sampling view that the entries take.

Clipping came from flight simulators. The Cohen-Sutherland outcode method dates from 1967 and became known through Newman and Sproull's textbook of 1973; Sproull and Sutherland built "A clipping divider" in hardware in 1968. Sutherland and Hodgman published reentrant polygon clipping in 1974, Cyrus and Beck the parametric clipper against convex regions in 1978, and Liang and Barsky its fast rectangle form in 1984. Blinn and Newell showed in 1978 that clipping belongs before the division by $w$, in homogeneous coordinates.

Scanline methods for filling and hidden surfaces grew at the University of Utah in the late 1960s, starting with the work of Wylie, Romney, Evans and Erdahl in 1967 and Watkins in 1970. Henri Gouraud published interpolated shading in 1971 and Bui Tuong Phong normal interpolation in 1975, and James Blinn added the half vector in 1977. Edwin Catmull's 1974 thesis introduced texture mapping, Lance Williams described the mipmap in "Pyramidal parametrics" in 1983, and Paul Heckbert surveyed texture mapping in 1986. Porter and Duff gave the compositing algebra in 1984, building on the alpha channel that Catmull and Smith had introduced.

Juan Pineda's 1988 paper made the edge function the basis of parallel triangle rasterization; GPU rasterizers still work this way, with the top-left rule as the usual tie rule. Molnar, Cox, Ellsworth and Fuchs classified parallel renderers as sort-first, sort-middle and sort-last in 1994. Antialiasing has a parallel history: Franklin Crow analyzed aliasing in 1977, Loren Carpenter's A-buffer appeared in 1984, Robert Cook introduced stochastic sampling in 1986, Xiaolin Wu published his line algorithm in 1991, and Kurt Akeley's RealityEngine of 1993 brought multisampling to hardware. Alexander Reshetov's morphological antialiasing of 2009 and Brian Karis's 2014 account of temporal antialiasing in Unreal Engine 4 mark the move to post-process and temporal methods.

## Further reading

- James D. Foley, Andries van Dam, Steven K. Feiner and John F. Hughes, *Computer Graphics: Principles and Practice*, 2nd ed. (Addison-Wesley, 1990). The classic treatment of scan conversion, clipping, filling and antialiasing, with derivations of the integer algorithms.
- Tomas Akenine-Möller, Eric Haines, Naty Hoffman, Angelo Pesce, Michał Iwanicki and Sébastien Hillaire, *Real-Time Rendering*, 4th ed. (CRC Press, 2018). The pipeline, texturing and antialiasing as current GPUs do them.
- Steve Marschner and Peter Shirley, *Fundamentals of Computer Graphics*, 4th ed. (CRC Press). A clear first course that covers rasterization, shading and texture mapping together.
- Matt Pharr, Wenzel Jakob and Greg Humphreys, *Physically Based Rendering: From Theory to Implementation*, 3rd ed. (Morgan Kaufmann, 2016). The best source on sampling and reconstruction filters, from the side of image synthesis.
- Paul S. Heckbert, "Survey of texture mapping", IEEE Computer Graphics and Applications (1986). A short survey of mapping and filtering that still explains the trade-offs well.
