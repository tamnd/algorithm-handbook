## History

The attack came first. In 1988 the Morris worm spread partly through a stack buffer overflow in `fingerd`, which read its request with `gets` (Spafford 1989). Stack smashing was described in public in the mid-1990s, and the first general answers followed. Cowan et al. built StackGuard in 1998, which put a canary word before the return address, and Etoh's ProPolice put arrays next to the canary and scalars below them; GCC and Microsoft compilers later made the check standard. In 1997 Solar Designer released a patch that made the Linux stack non-executable, and in the same year showed the reply to it, the return-to-libc attack. PaX made all data non-executable in 2000 and added address space layout randomization in 2001. Hardware no-execute bits on x86, OpenBSD W^X and Windows DEP made the policy standard in 2003 and 2004.

Then the mitigations were measured. Shacham, Page, Pfaff, Goh, Modadugu and Boneh (CCS 2004) found the 16-bit library offset of a forking server under PaX in 216 seconds on average. Shacham (CCS 2007) showed that short instruction sequences ending in a return, found in the C library on x86, form a Turing-complete set, and return-oriented programming became the standard way past non-executable data. Abadi, Budiu, Erlingsson and Ligatti (CCS 2005) answered with control-flow integrity, which they proved sound under three assumptions and paired with a protected shadow call stack. Cheaper and coarser forms of CFI followed, and then attacks on them: Carlini, Barresi, Payer, Wagner and Gross (USENIX Security 2015) showed that even fully precise static CFI leaves room, and Hu et al. (IEEE S&P 2016) built Turing-complete attacks from data alone, extending the non-control-data attacks of Chen et al. (2005). Kuznetsov, Szekeres, Payer, Candea, Sekar and Song (OSDI 2014) chose to protect only the code pointers, with code-pointer integrity.

Checking every access is older than most mitigations. Austin, Breach and Sohi (PLDI 1994) gave each pointer its bounds and a capability number. Jones and Kelly (AADEBUG 1997) kept the layout of pointers and looked objects up by address instead, and Ruwase and Lam's CRED (NDSS 2004) and Akritidis, Costa, Castro and Hand's baggy bounds (USENIX Security 2009) made that approach usable on real programs. Nagarakatte, Zhao, Martin and Zdancewic moved per-pointer bounds into a disjoint table in SoftBound (PLDI 2009) and added temporal checks with keys and locks in CETS (ISMM 2010). Shadow memory, used by Purify in the early 1990s and by Nethercote and Seward's Memcheck (VEE 2007), became the base of AddressSanitizer (Serebryany, Bruening, Potapenko and Vyukov, USENIX ATC 2012), cheap enough for routine testing.

Isolation and hardware support run alongside. Wahbe, Lucco, Anderson and Graham (SOSP 1993) introduced software fault isolation with segment matching and address sandboxing, and Native Client brought it to x86 code in a browser in 2009. Woodruff et al. (ISCA 2014) described the CHERI capability model, which puts bounds and permissions in every pointer and checks them in hardware. Arm added 4-bit memory tags for each 16-byte granule in Armv8.5-A, and Intel CET put a shadow stack in processors from 2020. Lee et al. (NDSS 2015) and Younan (NDSS 2015) attacked use after free from the other side, by finding and clearing the dangling pointers.

## Further reading

- Laszlo Szekeres, Mathias Payer, Tao Wei and Dawn Song, "SoK: Eternal war in memory", IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy 2013. A map of the steps from a memory error to an exploit, with each defense placed at the step it breaks.
- Arthur Azevedo de Amorim, Catalin Hritcu and Benjamin C. Pierce, "The meaning of memory safety", POST 2018. A formal definition of memory safety through the reasoning principles it supports.
- Nathan Burow et al., "Control-flow integrity: precision, security, and performance", ACM Computing Surveys 2017. Compares later CFI designs by precision and cost.
- Nathan Burow, Xinping Zhang and Mathias Payer, "SoK: Shining light on shadow stacks", IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy 2019. Shadow stack designs, attacks and costs side by side.
- Martín Abadi, Mihai Budiu, Úlfar Erlingsson and Jay Ligatti, "Control-flow integrity principles, implementations, and applications", ACM Transactions on Information and System Security 2009. The full CFI paper, with the proof of the guarantee.
