## History

Paul Kocher opened the subject in 1996 with *Timing attacks on implementations of Diffie-Hellman, RSA, DSS, and other systems*, at CRYPTO, where a statistical test on running times gives the private exponent bit by bit and the countermeasures of constant work and blinding are already named. In 1999 Kocher, Joshua Jaffe and Benjamin Jun published *Differential power analysis*, which applied the same statistics to the power drawn by a smart card. In the same years the active side appeared: Boneh, DeMillo and Lipton (1997) broke RSA with the Chinese remainder theorem from a faulty signature, and Biham and Shamir (1997) named differential fault analysis for block ciphers. David Brumley and Dan Boneh showed in 2003 that a timing attack on OpenSSL works across a network.

Power analysis became a field with its own statistics. Chari, Rao and Rohatgi gave the template attack in 2002, Brier, Clavier and Olivier correlation power analysis in 2004, and Gierlichs, Batina, Tuyls and Preneel mutual information analysis in 2008. Standaert, Malkin and Yung (2009) fixed the vocabulary of success rate and guessing entropy, and Veyrat-Charvillon, Gérard and Standaert (2013) and Glowacz et al. (2015) turned the scores of an attack into bounds on the rank of the key. For laboratories, Goodwill, Jun, Jaffe and Rohatgi proposed in 2011 the fixed-versus-random t-test now called TVLA.

Masking has a parallel history. Chari, Jutla, Rao and Rohatgi (1999) showed that splitting a value into shares makes the number of traces grow exponentially in the number of shares under Gaussian noise. Ishai, Sahai and Wagner (2003) defined the probing model and private circuits; Nikova, Rechberger and Rijmen (2006) gave threshold implementations, which stay secure in hardware with glitches; Rivain and Prouff (2010) brought the share count down to $t + 1$, and Coron et al. (2013) found the composition flaw that led to the notions of non-interference and strong non-interference of Barthe et al. (2016).

On processors, Bernstein (2005), Percival (2005) and Osvik, Shamir and Tromer (2006) showed that caches leak the table indices of AES and the exponent windows of RSA, and Yarom and Falkner's Flush+Reload of 2014 read a GnuPG key across cores. Kim et al. found the DRAM disturbance errors now called Rowhammer in 2014. In 2018 Meltdown (Lipp et al.) and Spectre (Kocher et al., published at IEEE S&P 2019) showed that work the processor discards still leaves secrets in the cache. On the program side, Almeida, Barbosa, Barthe, Dupressoir and Emmi reduced constant-time verification to a product program in 2016.

Lengths are a channel too. Kelsey (2002) observed that compression before encryption leaks, and the CRIME (2012) and BREACH (2013) attacks read web session tokens through it; TLS 1.3 dropped compression. Website fingerprinting against Tor matured with Wang, Cai, Nithyanand, Johnson and Goldberg (2014), and Juarez et al. (2014) showed how much the reported accuracy depends on the base rate and on the conditions of the experiment.

## Further reading

- Paul C. Kocher, *Timing attacks on implementations of Diffie-Hellman, RSA, DSS, and other systems*, CRYPTO 1996. The original attack and its countermeasures, in a few pages.
- Stefan Mangard, Elisabeth Oswald and Thomas Popp, *Power Analysis Attacks: Revealing the Secrets of Smart Cards*, Springer, 2007. The textbook on the statistics of power analysis and on masking, with the leakage models used in this chapter.
- Yuval Ishai, Amit Sahai and David Wagner, *Private circuits: securing hardware against probing attacks*, CRYPTO 2003. The probing model and the masked AND gate.
- Dag Arne Osvik, Adi Shamir and Eran Tromer, *Cache attacks and countermeasures: the case of AES*, CT-RSA 2006. The standard methods for measuring cache use from another process.
- Claudio Canella and others, *A systematic evaluation of transient execution attacks and defenses*, USENIX Security Symposium 2019. A classification of the Meltdown and Spectre variants with the defenses that cover each.
