A cryptographic algorithm can be secure on paper and still give its key away when it runs. The device that runs it uses time, draws power, warms up, fills caches and radiates, and each of these can depend on the secret. A side-channel attack measures such a quantity and works back to the key. The first and most widely used channel is time: in 1996 Paul Kocher recovered private exponents of RSA and Diffie-Hellman from the running times of the operations, and in 2003 David Brumley and Dan Boneh did the same over a network, against a web server that used OpenSSL.

The lesson for an implementer is that the cost of a secret computation must not depend on the secret. A left-to-right square and multiply that does an extra multiplication for every 1 bit of the exponent tells the bits to anyone who can see the cost of each step. A table lookup at an index derived from a key tells the index to anyone who shares the cache. A comparison that stops at the first wrong byte tells how many bytes were right. Each of these saves a little work, and each is a leak.

## What the chapter covers

1. **Timing attacks.** The [timing attack](/timing-attack/) models a device as a function $T(c) = f(c, d) + \text{noise}$ of the input $c$ and the secret $d$. For [modular exponentiation](/modular-exponentiation/) by square and multiply, one stopwatch reading gives the Hamming weight of $d$, and the cost of each step gives all of $d$. Kocher's statistical attack needs neither: it predicts, bit by bit, which inputs make some step slow and tests the prediction against the measured times. The extra reduction of [Montgomery multiplication](/montgomery-multiplication/) supplies such a step, which is what Brumley and Boneh used. The entry orders the fixes: remove the secret from the control flow with a Montgomery ladder, remove it from the addresses, blind the input with [RSA blinding](/rsa-blinding/), make the error path uniform, and measure the final binary.

The other topics of the chapter are not written yet. They are the side-channel attack as a general model; power analysis, with simple, differential and correlation power analysis, template attacks, mutual information analysis, key rank estimation and test vector leakage assessment; cache and microarchitectural attacks, with Flush+Reload, Prime+Probe, the Spectre and Meltdown attacks and Rowhammer; fault attacks, with differential fault analysis; leaks through compression and website fingerprinting; and the defences, with constant-time programming and its verification, Boolean masking, the probing model, the private circuits of Ishai, Sahai and Wagner, and threshold implementations.

## A first reading

Read the [timing attack](/timing-attack/) in full, with its toy device and its two tables of counts. Then read the defences it links: the Montgomery ladder in [binary exponentiation](/binary-exponentiation/), [RSA blinding](/rsa-blinding/), and the [padding oracle attack](/padding-oracle-attack/), where a fast rejection becomes an oracle. [X25519](/x25519/) shows a primitive designed so that its natural implementation does the same work for every key. The [RSA fault attack](/rsa-fault-attack/) on the Chinese remainder method is the active counterpart: the attacker disturbs the computation instead of watching it.

## Before you start

You need [modular exponentiation](/modular-exponentiation/) and Montgomery multiplication from Volume 10, and the [RSA cryptosystem](/rsa-cryptosystem/) and [Diffie-Hellman key exchange](/diffie-hellman/) from Volume 23. The statistical part uses only means and standard deviations: the standard error of a mean of $k$ independent measurements falls as $1/\sqrt{k}$. [Noninterference](/noninterference/), in Chapter 45.1, is the matching definition for programs; constant-time code is noninterference with the running time counted as a public output.
