A smart card computes $y = S(p \oplus k)$ for a 4-bit key $k$, where $S$ is the S-box of the PRESENT cipher and $p$ is a plaintext nibble that the attacker chooses. The card draws a little more current when the value $y$ has more 1 bits. The attacker feeds it the 16 nibbles $p = 0, 1, \dots, 15$, records the 16 power readings, and for each of the 16 guesses $g$ of the key computes the Hamming weight of $S(p \oplus g)$ and its correlation with the readings. With the key $k = \mathrm{B}$ and no noise, the right guess has correlation exactly 1 and the strongest wrong guess $-0.75$. The cipher was never attacked; its implementation was measured.

That is the subject of the chapter. A device that runs a secure algorithm uses time, draws power, fills caches and sends packets of some length, and each of these quantities can depend on the secret. An attack is a statistical experiment on such measurements, so the entries quote the number of traces and the success rate of an attack only as measured, together with the device or the simulated leakage model. A defense is proved only in a model, and the model says what it leaves out: constant-time code says nothing about power, and masking proved secure against $t$ probes says nothing about glitches unless the scheme handles them.

## What the chapter covers

1. **Leakage and the first attacks.** The [side-channel attack](/side-channel-attack/) entry sets up the leakage function and divide and conquer. The [timing attack](/timing-attack/) reads an RSA exponent from running times, and [simple power analysis](/simple-power-analysis/) reads it from one trace.
2. **Statistical power analysis.** [Differential power analysis](/differential-power-analysis/), [correlation power analysis](/correlation-power-analysis/), [mutual information analysis](/mutual-information-analysis/) and the [template attack](/template-attack/), from the weakest assumptions on the attacker to the strongest.
3. **Masking.** [Boolean masking](/boolean-masking/), the [probing model](/probing-model/) that defines its security, the [ISW private circuits](/isw-private-circuits/) that compute on shares, and the [threshold implementation](/threshold-implementation/) for hardware with glitches.
4. **Evaluating a device.** [Test vector leakage assessment](/test-vector-leakage-assessment/) and [key rank estimation](/side-channel-key-rank-estimation/).
5. **Cache channels.** [Flush+Reload](/flush-and-reload/) and [Prime+Probe](/prime-and-probe/).
6. **Constant-time code.** [Constant-time programming](/constant-time-programming/) and [constant-time verification](/constant-time-verification/).
7. **Transient execution.** The [Meltdown attack](/meltdown-attack/) and the [Spectre attack](/spectre-attack/).
8. **Faults.** [Differential fault analysis](/differential-fault-analysis/) and [Rowhammer](/rowhammer/).
9. **Lengths and traffic.** The [compression side channel](/compression-side-channel/) and [website fingerprinting](/website-fingerprinting/).

## A first reading

Start with the [side-channel attack](/side-channel-attack/) entry: its leakage model $\ell = \varepsilon\,\mathrm{HW}(v) + N$ is used by most of the others. Then read the [timing attack](/timing-attack/) and [simple power analysis](/simple-power-analysis/), which need only square and multiply. [Differential power analysis](/differential-power-analysis/) and [correlation power analysis](/correlation-power-analysis/) work through the example above, with tables of scores and measured success rates. [Boolean masking](/boolean-masking/) and the [probing model](/probing-model/) are the main defense against them, and [test vector leakage assessment](/test-vector-leakage-assessment/) is how laboratories look for what remains. For software, read [constant-time programming](/constant-time-programming/), then [Flush+Reload](/flush-and-reload/) to see what it prevents and the [Spectre attack](/spectre-attack/) to see what it does not. [Differential fault analysis](/differential-fault-analysis/) shows the active side. That is ten entries; the rest of the chapter refines them.

## Before you start

From Volume 10 you need [modular exponentiation](/modular-exponentiation/) and [binary exponentiation](/binary-exponentiation/), whose square and multiply loop is the first target. From Volume 23 you need the [block cipher](/block-cipher/), the [S-box](/s-box/) and [secret sharing](/secret-sharing/), and for faults [differential cryptanalysis](/differential-cryptanalysis/). The [Hamming weight](/hamming-weight/) in Volume 24 is the usual leakage model, and [Shannon entropy](/shannon-entropy/) from the same volume is used by mutual information analysis. The statistics come from the [Welch t-test](/welch-t-test/) in Volume 33 and from [maximum likelihood estimation](/maximum-likelihood-estimation/) and the [covariance matrix](/covariance-matrix/) in Volume 25. The cache attacks assume the [set-associative cache](/set-associative-cache/) of Volume 26, and constant-time verification builds on [self-composition](/self-composition/) in Volume 34 and the [safety property](/safety-property/) in Volume 16. [Bit manipulation](/bit-manipulation/) in Volume 2 supplies the mask tricks of constant-time code. In this volume, [noninterference](/noninterference/) in Chapter 45.1 is the matching definition for programs: constant-time code is noninterference with the trace of branches and addresses counted as a public output.
