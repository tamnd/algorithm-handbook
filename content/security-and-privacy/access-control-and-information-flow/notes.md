## History

Access control took its shape in the time-sharing systems of the 1960s and early 1970s. Dennis and Van Horn defined capabilities in 1966. Lampson's paper "Protection" (1971) gave the access matrix and showed that access lists and capability lists are its columns and rows. Anderson's planning study for the US Air Force (1972) named the reference monitor, and Saltzer and Schroeder's "The protection of information in computer systems" (1975) set out least privilege and the other design principles that are still quoted.

The military need for multilevel systems led to the formal models. Bell and LaPadula wrote their model at MITRE from 1973, with the unified exposition in 1976, and Biba gave its integrity dual in 1977. In 1976 Harrison, Ruzzo and Ullman proved that safety of a general protection system is undecidable, and in the same year Jones, Lipton and Snyder gave the Take-Grant model and a linear time test for it. McLean's papers of 1985 and 1987 showed that the basic security theorem says less than it seems to. Commercial policies came next: Clark and Wilson in 1987 and Brewer and Nash, with the Chinese Wall, in 1989. Role-based access control was fixed in the form used today by Sandhu, Coyne, Feinstein and Youman in 1996, the year Blaze, Feigenbaum and Lacy started trust management.

Information flow has its own line. Lampson posed the confinement problem in 1973. Denning's lattice model (1976) and the certification method of Denning and Denning (1977) treated flow through a program, implicit flows included. Goguen and Meseguer defined noninterference in 1982. Kemmerer's shared resource matrix (1982, journal version 1983) gave a method for finding storage and timing channels, and the Trusted Computer System Evaluation Criteria of 1985 asked for covert channel analysis at the higher classes. Volpano, Irvine and Smith proved in 1996 that a simple type system ensures noninterference, and Myers and Liskov gave the decentralized label model in 1997. Sabelfeld and Myers surveyed the field in 2003.

Since 2000 the questions have been about what can be enforced and how much leaks. Schneider characterized the policies that execution monitors enforce (2000). Askarov, Hunt, Sabelfeld and Sands bounded what termination-insensitive noninterference lets through (2008). Clarkson and Schneider defined hyperproperties (2010), building on the 2-safety view of Terauchi and Aiken (2005). Dynamic enforcement grew with Austin and Flanagan's no-sensitive-upgrade monitor (2009), secure multi-execution by Devriese and Piessens (2010) and faceted execution (2012). Smith's min-entropy leakage (2009) and the g-leakage of Alvim, Chatzikokolakis, Palamidessi and Smith (2012) gave the quantitative theory its present form.

## Further reading

- Matt Bishop, *Computer Security: Art and Science*, second edition, Addison-Wesley (2018). A textbook that covers the access control models, HRU and Take-Grant with full proofs.
- Andrei Sabelfeld and Andrew C. Myers, "Language-based information-flow security", *IEEE Journal on Selected Areas in Communications* 21(1) (2003). The standard survey of type systems and semantics for noninterference.
- Fred B. Schneider, "Enforceable security policies", *ACM Transactions on Information and System Security* 3(1) (2000). The paper that defines security automata and proves that monitors enforce only safety properties.
- Michael R. Clarkson and Fred B. Schneider, "Hyperproperties", *Journal of Computer Security* 18(6) (2010). Sets of traces as the setting for noninterference and the k-safety view.
- Mario S. Alvim, Konstantinos Chatzikokolakis, Annabelle McIver, Carroll Morgan, Catuscia Palamidessi and Geoffrey Smith, *The Science of Quantitative Information Flow*, Springer (2020). A book on channels, gain functions and leakage bounds.
