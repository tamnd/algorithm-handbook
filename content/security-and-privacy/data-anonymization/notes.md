## History

Disclosure control began in national statistical offices, which publish tables and samples of microdata and must not reveal the answers of one respondent. Cox (1980) gave the first method for complementary cell suppression in tables with totals. Dalenius and Reiss (1982) proposed data swapping, which exchanges values between records so that chosen counts are kept. Later work at Statistics Netherlands produced the post-randomization method: Kooiman, Willenborg and Gouweleeuw proposed it in 1997, and Gouweleeuw, Kooiman, Willenborg and de Wolf gave its theory and the invariant form in 1998. Microaggregation, with groups of at least $k$ similar records replaced by their mean, came from the same tradition; MDAV was first used in the mu-Argus software, and Domingo-Ferrer and Torra described its generic form in 2005.

In computer science, the turning point was the work of Sweeney. She described the Datafly system for medical data in 1997, and in 2002 she published how a release of medical data without names had been linked to a voter list on ZIP code, birth date and sex. Her 2002 papers set out k-anonymity as a model and gave the Datafly algorithm with its analysis. Samarati defined minimal generalization over the lattice of generalization vectors and gave the binary search on the height in 2001, after a 1998 report with Sweeney. Search methods followed: Incognito (LeFevre, DeWitt and Ramakrishnan, SIGMOD 2005), the exact search of Bayardo and Agrawal (ICDE 2005), and the multidimensional Mondrian partitioning (LeFevre, DeWitt and Ramakrishnan, ICDE 2006). Meyerson and Williams (PODS 2004) showed that optimal k-anonymity is NP-hard and gave an $O(k \log k)$ approximation, and Aggarwal et al. (ICDT 2005) improved the ratio to $O(k)$.

Weaknesses of k-anonymity led to stronger syntactic conditions. Machanavajjhala, Gehrke, Kifer and Venkitasubramaniam (ICDE 2006) showed the homogeneity and background knowledge attacks and proposed l-diversity. Li, Li and Venkatasubramanian (ICDE 2007) showed the skewness and similarity attacks on l-diversity and proposed t-closeness with the earth mover's distance. Xiao and Tao gave anatomy (VLDB 2006), which keeps the exact quasi-identifiers, and m-invariance (SIGMOD 2007) for tables that are published again after changes. Terrovitis, Mamoulis and Kalnis (PVLDB 2008) carried the idea to set-valued data with $k^m$-anonymity.

Attacks on other kinds of data came at the same time. Backstrom, Dwork and Kleinberg (WWW 2007) showed that an attacker who plants a few accounts can find people in a published graph with no names. Narayanan and Shmatikov de-anonymized the Netflix Prize ratings (IEEE S&P 2008) and large social networks by their structure (IEEE S&P 2009). Liu and Terzi (SIGMOD 2008) answered with k-degree anonymization. Gruteser and Grunwald (MobiSys 2003) brought k-anonymity to location services, and Schnell, Bachteler and Reiher (2009) encoded names in Bloom filters for record linkage without plain identifiers; later work found attacks on that encoding. The US Census Bureau, which had used household swapping in the 1990, 2000 and 2010 censuses, changed to [differential privacy](/differential-privacy-definition/) for 2020, whose bound on what a release says about one person holds whatever the attacker knows from elsewhere; Chapter 45.8 treats it.

## Further reading

- Anco Hundepool, Josep Domingo-Ferrer, Luisa Franconi, Sarah Giessing, Eric Schulte Nordholt, Keith Spicer and Peter-Paul de Wolf, *Statistical Disclosure Control*, Wiley, 2012. The handbook of the statistical offices, covering microdata methods, tables, sensitivity rules and cell suppression.
- Leon Willenborg and Ton de Waal, *Elements of Statistical Disclosure Control*, Lecture Notes in Statistics 155, Springer, 2001. A compact and more mathematical account of the same field.
- Benjamin C. M. Fung, Ke Wang, Rui Chen and Philip S. Yu, "Privacy-preserving data publishing: a survey of recent developments", ACM Computing Surveys 42(4), 2010. A survey of the computer science line, from k-anonymity to its successors, with the attack models side by side.
- Valentina Ciriani, Sabrina De Capitani di Vimercati, Sara Foresti and Pierangela Samarati, "k-Anonymity", in *Secure Data Management in Decentralized Systems*, Springer, 2007. A careful account of generalization, suppression and the algorithms that search the lattice.
