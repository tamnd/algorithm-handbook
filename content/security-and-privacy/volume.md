A program that checks a password compares the guess with the stored value byte by byte and stops at the first difference. The time it takes tells an attacker how many leading bytes were right, and measured attempts recover the password one byte at a time. A second program never prints its secret, yet copies one bit of it into a public variable inside a branch. A third publishes a table of hospital visits with names removed, and a join with a voter list on ZIP code, birth date and sex puts the names back. A fourth publishes only a model trained on the records, and its answers tell who was in the data. None has a bug in the usual sense. All of them leak, and security is the study of such leaks and of the algorithms that prevent, detect or bound them.

This volume collects the algorithms and models of computer security and privacy: who may do what with data and how information flows through programs, how memory errors are stopped and found, how secrets leak through time, power and caches, how networks are attacked and defended, how intrusions and malware are detected, how data are released without exposing the people in them, and how learned models are attacked and defended. It has nine chapters and 277 entries, all of them written in this edition.

## Why security gets a volume of its own

A few ideas run through the whole volume:

- **An adversary, not a distribution.** Most of the handbook assumes inputs from a user or from a known distribution. Here the inputs are chosen by someone who knows the algorithm and wants it to fail, so every claim names its threat model: what the attacker sees, controls and can compute. For a learned model it says whether the attacker sees the weights, the scores or only the labels, which changes are allowed, such as an $\ell_p$ ball, and whether the attack comes at training or at test time.
- **Safety questions are often undecidable.** Whether a right can ever leak in the [Harrison-Ruzzo-Ullman model](/harrison-ruzzo-ullman-model/) is [undecidable](/hru-safety-undecidability/) in general; the [take-grant model](/take-grant-model/) was built so that its safety question takes linear time. By Cohen's theorem, no program decides for every program whether it is a [virus](/virus-detection-undecidability/).
- **Properties of many runs.** [Noninterference](/noninterference/) compares two runs that differ only in secrets, so it is a [hyperproperty](/hyperproperty/), and by the [theorem on execution monitors](/em-enforceable-policies-theorem/) a monitor that watches one run enforces only safety properties. A [security type system](/security-type-system/) is sound for termination-insensitive noninterference and rejects some secure programs.
- **Detect, prevent, mitigate.** [AddressSanitizer](/addresssanitizer/) detects an unsafe access of its class; [control-flow integrity](/control-flow-integrity/) prevents jumps outside a static graph but not the memory error; a [stack canary](/stack-canary/) or [address space layout randomization](/address-space-layout-randomization/) only makes an attack fail with high probability, and an information leak removes even that.
- **Each model proves only its own statement.** The [basic security theorem](/basic-security-theorem/) of [Bell-LaPadula](/bell-lapadula-model/) is about the simple security and star properties of its model and says nothing about [covert channels](/covert-channel/). [k-anonymity](/k-anonymity/) stops linkage on the quasi-identifiers, but not the disclosure of a sensitive value shared by a whole group.
- **A guarantee about the mechanism, not the output.** A mechanism is [differentially private](/differential-privacy-definition/) if adding or removing one record changes the probability of every set of outputs by at most a factor $e^{\varepsilon}$; the [approximate form](/approximate-differential-privacy/) adds $\delta$, which must be much smaller than $1/n$. It holds whatever else the attacker knows, and it hides only what depends on one person's record, not what the data reveal about a population. The losses add up: [basic composition](/sequential-composition-theorem/) adds the epsilons, and the [advanced composition theorem](/advanced-composition-theorem/) bounds the total for $k$ runs by $\varepsilon\sqrt{2k \ln(1/\delta')} + k\varepsilon(e^{\varepsilon} - 1)$, with $k\delta + \delta'$ in place of $\delta$.
- **Certified and empirical defenses.** [Randomized smoothing](/randomized-smoothing/) certifies an $\ell_2$ radius for the smoothed classifier, not the base one, with a stated failure probability. Empirical defenses are tested against stated attacks, and several fell to adaptive ones: in the study behind [BPDA](/backward-pass-differentiable-approximation/), 7 of 9 defenses relied on obfuscated gradients, and the new attacks went around 6 of them completely and 1 in part.
- **Measured, not proved.** [Fuzzing](/fuzz-testing/) finds bugs and never proves their absence. Detection rates, coverage counts, numbers of traces, re-identification rates and robust accuracies are measurements on the programs, devices, data and models the entries name. Because attacks are rare, the [base-rate fallacy](/base-rate-fallacy-intrusion-detection/) makes most alarms false unless the false alarm rate is tiny.

## The chapters

**Chapter 45.1, Access control and information flow** (43 entries), states who may do what. It starts from the [reference monitor](/reference-monitor/) and the [access control matrix](/access-control-matrix/) and covers [role-based access control](/role-based-access-control/), the policy models of Bell-LaPadula, its integrity dual [Biba](/biba-integrity-model/) and the [Chinese Wall](/chinese-wall-model/), and the safety of protection systems. Information flow runs from [implicit flows](/implicit-flow/) to noninterference, [declassification](/declassification/) and [min-entropy leakage](/min-entropy-leakage/).

**Chapter 45.2, Memory safety defenses** (25 entries), starts from [memory safety](/memory-safety/), the [buffer overflow](/buffer-overflow/) and [stack smashing](/stack-smashing/). It covers canaries, [data execution prevention](/data-execution-prevention/) and [shadow stacks](/shadow-stack/), the code reuse of [return-oriented programming](/return-oriented-programming/) and the [derandomization attack](/aslr-derandomization-attack/) on ASLR, control-flow integrity and [data-oriented programming](/data-oriented-programming/), which stays inside it, bounds checking by [fat pointers](/fat-pointer/) and [shadow memory](/shadow-memory/), and temporal safety down to [memory tagging](/memory-tagging/) and [CHERI](/cheri-capability-model/).

**Chapter 45.3, Fuzzing and vulnerability discovery** (19 entries), finds the inputs that break programs: [mutation-based fuzzing](/mutation-based-fuzzing/), the [edge coverage bitmap](/edge-coverage-bitmap/) of greybox fuzzers, [hybrid fuzzing](/hybrid-fuzzing/), [crash bucketing](/crash-bucketing/), static detection by [range analysis](/buffer-overrun-detection-by-range-analysis/) and models of the discovery process itself, from the [species model](/stads-residual-risk/) and its Good-Turing estimate to the measured [exponential cost](/exponential-cost-of-vulnerability-discovery/) of each new bug.

**Chapter 45.4, Side channels** (23 entries), treats attacks as statistical experiments on time, power, caches and lengths. It goes from the [timing attack](/timing-attack/) to [differential](/differential-power-analysis/) and [correlation power analysis](/correlation-power-analysis/), defends with [Boolean masking](/boolean-masking/) and [threshold implementations](/threshold-implementation/), evaluates devices by [TVLA](/test-vector-leakage-assessment/), which detects leakage and does not prove its absence, and covers [Flush+Reload](/flush-and-reload/), [constant-time programming](/constant-time-programming/), [Spectre](/spectre-attack/), [Rowhammer](/rowhammer/) and [website fingerprinting](/website-fingerprinting/).

**Chapter 45.5, Network and web security** (25 entries), covers the [same-origin policy](/same-origin-policy/), [cross-site scripting](/cross-site-scripting/) and [SQL injection](/sql-injection/) with their defenses, [SYN flooding](/syn-flooding/) and [SYN cookies](/syn-cookies/), [IP traceback](/ip-traceback-problem/), [BGP prefix hijacking](/bgp-prefix-hijacking/), [DNS cache poisoning](/dns-cache-poisoning/), password guessing models and the [Sybil attack](/sybil-attack/).

**Chapter 45.6, Intrusion and malware detection** (34 entries), starts from the [intrusion detection](/intrusion-detection-problem/) and [anomaly detection](/anomaly-detection-problem/) problems, the base-rate fallacy and the undecidability of virus detection. It covers [misuse detection](/misuse-detection/), [system call sequences](/system-call-sequence-detection/), the [threshold random walk](/threshold-random-walk-scan-detection/) for scanners, outlier detectors such as the [isolation forest](/isolation-forest/), [disassembly](/recursive-traversal-disassembly/) and [attack graphs](/attack-graph/).

**Chapter 45.7, Anonymization and disclosure control** (28 entries), releases data about people with syntactic conditions. A [quasi-identifier](/quasi-identifier/) enables a [linkage attack](/linkage-attack/); k-anonymity, [l-diversity](/l-diversity/) and [t-closeness](/t-closeness/) answer it, each with known weaknesses. Optimal k-anonymity is [NP-hard](/k-anonymity-np-hardness/) for $k \ge 3$, with an [$O(k)$ approximation](/k-anonymity-approximation/) and heuristics such as [Mondrian](/mondrian-anonymization/). The chapter also covers [microaggregation](/microaggregation/), [cell suppression](/cell-suppression-problem/) and the re-identification of [sparse data](/sparse-dataset-deanonymization/).

**Chapter 45.8, Differential privacy** (44 entries), gives the guarantee that the syntactic conditions lack. It defines [global sensitivity](/global-sensitivity/) and the basic mechanisms: [randomized response](/randomized-response/), the [Laplace mechanism](/laplace-mechanism/) with noise scaled to the $\ell_1$ sensitivity, the [Gaussian mechanism](/gaussian-mechanism/) scaled to the $\ell_2$ sensitivity, whose classical calibration is proved only for $\varepsilon < 1$, the [exponential mechanism](/exponential-mechanism/) and the [sparse vector technique](/sparse-vector-technique/). Then come the [post-processing](/post-processing-theorem/), [group privacy](/group-privacy-theorem/) and composition theorems up to the [optimal](/optimal-composition-theorem/) one; the accountants, [Rényi DP](/renyi-differential-privacy/), [zCDP](/zero-concentrated-differential-privacy/) and the [moments accountant](/moments-accountant/); [amplification by subsampling](/privacy-amplification-by-subsampling/) and [by shuffling](/privacy-amplification-by-shuffling/) under their hypotheses; [smooth sensitivity](/smooth-sensitivity/); query release by the [binary tree mechanism](/binary-tree-mechanism/) and [MWEM](/mwem/); private learning by [objective perturbation](/objective-perturbation/), [DP-SGD](/dp-sgd/) and [PATE](/pate/); [local privacy](/local-differential-privacy/) with [RAPPOR](/rappor/); and the lower bounds of the [Dinur-Nissim reconstruction attack](/dinur-nissim-reconstruction-attack/), [packing](/packing-lower-bound/) and [fingerprinting codes](/fingerprinting-codes-lower-bound/), which say what accuracy no private mechanism can have.

**Chapter 45.9, Adversarial machine learning** (36 entries), is written for defenders and frames each attack by its threat model, what it shows and how it is prevented, detected or measured. It starts from the [adversarial example](/adversarial-example/), with the gradient attacks [FGSM](/fast-gradient-sign-method/), the [PGD attack](/pgd-attack/) used to measure robust accuracy and [Carlini-Wagner](/carlini-wagner-attack/), [transferability](/adversarial-transferability/) and the label-only [boundary attack](/boundary-attack/). It shows how [defensive distillation](/defensive-distillation/) was broken and how BPDA tests defenses. Certified defenses follow: the [verification problem](/neural-network-verification-problem/), NP-complete for ReLU networks, solved exactly by [Reluplex](/reluplex/) or soundly but incompletely by [interval](/interval-bound-propagation/) and [linear bound propagation](/linear-bound-propagation/), and randomized smoothing. Training-time attacks are [data poisoning](/data-poisoning/) and [backdoors](/backdoor-attack/), with [spectral signatures](/spectral-signatures-defense/) and [Krum](/krum-aggregation/), which needs $n > 2f + 2$. Privacy attacks on models are [membership inference](/membership-inference-attack/), whose advantage $\varepsilon$-DP training bounds by $e^{\varepsilon} - 1$, [model inversion](/model-inversion-attack/) and [model extraction](/model-extraction-attack/), and [machine unlearning](/machine-unlearning/) removes training points after the fact. The chapter ends with language models: [prompt injection](/prompt-injection/), [training data extraction](/training-data-extraction/) and the [green list watermark](/language-model-watermarking/), whose detection is a hypothesis test with a bounded false positive rate.

## How to read this volume

The chapters form three routes that need little of each other. The route through programs is Chapters 45.1 to 45.4: 45.1 is the most formal and gives the vocabulary of rights and flows; 45.2 and 45.3 need only C, the stack and the control-flow graph; 45.4 needs some cryptography and statistics. The route through systems is Chapters 45.5 and 45.6, which need the basics of TCP/IP and the web, some statistics and, for the outlier detectors, the classifiers of Volume 25. The route through data is Chapters 45.7, 45.8 and 45.9, read in that order: 45.8 answers the weaknesses of 45.7, and 45.9 uses the definition of 45.8 for its privacy attacks and for unlearning.

For a first course, take these entries in order: [reference monitor](/reference-monitor/), [access control matrix](/access-control-matrix/), [HRU safety undecidability](/hru-safety-undecidability/), [noninterference](/noninterference/), [security type system](/security-type-system/), [memory safety](/memory-safety/), [stack smashing](/stack-smashing/), [control-flow integrity](/control-flow-integrity/), [fuzz testing](/fuzz-testing/), [timing attack](/timing-attack/), [SQL injection](/sql-injection/), [base-rate fallacy](/base-rate-fallacy-intrusion-detection/), [undecidability of virus detection](/virus-detection-undecidability/), [k-anonymity](/k-anonymity/), [differential privacy](/differential-privacy-definition/), [Laplace mechanism](/laplace-mechanism/), [basic composition](/sequential-composition-theorem/), [adversarial example](/adversarial-example/), [PGD attack](/pgd-attack/) and [randomized smoothing](/randomized-smoothing/). A second course can add return-oriented programming, constant-time programming, l-diversity, the Gaussian mechanism, DP-SGD and membership inference. The learning path *Security and privacy* follows the volume step by step.

Each entry stands on its own; its facts box lists what it needs, by entry number.

## What you need first

Basic probability runs through the whole volume: [conditional probability](/conditional-probability/) and the [Chernoff bound](/chernoff-bound/) of Volume 1, and [Bayes' theorem](/bayes-theorem/) of Volume 25. The undecidability and hardness results use the [halting problem](/halting-problem/), [Rice's theorem](/rice-theorem/) and [NP-completeness](/np-complete/) of Volume 39.

For programs, information flow needs the [safety property](/safety-property/) of Volume 16 and the [type system](/type-system/), [self-composition](/self-composition/) and [taint analysis](/taint-analysis/) of Volume 34. Stack attacks need the [activation record](/activation-record/), and fuzzing the [control-flow graph](/control-flow-graph/) and [coverage-guided fuzzing](/coverage-guided-fuzzing/), all in Volume 34. Side channels need [modular exponentiation](/modular-exponentiation/) from Volume 10, the [RSA cryptosystem](/rsa-cryptosystem/) and the [block cipher](/block-cipher/) from Volume 23, the [Welch t-test](/welch-t-test/) of Volume 33 and the [set-associative cache](/set-associative-cache/) of Volume 26.

For systems, read [BGP](/bgp/) in Volume 26, [HMAC](/hmac/) and [password hashing](/password-hashing/) in Volume 23 and the [Bloom filter](/bloom-filter/) of Volume 3. Detection uses [precision and recall](/precision-and-recall/) from Volume 25 and the [Neyman-Pearson lemma](/neyman-pearson-lemma/) and the [sequential probability ratio test](/sequential-probability-ratio-test/) of Volume 33.

For data, anonymization uses [linear programming](/linear-programming-problem/) from Volume 15, and differential privacy starts from the [randomized algorithm](/randomized-algorithm/) of Volume 2 and uses the [Kullback-Leibler divergence](/kullback-leibler-divergence/) and the [type I and type II errors](/type-i-and-type-ii-errors/) of Volume 33 and the [multiplicative weights update](/multiplicative-weights-update/) of Volume 19. Private learning and adversarial machine learning need [empirical risk minimization](/empirical-risk-minimization/), [logistic regression](/logistic-regression/), [backpropagation](/backpropagation/) and [adversarial training](/adversarial-training/) of Volume 25, [stochastic gradient descent](/stochastic-gradient-descent/) and [projected gradient descent](/projected-gradient-descent/) of Volume 15, and for the last entries the [language model](/language-model/) of Volume 41.
