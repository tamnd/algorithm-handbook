## History

Randomized response is older than the field. Warner (1965) proposed it so that survey respondents could answer a sensitive question truthfully without the interviewer learning their answer. The modern subject began with an impossibility result: Dinur and Nissim (PODS 2003) showed that answering about $n$ random subset sums with error $o(\sqrt n)$ lets anyone rebuild almost all of a dataset of $n$ secret bits. Any useful definition of privacy had to allow error, and to say how much.

Dwork, McSherry, Nissim and Smith (TCC 2006) gave the definition and the Laplace mechanism calibrated to sensitivity, and Dwork (ICALP 2006) named it differential privacy. Dwork, Kenthapadi, McSherry, Mironov and Naor (EUROCRYPT 2006) added the slack $\delta$, which allows Gaussian noise. McSherry and Talwar (FOCS 2007) gave the exponential mechanism for outputs that are not numbers, Nissim, Raskhodnikova and Smith (STOC 2007) gave smooth sensitivity and subsample and aggregate, and Dwork and Lei (STOC 2009) gave propose-test-release. Ghosh, Roughgarden and Sundararajan (STOC 2009) proved that the geometric mechanism is universally optimal for one count. McSherry's PINQ system (2009) made sequential and parallel composition the working rules of a query language.

The next problem was answering many queries. Blum, Ligett and Roth (STOC 2008) showed with SmallDB that exponentially many counting queries can be answered from a dataset of modest size. Dwork, Naor, Reingold, Rothblum and Vadhan (STOC 2009) introduced the sparse vector technique, Dwork, Rothblum and Vadhan (2010) proved the advanced composition theorem, and Hardt and Rothblum (FOCS 2010) gave private multiplicative weights, and MWEM (Hardt, Ligett and McSherry, NIPS 2012) gave a practical offline method for synthetic data. Also in 2010, Chan, Shi and Song and Dwork, Naor, Pitassi and Rothblum found the binary tree mechanism for counters, and Li, Hay, Rastogi, Miklau and McGregor (PODS 2010) unified the strategies for linear queries in the matrix mechanism. On the side of limits, Hardt and Talwar (STOC 2010) used packing, and Bun, Ullman and Vadhan (STOC 2014) used fingerprinting codes.

Accounting became central once privacy met machine learning. Chaudhuri, Monteleoni and Sarwate (JMLR 2011) gave output and objective perturbation for convex risk minimization. Kairouz, Oh and Viswanath (ICML 2015) found the optimal composition, and Murtagh and Vadhan (TCC 2016) showed that computing it is #P-complete. Bun and Steinke (2016) defined zCDP, Abadi et al. (CCS 2016) gave DP-SGD with the moments accountant, Mironov (2017) defined Rényi DP, and Dong, Roth and Su (2019) defined Gaussian DP. Papernot et al. (ICLR 2017) gave PATE. Numerical accountants followed from 2018 on.

Deployment brought the local and shuffle models. Erlingsson, Pihur and Korolova (CCS 2014) built RAPPOR for Chrome, and Wang, Blocki, Li and Jha (USENIX Security 2017) compared local frequency oracles and proposed optimized local hashing. Erlingsson et al. (SODA 2019) and Feldman, McMillan and Talwar (FOCS 2021) proved amplification by shuffling. Canonne, Kamath and Steinke (NeurIPS 2020) gave the exact discrete Gaussian sampler used in the 2020 United States Census, after Mironov (2012) had shown that floating-point Laplace samplers leak.

## Further reading

- Cynthia Dwork and Aaron Roth, *The Algorithmic Foundations of Differential Privacy*, Foundations and Trends in Theoretical Computer Science 9(3–4), 2014. The standard text for the definition, the basic mechanisms, composition, sparse vector and query release.
- Salil Vadhan, "The complexity of differential privacy", in *Tutorials on the Foundations of Cryptography*, 2017. Proofs of the reconstruction attacks, propose-test-release and the packing and fingerprinting lower bounds.
- Min Lyu, Dong Su and Ninghui Li, "Understanding the sparse vector technique for differential privacy", Proceedings of the VLDB Endowment 10(6), 2017. A careful study of how published variants of one mechanism fail, and a model for checking privacy claims.
- Jinshuo Dong, Aaron Roth and Weijie Su, "Gaussian differential privacy", Journal of the Royal Statistical Society Series B, 2022. Privacy as hypothesis testing, with the trade-off curves behind the modern accountants.
