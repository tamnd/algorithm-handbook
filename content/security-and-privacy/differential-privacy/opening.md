A statistics office wants to publish how many people in a town have a rare disease. The count is 3. If it publishes 3, someone who knows the health of all the other residents learns about the last one. If it publishes $3 + Z$, with $Z$ drawn from a Laplace distribution of scale $1/\varepsilon$, then any one person's record changes the chance of every published value by at most a factor $e^{\varepsilon}$. That bound is the definition of differential privacy. It is a property of the publishing procedure, not of the number 3, and it holds whatever an observer knew beforehand.

Dwork, McSherry, Nissim and Smith gave the definition in 2006. It followed the result of Dinur and Nissim (2003): answering too many counting queries too accurately lets anyone rebuild the data, so some error is forced, and the question is how little error a guarantee allows. The answer depends on how much one record can move a query, on how many queries are asked, and on who is trusted to hold the raw data. Census bureaus and several large companies now release data under this definition.

The definition is about the mechanism. It does not hide facts about the population: a study that finds smoking causes cancer still tells an insurer something about a smoker, whether or not that smoker took part. It limits only what one person's presence in the data adds.

## A first reading

Read the [definition](/differential-privacy-definition/) and [global sensitivity](/global-sensitivity/) first, then the [Laplace mechanism](/laplace-mechanism/) and [randomized response](/randomized-response/), the two mechanisms that every later one builds on. The [post-processing theorem](/post-processing-theorem/) and [sequential composition](/sequential-composition-theorem/) are short and are used in almost every proof. Next read [approximate differential privacy](/approximate-differential-privacy/) and the [Gaussian mechanism](/gaussian-mechanism/), then the [exponential mechanism](/exponential-mechanism/) for choices that are not numbers. [Advanced composition](/advanced-composition-theorem/) explains why many queries cost about $\sqrt k$ and not $k$. [Local differential privacy](/local-differential-privacy/) shows the price of trusting no one, and the [Dinur-Nissim attack](/dinur-nissim-reconstruction-attack/) shows why noise is needed at all.

Skip at first the accounting group after the privacy loss random variable, the shuffle and subsampling amplification bounds, smooth sensitivity, SmallDB and the fingerprinting lower bound. They refine results that the first reading states in a simpler, weaker form. Come back to the accountants before reading [DP-SGD](/dp-sgd/), whose guarantee depends on them.

## Before you start

The chapter assumes discrete and continuous probability: [conditional probability](/conditional-probability/), [expected value](/expected-value/), the [binomial distribution](/binomial-distribution/) and the [geometric distribution](/geometric-distribution/) of Volume 1, and the idea of a [randomized algorithm](/randomized-algorithm/) from Volume 2. Accuracy bounds use the [union bound](/union-bound/), the [Chernoff bound](/chernoff-bound/) and [Hoeffding's inequality](/hoeffding-inequality/), and advanced composition uses [Azuma's inequality](/azuma-inequality/). The accounting entries compare output laws with the [total variation distance](/total-variation-distance/) of Volume 17 and the [Kullback-Leibler divergence](/kullback-leibler-divergence/) of Volume 33, and read privacy as a hypothesis test, with [type I and type II errors](/type-i-and-type-ii-errors/) and the [Neyman-Pearson lemma](/neyman-pearson-lemma/). The learning group assumes [empirical risk minimization](/empirical-risk-minimization/), [convex functions](/convex-function/) and [stochastic gradient descent](/stochastic-gradient-descent/), and MWEM is the [multiplicative weights update](/multiplicative-weights-update/) of Volume 19. For the weaker, syntactic notions that came before, see [k-anonymity](/k-anonymity/) in Chapter 45.7.
