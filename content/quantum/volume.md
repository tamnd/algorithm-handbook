A quantum computer stores a state of $n$ qubits as $2^n$ complex amplitudes, changes them with unitary gates, and at the end returns one bit string, sampled with probability equal to the squared magnitude of its amplitude. For a few problems this changes what is efficient. Shor's algorithm factors an $n$-bit integer with a polynomial number of gates, where the best known classical algorithm, the [general number field sieve](/general-number-field-sieve/), takes heuristic time $\exp(O(n^{1/3} (\log n)^{2/3}))$. Grover's algorithm finds a marked item among $N$ with about $\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt N$ queries, where a classical search needs about $N/2$ on average. A quantum computer can follow the time evolution of a molecule or a spin system with a cost polynomial in its size, which no known classical method does in general. For most other problems no speedup is known, and for some it is ruled out.

This volume is about those algorithms and the parts they are built from. Its four chapters hold 152 entries: quantum primitives, quantum search, quantum number theory and quantum simulation.

## Why quantum algorithms get a volume of their own

Quantum algorithms use the linear algebra of Volumes 11 and 12, the number theory of Volume 10 and the probability of Volume 1, but they run on a different machine with a different cost model. A few ideas come back in every chapter:

- **Amplitudes cancel; probabilities do not.** A measurement returns one sample, so a quantum algorithm cannot read out its $2^n$ amplitudes. It wins only when it arranges [interference](/quantum-interference/) so that the paths to wrong answers cancel. Evaluating a function on every input at once, [quantum parallelism](/quantum-parallelism/), is not enough by itself.
- **Every guarantee names its model.** A cost counts oracle queries or gates. An answer is exact, bounded-error or zero-error. A circuit is noiseless or runs under a stated noise model. The separations of [Deutsch-Jozsa](/deutsch-jozsa-algorithm/), [Bernstein-Vazirani](/bernstein-vazirani-algorithm/) and [Simon](/simon-algorithm/) are proved, but in the query model and relative to an oracle; the classes BQP and BPP are not known to differ.
- **Speedups come in sizes.** Unstructured search gains a square root, and the $\Omega(\sqrt N)$ query lower bound shows that no quantum algorithm does better in the query model. Element distinctness and collision finding gain a polynomial factor. Factoring, discrete logarithms and Pell's equation run in quantum polynomial time, but nobody has proved that they are hard for classical computers, so even this gap is a gap to the best known classical algorithm.
- **A few patterns make most algorithms.** [Phase kickback](/phase-kickback/) followed by a [Fourier transform](/quantum-fourier-transform/) gives [phase estimation](/quantum-phase-estimation/), which drives order finding, discrete logarithms, quantum counting and energy estimation. Reflections give [amplitude amplification](/amplitude-amplification/), which drives search. Product formulas and block encodings give Hamiltonian simulation.
- **Proved, estimated and measured are different claims.** The [threshold theorem](/quantum-threshold-theorem/) proves that a noisy machine can run long circuits below a constant error rate; the numeric thresholds and the qubit counts for breaking RSA are estimates that depend on their assumptions. Some algorithms rest on conjectures or heuristics, such as the generalized Riemann hypothesis behind the class group algorithm or the number-theoretic heuristic behind Regev's factoring algorithm. Variational methods are judged by experiment, not by theorems.

## The chapters

**Chapter 30.1, Quantum primitives**, builds the machine. It covers [qubits](/qubit/), [states](/quantum-state/), the [Born rule](/born-rule/) and [measurement](/quantum-measurement/); the [Pauli](/pauli-gates/), [Hadamard](/hadamard-gate/), [CNOT](/cnot-gate/), [T](/phase-and-t-gates/) and [Toffoli](/toffoli-gate/) gates and the [circuit model](/quantum-circuit-model/); [entanglement](/entanglement/), [teleportation](/quantum-teleportation/), [no-cloning](/no-cloning-theorem/) and the [CHSH inequality](/chsh-inequality/); the [quantum oracle](/quantum-oracle/) and the first query separations; the quantum Fourier transform, with $O(n^2)$ gates on $n$ qubits, and phase estimation; [density matrices](/density-matrix/), [channels](/quantum-channel/) and distances between states; [universal gate sets](/universal-gate-set/), the [Solovay-Kitaev theorem](/solovay-kitaev-theorem/) and the [Gottesman-Knill theorem](/gottesman-knill-theorem/); and [error correction](/quantum-error-correction/), from the [Shor code](/shor-code/) to the threshold theorem.

**Chapter 30.2, Quantum search**, gains a square root and sometimes more. It covers [unstructured search](/unstructured-search/) and [Grover's algorithm](/grover-search/), with about $\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N/M}$ queries for $M$ marked items, and its [optimality](/grover-optimality/); amplitude amplification, which needs $O(1/\sqrt a)$ runs where repetition needs $O(1/a)$; search with an unknown number of solutions ([BBHT](/bbht-search/)), [quantum counting](/quantum-counting/), [minimum finding](/durr-hoyer-minimum-finding/) and [fixed-point search](/fixed-point-quantum-search/); [collision finding](/brassard-hoyer-tapp-collision/) with $O((N/r)^{1/3})$ queries; [quantum walk search](/quantum-walk-search/) and [Ambainis's algorithm](/ambainis-element-distinctness/) for element distinctness with $O(N^{2/3})$ queries; [triangle finding](/triangle-finding/) and [k-distinctness](/k-distinctness/); and [ordered search](/quantum-ordered-search/), where the gain is only a constant factor over binary search. The search algorithms apply to any problem with a checkable answer, such as [SAT](/sat/) (Volume 16), and give a square root there, not more.

**Chapter 30.3, Quantum number theory**, holds the exponential speedups that threaten public-key cryptography. It covers [order finding](/order-finding/) and the [reduction from factoring](/factoring-to-order-finding/); [quantum modular exponentiation](/quantum-modular-exponentiation/) and [continued-fraction post-processing](/continued-fraction-post-processing/); [Shor's algorithm](/shor-algorithm/) and the theorem that [factoring is in BQP](/factoring-in-bqp/); [discrete logarithms](/quantum-discrete-logarithm/), in $\mathbb{Z}_p^*$ and on [elliptic curves](/quantum-elliptic-curve-discrete-log/), and the [short discrete logarithm](/short-discrete-logarithm/) of [Ekerå and Håstad](/ekera-hastad-algorithm/); [resource estimates](/quantum-resource-estimation-factoring/) for factoring RSA-sized numbers; [Hallgren's algorithm](/hallgren-pell-algorithm/) for [Pell's equation](/pell-equation/), the [unit group](/unit-group/), the [class group](/quantum-class-group-algorithm/) and the [principal ideal problem](/quantum-principal-ideal-algorithm/); and two newer results that rest on heuristics, [Regev's factoring algorithm](/regev-factoring-algorithm/) and [quantum lattice sieving](/quantum-sieving/), which lowers the exponent of the best lattice sieve by a constant factor only. The targets are the [RSA cryptosystem](/rsa-cryptosystem/) and [elliptic-curve cryptography](/elliptic-curve-cryptography/) of Volume 23, and [post-quantum cryptography](/post-quantum-cryptography/) is the answer to them.

**Chapter 30.4, Quantum simulation**, returns to the problem that started the field. It covers [Feynman's idea](/feynman-quantum-simulation/) and [Lloyd's universal simulator](/universal-quantum-simulator/); the [Hamiltonian simulation](/hamiltonian-simulation/) problem; the [Lie-Trotter formula](/lie-trotter-product-formula/), whose error is at most $t^2\lVert[A, B]\rVert/(2r)$ in the spectral norm, the [Trotter-Suzuki formulas](/trotter-suzuki-product-formulas/), [commutator scaling](/commutator-scaling/) and [qDRIFT](/qdrift/); [linear combinations of unitaries](/linear-combination-of-unitaries/), [block encodings](/block-encoding/), [quantum signal processing](/quantum-signal-processing/), [qubitization](/qubitization/) and [simulation](/optimal-hamiltonian-simulation/) with $O(\alpha t + \log(1/\varepsilon))$ queries; the [no-fast-forwarding theorem](/no-fast-forwarding-theorem/) and the [Lieb-Robinson bound](/lieb-robinson-bound/); [second quantization](/second-quantization/) and the [Jordan-Wigner](/jordan-wigner-transformation/) and [Bravyi-Kitaev](/bravyi-kitaev-transformation/) mappings; [quantum chemistry](/quantum-chemistry-simulation/) and [ground-state energy estimation](/ground-state-energy-estimation/), which is QMA-hard for general local Hamiltonians and easier with a good guiding state; [Gibbs states](/gibbs-state-preparation/), [open systems](/open-quantum-system-simulation/) and [field theories](/quantum-field-theory-simulation/); and the [variational quantum eigensolver](/variational-quantum-eigensolver/), whose energies are upper bounds but whose performance is measured, not proved.

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course, read these entries in this order: qubit, quantum state, Born rule, tensor product, Hadamard gate, CNOT gate, entanglement, quantum oracle, phase kickback, the Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm, Simon's algorithm, the quantum Fourier transform, quantum phase estimation, Grover's algorithm, the optimality of Grover's search, amplitude amplification, order finding, Shor's algorithm, Hamiltonian simulation and the Lie-Trotter formula. Do the exercises marked ◦ at the end of each chapter you touch.

If you already know the basics, start with the opening page of each chapter. It names the groups in the chapter and the order to read them. Chapter 30.1 comes first; the other three use its gates, oracles, Fourier transform and phase estimation, and can be read in any order. Quantum counting in Chapter 30.2 and energy estimation in Chapter 30.4 are phase estimation again, and the resource estimates of Chapter 30.3 need the error correction at the end of Chapter 30.1.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained. Entries in other volumes are cited by number too. Throughout the volume qubit 0 is the least significant bit and the rightmost symbol of a ket.

## What you need first

You need linear algebra over the complex numbers: inner products, unitary and Hermitian matrices, [eigenvalues](/eigenvalue/) and the [spectral theorem](/spectral-theorem/) from Volume 12, and the [Kronecker product](/kronecker-product/) from Volume 11. The Fourier transforms build on the [discrete Fourier transform](/discrete-fourier-transform/) and the [FFT](/fast-fourier-transform/) of Volume 11. From Volume 1 you need [probability distributions](/probability-distribution/), the [Chernoff bound](/chernoff-bound/) and the [Boolean circuit](/boolean-circuit/); from Volume 17, [error amplification](/error-amplification/) for bounded-error algorithms. Chapter 30.3 uses [modular exponentiation](/modular-exponentiation/), [continued fractions](/continued-fraction/), [integer factorization](/integer-factorization/) and the [discrete logarithm problem](/discrete-logarithm-problem/) from Volume 10. Error correction starts from the [Hamming code](/hamming-code/) of Volume 24, and the variational methods of Chapter 30.4 call the [gradient descent](/gradient-descent/) of Volume 15.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
