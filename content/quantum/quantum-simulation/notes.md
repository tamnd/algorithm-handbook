## History

The idea came before the machines. Yuri Manin remarked in a 1980 book that quantum states are exponentially large, and Richard Feynman's lecture at the 1981 MIT conference on physics and computation, printed in 1982 as "Simulating physics with computers", proposed a simulator built from quantum parts. Seth Lloyd made the proposal precise in 1996: a Hamiltonian made of local terms can be simulated with polynomially many gates by the product formula that Trotter had proved in 1959. Abrams and Lloyd applied the method to fermions in 1997. Suzuki's recursive formulas of 1990 and 1991, built for many-body physics and Monte Carlo methods, gave every even order.

The next step changed the input. Aharonov and Ta-Shma (2003) simulated sparse Hamiltonians given by oracles. Berry, Ahokas, Cleve and Sanders (2007) analysed the higher-order formulas for this model and proved that no general method runs in time sublinear in $t$. Childs (2010) used a quantum walk, and Childs and Wiebe (2012) introduced the linear combination of unitaries. Berry, Childs, Cleve, Kothari and Somma added oblivious amplitude amplification and reached a cost polylogarithmic in $1/\epsilon$ in 2014, and gave the simpler truncated Taylor series in 2015. Low and Chuang combined quantum signal processing with a quantum walk in 2017 and with any block encoding in their qubitization paper of 2019, and Gilyén, Su, Low and Wiebe (2019) placed it all in the quantum singular value transformation.

Product formulas were not left behind. Campbell's qDRIFT (2019) made the cost independent of the number of terms by sampling them, and Childs, Su, Tran, Wiebe and Zhu (2021) proved error bounds with nested commutators that explain why product formulas work far better on lattices than the norm bounds say. Haah, Hastings, Kothari and Low (2018) used the Lieb-Robinson bound of 1972 to simulate lattice Hamiltonians with a nearly optimal gate count.

Chemistry drove much of the work. The Jordan-Wigner transformation dates from 1928, and Bravyi and Kitaev gave their logarithmic encoding in 2002. Aspuru-Guzik, Dutoi, Love and Head-Gordon (2005) proposed phase estimation for molecular energies, and Reiher, Wiebe, Svore, Wecker and Troyer (2017) estimated the cost for the FeMoco cofactor of nitrogenase. Peruzzo and coworkers ran the first variational quantum eigensolver in 2014, and McClean, Romero, Babbush and Aspuru-Guzik gave its theory in 2016. On the side of hardness, Kempe, Kitaev and Regev (2006) showed that the 2-local Hamiltonian problem is QMA-complete, which is why proved ground-state methods, such as those of Lin and Tong (2020), assume a guiding state.

Other physics followed: open systems through the Lindblad form of 1976 (Lindblad; Gorini, Kossakowski and Sudarshan), field theory and scattering through Jordan, Lee and Preskill (2012), thermal states through Poulin and Wocjan (2009) and quantum Metropolis sampling (Temme and coworkers, 2011), and density matrix exponentiation through Lloyd, Mohseni and Rebentrost (2014).

## Further reading

- Michael A. Nielsen and Isaac L. Chuang, *Quantum Computation and Quantum Information*, 10th anniversary edition, Cambridge University Press, 2010. The standard textbook; its section on the simulation of quantum systems and its chapters on quantum operations and distance measures are the background for this chapter.
- I. M. Georgescu, S. Ashhab and F. Nori, "Quantum simulation", *Reviews of Modern Physics* 86 (2014), 153–185. A survey of analog and digital simulation and of the physical systems it targets.
- Andrew M. Childs, Yuan Su, Minh C. Tran, Nathan Wiebe and Shuchen Zhu, "Theory of Trotter error with commutator scaling", *Physical Review X* 11 (2021), article 011020. The commutator bounds for product formulas of every order, with comparisons against other methods.
- John M. Martyn, Zane M. Rossi, Andrew K. Tan and Isaac L. Chuang, "Grand unification of quantum algorithms", *PRX Quantum* 2 (2021), article 040203. A tutorial on quantum signal processing and the eigenvalue and singular value transformations, with simulation as one application.
- Sam McArdle, Suguru Endo, Alán Aspuru-Guzik, Simon C. Benjamin and Xiao Yuan, "Quantum computational chemistry", *Reviews of Modern Physics* 92 (2020), article 015003. Fermion encodings, phase estimation and variational methods for molecules.
