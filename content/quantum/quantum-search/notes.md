## History

The lower bound came first. Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard and Vazirani circulated in 1994 a proof that a quantum computer needs order $\sqrt N$ queries to find a marked item, published in 1997, before anyone knew an algorithm that reached it. Grover presented the algorithm at STOC in 1996, with the title "A fast quantum mechanical algorithm for database search", and in Physical Review Letters in 1997. Boyer, Brassard, Høyer and Tapp gave the exact analysis the same year: the success probability $\sin^2((2k+1)\theta)$, the search for an unknown number of solutions, a sketch of counting, and a tighter lower bound; the paper appeared in 1998. Zalka proved in 1999 that Grover's algorithm is exactly optimal, and Beals, Buhrman, Cleve, Mosca and de Wolf showed with polynomials that an exact decision needs all $N$ queries.

The rotation was soon used for other problems. Dürr and Høyer found the minimum in 1996. Brassard, Høyer and Tapp found collisions with order $N^{1/3}$ queries in 1997 and gave quantum counting in 1998. The general form, amplitude amplification and estimation, is the paper of Brassard, Høyer, Mosca and Tapp, published in 2002. Grover's $\pi/3$ recursion of 2005 gave a search that never overshoots, and in 2014 Yoder, Low and Chuang found phases that keep this property with $O(\log(1/\delta)/\sqrt w)$ queries, which restores the square-root dependence on $1/w$.

For sorted data the gain is only a constant factor. Farhi, Goldstone, Gutmann and Sipser gave exact algorithms with fewer queries than binary search in 1999, and Høyer, Neerbek and Shi gave both the $\log_3 N$ algorithm and the $\frac{1}{\pi}(\ln N - 1)$ lower bound at ICALP 2001. Childs, Landahl and Parrilo found better algorithms by semidefinite programming in 2007. The best constant has no refereed answer; a 2026 preprint of Carolan and Childs claims that the lower bound can be met.

Quantum walks gave the next new bounds. Ambainis posted his walk for element distinctness in 2003, with $O(N^{2/3})$ queries against the $O(N^{3/4})$ of Buhrman and others, and Magniez, Santha and Szegedy used it at once for triangles. Szegedy defined the walk of any Markov chain in 2004, and Magniez, Nayak, Roland and Santha turned it into a general search in 2007. On grids, Aaronson and Ambainis started the study of search with local moves, and walk algorithms brought the 2D grid to $O(\sqrt{N \log N})$ steps. In 2011 and 2012 Belovs's learning graphs, which build solutions of the dual adversary bound, lowered the exponents for triangles and for $k$-distinctness; Le Gall reached $\tilde O(n^{5/4})$ for triangles in 2014. Grover adaptive search goes back to Bulger, Baritompa and Wood in 2003; Gilliam, Woerner and Gonciulea built its oracles for binary optimization, published in 2021.

## Further reading

- Michael A. Nielsen and Isaac L. Chuang, *Quantum Computation and Quantum Information*, 10th anniversary edition, Cambridge University Press, 2010. The chapter on quantum search covers the Grover iteration as a rotation, quantum counting and the optimality proof.
- Ronald de Wolf, *Quantum Computing: Lecture Notes*, arXiv:1907.09415. Short chapters on Grover search, amplitude amplification, the polynomial and adversary lower bounds, and quantum walks.
- Michel Boyer, Gilles Brassard, Peter Høyer and Alain Tapp, "Tight bounds on quantum searching", *Fortschritte der Physik* 46 (1998), 493–505. The exact analysis, the algorithm for an unknown number of solutions, and the lower bound with constants.
- Gilles Brassard, Peter Høyer, Michele Mosca and Alain Tapp, "Quantum amplitude amplification and estimation", in *Quantum Computation and Information*, Contemporary Mathematics 305, American Mathematical Society, 2002. Amplification in its general form, the exact version, amplitude estimation and counting.
- Frédéric Magniez, Ashwin Nayak, Jérémie Roland and Miklos Santha, "Search via quantum walk", *SIAM Journal on Computing* 40 (2011), 142–164. The general walk search, its cost formula and its proof.
