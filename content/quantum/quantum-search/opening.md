A function $f$ on $N = 2^{20}$ inputs, about a million, returns 1 on one of them, and nothing about the inputs says which. A classical program has to try inputs one by one: about half a million evaluations on average and all of them in the worst case, and no randomized method does better than order $N$. A quantum computer that can evaluate $f$ on a superposition of inputs finds the marked input with 804 evaluations and fails with probability below $10^{-6}$. That is [Grover's algorithm](/grover-search/), and the chapter is about it, its relatives, and the limits of the speedup.

Almost every result here is stated in the query model. The input is an oracle, a unitary that computes $f(x)$ or flips the sign of the marked inputs ([quantum oracle](/quantum-oracle/)), and the cost is the number of oracle calls. Grover's algorithm makes about $\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N/M}$ queries when $M$ of the $N$ inputs are marked, and the [optimality theorem](/grover-optimality/) shows that every quantum algorithm needs $\Omega(\sqrt{N/M})$. Both bounds count queries. A query lower bound is not a bound on gates or time, and a query upper bound becomes a time bound only when the oracle is cheap: a formula or a circuit for $f$, or a stored table read through [quantum random access memory](/quantum-random-access-memory/). Loading a classical table of $N$ entries already costs order $N$, so "searching a database" is the wrong picture. The right one is a search over the inputs of a function that a circuit computes, such as the assignments of a formula or the [keys of a cipher](/exhaustive-key-search/).

The speedup is quadratic, not exponential. Search over the $2^n$ assignments of a [satisfiability](/sat/) instance takes order $2^{n/2}$ queries, which is still exponential, and the optimality theorem shows that a black-box search cannot do better; it gives no reason to think that quantum computers solve NP-complete problems in polynomial time. The pages also keep the error types apart. Grover's algorithm with a known $M$ has bounded one-sided error and a variant is exact; with an unknown $M$ the expected number of queries is $O(\sqrt{N/M})$; deciding with certainty whether any input is marked needs all $N$ queries. Each statement names its model, its error, and whether it holds in the worst case or on average.

The same idea of rotating amplitude toward the good states, a little per query, runs through the rest of the chapter. [Amplitude amplification](/amplitude-amplification/) applies it to any quantum algorithm that sometimes succeeds. Phase estimation on the Grover iteration counts the marked items. Repeated search below a threshold finds a minimum. Quantum walks apply the rotation to a Markov chain and give the best known query bounds for element distinctness and triangle finding, two problems where plain Grover search is not the best quantum method.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups, read in order.

1. **Unstructured search and its limits.** The [search problem](/unstructured-search/), the [diffusion operator](/grover-diffusion-operator/), Grover's algorithm and the matching lower bound.
2. **Amplification and an unknown number of solutions.** Amplitude amplification, [BBHT search](/bbht-search/) for an unknown $M$, and [fixed-point search](/fixed-point-quantum-search/), which never overshoots.
3. **Counting.** [Counting marked items](/counting-marked-items/) as a problem, and [quantum counting](/quantum-counting/), its algorithm by phase estimation.
4. **Minimum finding and optimization.** [Minimum finding](/minimum-finding/), the algorithm of [Dürr and Høyer](/durr-hoyer-minimum-finding/), and [Grover adaptive search](/grover-adaptive-search/) for binary optimization.
5. **Quantum walks.** [Spatial search](/spatial-search/) on graphs and grids, and [quantum walk search](/quantum-walk-search/) on a Markov chain.
6. **Collisions and distinctness.** The [Brassard-Høyer-Tapp collision algorithm](/brassard-hoyer-tapp-collision/), [k-distinctness](/k-distinctness/), [Ambainis's walk](/ambainis-element-distinctness/) and [Belovs's algorithm](/belovs-k-distinctness/).
7. **Triangles.** [Triangle finding](/triangle-finding/) and the nested walk of [Magniez, Santha and Szegedy](/magniez-santha-szegedy-triangle/).
8. **Ordered search.** [Quantum ordered search](/quantum-ordered-search/) and the exact algorithm of [Høyer, Neerbek and Shi](/hoyer-neerbek-shi-ordered-search/), a constant factor below binary search.

## A first reading

Take these eight entries in order: unstructured search, the diffusion operator, Grover's algorithm, the optimality theorem, amplitude amplification, BBHT search, quantum counting and minimum finding. They give the model, the algorithm with its exact success probability $\sin^2((2k+1)\theta)$, the proof that it cannot be beaten, the general form, the fix for an unknown count, and the two first uses of the rotation beyond search.

Leave for later the walk-based groups 5 to 7. They are research results stated as query bounds, and their time bounds depend on data structures in quantum memory. Ordered search is short and independent of the rest; read it when binary search is familiar.
