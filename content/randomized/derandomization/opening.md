A randomized algorithm reads random bits, and derandomization asks how many of them it really needs. Sometimes the answer is none. The random cut that puts each vertex on a side by a coin flip cuts half of the $m$ edges in expectation, and fixing the vertices one at a time, each on the side opposite to most of its placed neighbours, gives a cut of at least $m/2$ edges with no coin at all. Sometimes the answer is a few bits. The same random cut needs only that each pair of vertices gets independent sides, and $n$ pairwise independent bits come from a seed of about $\log_2 n$ bits, so trying all $n + 1$ or so seeds is a deterministic algorithm. And sometimes the bits are there but of poor quality: a coin with $\Pr[1] = 0.2$ is still a source of randomness, and pairing its flips turns it into fair bits.

The chapter collects these ideas and the theory behind them. Its central objects are the pseudorandom generator, which makes a few random bits look like many, and the extractor, which makes many weak random bits into a few good ones.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in six groups, read in order.

1. **Fixing the coins one at a time.** The [method of conditional expectations](/method-of-conditional-expectations/) turns an expectation argument into a greedy algorithm: MAX-CUT with at least $m/2$ edges, MAX-3SAT with at least $7m/8$ of $m$ clauses of three literals. [Pessimistic estimators](/pessimistic-estimators/) extend it to Chernoff-type bounds.
2. **Few independent bits.** [$k$-wise independence](/k-wise-independence/) needs only $O(k \log n)$ seed bits, and [limited independence](/limited-independence/) says which tail bounds survive. [Sample space reduction](/sample-space-reduction/) searches the small space, and [small-bias spaces](/small-bias-sample-space/) fool every parity with about $\log n$ seed bits.
3. **Saving random bits with expanders.** The [expander walk sampler](/expander-walk-sampler/) reduces the error of an algorithm with one-sided error to $2^{-k}$ with $m + O(k)$ random bits, and [randomness amplification](/randomness-amplification/) shows how close to optimal that is.
4. **Pseudorandom generators.** [Computational indistinguishability](/computational-indistinguishability/), the [pseudorandom generator](/pseudorandom-generator/), Yao's [next-bit test](/yao-next-bit-unpredictability/), the construction from one-way functions, and the one-sided [hitting set generator](/hitting-set-generator/).
5. **Hardness versus randomness.** The [Nisan-Wigderson generator](/nisan-wigderson-generator/) turns a hard function into a generator with a seed of $O(\log m)$ bits, and the [Impagliazzo-Wigderson theorem](/impagliazzo-wigderson-theorem/) gives BPP $=$ P from worst-case circuit lower bounds. [Nisan's generator](/nisan-prg/) fools small-space machines with no assumption.
6. **Weak sources and extractors.** From [von Neumann debiasing](/von-neumann-debiasing/) through [min-entropy](/min-entropy/) to the [randomness extractor](/randomness-extractor/), the [leftover hash lemma](/leftover-hash-lemma/), dispersers, two-source extractors and [Trevisan's extractor](/trevisan-extractor/).

For a first reading, take these thirteen entries in order: [the method of conditional expectations](/method-of-conditional-expectations/), [$k$-wise independence](/k-wise-independence/), [limited independence](/limited-independence/), [sample space reduction](/sample-space-reduction/), [the expander walk sampler](/expander-walk-sampler/), [pseudorandom generation](/pseudorandom-generation/), [computational indistinguishability](/computational-indistinguishability/), [the pseudorandom generator](/pseudorandom-generator/), [von Neumann debiasing](/von-neumann-debiasing/), [min-entropy](/min-entropy/), [randomness extraction](/randomness-extraction/), [the randomness extractor](/randomness-extractor/) and [the leftover hash lemma](/leftover-hash-lemma/). Leave the entries marked *Research* for later, and skip the Nisan-Wigderson and Impagliazzo-Wigderson proofs at first: they are long and use circuit complexity.

## Before you start

From Volume 1 you need [conditional expectation](/conditional-expectation/), [Chebyshev's inequality](/chebyshev-inequality/), the [Chernoff bound](/chernoff-bound/) and the [union bound](/union-bound/). From Volume 2 you need the idea of a [randomized algorithm](/randomized-algorithm/) and the [probabilistic method](/probabilistic-method/), which this chapter turns into algorithms. The [universal hash family](/universal-hash-family/) of Volume 3 reappears in the leftover hash lemma. The chapter on random number generation in Volume 12 has the practical side: [unbiased bits from a biased coin](/unbiased-bits-from-biased-coin/), the [pseudorandom number generator](/pseudorandom-number-generator/), the [cryptographically secure PRNG](/cryptographically-secure-prng/) and the [Blum-Micali generator](/blum-micali/). Expanders are [regular graphs](/regular-graph/) from Volume 7 with a large [spectral gap](/spectral-gap/), and [total variation distance](/total-variation-distance/) is the distance used for every extractor; both are in Chapter 17.5. From earlier in this volume you need [pairwise independence](/pairwise-independence/) from Chapter 17.3, and [error amplification](/error-amplification/) and [one-sided error](/one-sided-error/) from Chapter 17.4.

$k$-wise independence appears three times in the book. Chapter 17.3 has [$k$-independent hashing](/k-independent-hashing/), and Volume 3 has [$k$-wise independent hashing](/k-wise-independent-hashing/); both treat the random polynomial of degree $k - 1$ as a hash function for keys. Here the same polynomial is a sample space of random variables, and the question is its size.
