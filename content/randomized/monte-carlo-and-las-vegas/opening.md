Someone hands you three $n \times n$ matrices $A$, $B$ and $C$ and claims that $C = AB$. Multiplying $A$ by $B$ and comparing settles it, at the cost of a matrix product. [Freivalds' algorithm](/freivalds-algorithm/) does less. It draws a random vector $r$ with entries in $\{0, 1\}$ and compares $A(Br)$ with $Cr$, which takes three matrix-vector products and $O(n^2)$ time. If $C = AB$ the two sides always agree. If $C \ne AB$ they disagree with probability at least $1/2$. Twenty independent rounds leave an error of at most $2^{-20} \approx 9.54 \times 10^{-7}$, and the answer "not equal" is never wrong.

That is a [Monte Carlo algorithm](/monte-carlo-algorithm/): its running time is bounded on every input, and its answer is wrong with a small probability that holds for every input. The other kind is the [Las Vegas algorithm](/las-vegas-algorithm/), which is never wrong and has a random running time. [Randomized quicksort](/randomized-quicksort/) is the standard example. On $10$ keys it makes about $24.44$ comparisons on average; the worst case of $45$ has probability $2/14175$. In both kinds the probability is over the algorithm's own coins, never over the input.

This chapter sets the two kinds side by side. It shows how to lower the error of a Monte Carlo algorithm by repetition and how to turn one kind into the other. Then it works through the standard examples: two primality tests, the testing of polynomial and matrix identities, matching nuts and bolts, and the count of minimum cuts. Two entries overlap Volume 10. The [Miller-Rabin test](/miller-rabin-primality-test/) and the [Solovay-Strassen test](/solovay-strassen-primality-test/) here are the same tests as Volume 10's [Miller-Rabin](/miller-rabin/) and [Solovay-Strassen](/solovay-strassen/). Volume 10 treats them as number theory, with strong liars, pseudoprimes and fixed sets of bases. This chapter treats them as one-sided Monte Carlo algorithms and stresses the error bound and the number of rounds.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in five groups, read in order.

1. **Two kinds of randomized algorithm.** The definitions of the [Las Vegas](/las-vegas-algorithm/) and [Monte Carlo](/monte-carlo-algorithm/) algorithm, and the two shapes of error. With [one-sided error](/one-sided-error/) one answer is always right, so $k$ runs combined by OR err with probability $\delta^k$. With [two-sided error](/two-sided-error/) either answer can be wrong, and what matters is the gap between the success probability and $1/2$.
2. **Lowering the error, changing the kind.** [Error amplification](/error-amplification/) gives the number of runs that a target error needs: $\lceil \ln(1/\delta)/\ln(1/\delta_0) \rceil$ for one-sided error, and a majority vote bounded by a Chernoff-type tail for two-sided error. The [conversion](/las-vegas-to-monte-carlo-conversion/) cuts a Las Vegas algorithm off at a multiple of its expected time, and it turns a Monte Carlo algorithm with a verifier into a Las Vegas one.
3. **Primality: witnesses of compositeness.** Miller-Rabin errs with probability at most $4^{-k}$ after $k$ rounds, Solovay-Strassen with at most $2^{-k}$. In both the answer "composite" is always right.
4. **Testing identities at random points.** The [Schwartz-Zippel lemma](/schwartz-zippel-lemma/) bounds the zeros of a nonzero polynomial, which puts [polynomial identity testing](/polynomial-identity-testing/) in co-RP. [Matrix product verification](/matrix-product-verification/) is the problem that Freivalds' algorithm solves.
5. **Random choices in combinatorial problems.** [Nuts and bolts](/nuts-and-bolts/) is a quicksort on two sets at once, with $4(n+1)H_n - 7n$ expected comparisons. The [minimum cut count bound](/minimum-cut-count-bound/) shows that a graph has at most $\binom{n}{2}$ minimum cuts.
