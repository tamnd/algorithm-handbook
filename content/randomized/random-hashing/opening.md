A hash table is fast when its keys spread out over the slots. For any one fixed hash function, some set of keys lands in a single slot: under $x \bmod p$, every multiple of $p$ does. Random hashing moves the randomness from the keys to the function. The table draws its function at random from a small family, after the keys are fixed, and the analysis takes the probability over that draw. Then no key set is bad on average, and the guarantee holds for every input, as long as the keys do not depend on the function.

This chapter is about what such a family must promise and what each promise buys. The weakest useful promise is about pairs: two distinct keys collide with probability at most $1/m$. That is enough for a chained table to take $O(1 + \alpha)$ expected time per operation. Stronger promises, about $k$ keys at once or about which key of a set gets the least value, are what [linear probing](/linear-probing/), [MinHash](/minhash/) and the analysis of [cuckoo hashing](/cuckoo-hashing/) need. Each family here is a few random numbers, so it is cheap to store and to draw again.

Volume 3 builds the tables themselves and defines the [universal hash family](/universal-hash-family/) and [universal hashing](/universal-hashing/). This chapter treats the families as random objects: their independence, their exact collision probabilities, and the theorems about tables that follow. Two of its entries have close relatives elsewhere. [k-independent hashing](/k-independent-hashing/) is the same notion as Volume 3's [k-wise independent hashing](/k-wise-independent-hashing/), and the [expected chain length](/universal-hashing-expected-chain-length/) theorem states the bound of the [universal hashing lemma](/universal-hashing-lemma/) of Volume 5 and adds the expected sum of squared chain lengths and the cost of a whole sequence of operations.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in two groups, read in order.

1. **Two keys at a time.** [Pairwise independence](/pairwise-independence/) asks that the hash values of any two distinct keys be uniform on $[m]^2$; it implies universality and makes variances add, so [Chebyshev's inequality](/chebyshev-inequality/) applies. The [dot-product family](/dot-product-hashing/) $a \cdot x \bmod p$ collides with probability exactly $1/p$, and an offset makes it pairwise independent. [Multiply-shift hashing](/multiply-shift-hashing/) uses one multiplication and one shift and collides with probability at most $2/m$. The [chain length theorem](/universal-hashing-expected-chain-length/) turns the collision bound into expected costs: at most $\alpha$ for an absent key and $1 + (n-1)/m$ for a stored one.
2. **More than two keys.** [k-independent hashing](/k-independent-hashing/) uses a random polynomial of degree $k - 1$ over a prime field. [Min-wise independent hashing](/min-wise-independent-hashing/) makes the minima of two sets agree with probability equal to their Jaccard similarity; exact families are huge, but $O(\log(1/\varepsilon))$-wise independence gives an approximate one. The [cuckoo hashing load threshold](/cuckoo-hashing-load-threshold/) shows that two-choice cuckoo hashing fails with probability $O(1/n)$ below load $1/2$ and almost surely above it.

## A first reading

Read the four entries of the first group in order: [pairwise independence](/pairwise-independence/), [dot-product hashing](/dot-product-hashing/), [multiply-shift hashing](/multiply-shift-hashing/) and [the expected chain length](/universal-hashing-expected-chain-length/). Work the small examples in them by hand; the prime $p = 5$ or $p = 7$ is enough to see every fact. Then read [k-independent hashing](/k-independent-hashing/) for its table of what each $k$ buys. The other two entries are advanced and can wait: the proof of the cuckoo threshold uses random graphs and a martingale bound, and min-wise independence matters mainly for similarity search.
