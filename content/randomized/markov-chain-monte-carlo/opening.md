In the Land of Oz the weather is rain, nice or snow, and tomorrow depends only on today. After a rainy day it rains again with probability $1/2$, and it is nice or snowy with probability $1/4$ each. After a nice day it is never nice again. Whatever the weather today, in a few weeks the chance of rain is very close to $2/5$, of a nice day $1/5$ and of snow $2/5$. The starting day is forgotten, and the chain settles into its [stationary distribution](/stationary-distribution/).

Markov chain Monte Carlo runs this in reverse. You have a distribution $\pi$ that you want to sample, often known only up to a constant: a posterior in statistics, a Boltzmann distribution $e^{-E(x)/kT}$ in physics, the uniform distribution on the colourings of a graph. You cannot draw from it directly, and the state space is far too large to list. So you build a [Markov chain](/markov-chain/) whose stationary distribution is $\pi$, run it for a while, and use its states as samples. The rule that builds the chain needs only ratios $\pi(y)/\pi(x)$, so the unknown constant cancels.

Two questions follow from this. Is the chain right, in the sense that it keeps $\pi$ and converges to it? And how long must it run before its state is close to $\pi$? The first question is usually easy. The second is the hard one, and much of the chapter is about it.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups, read in order.

1. **Markov chains and their limits.** [Transition matrices](/transition-matrix/), [irreducibility](/irreducibility/), [aperiodicity](/aperiodicity/), the convergence theorem for [ergodic](/ergodicity/) chains, [detailed balance](/detailed-balance/), and the [ergodic theorem](/ergodic-theorem/) for time averages.
2. **How far from stationary.** [Total variation distance](/total-variation-distance/), the [mixing time](/mixing-time/) $t_{mix}$, and what it means for a family of chains to be [rapidly mixing](/rapid-mixing/).
3. **Metropolis-Hastings and Gibbs.** The general recipe of [Metropolis-Hastings](/metropolis-hastings/), its [acceptance probability](/acceptance-probability/) and its two basic proposals, then [Gibbs sampling](/gibbs-sampling/) and [slice sampling](/slice-sampling/).
4. **Reading the output.** [Autocorrelation](/mcmc-autocorrelation/), [effective sample size](/effective-sample-size/), [thinning](/thinning/), [burn-in](/burn-in/) and the [Gelman-Rubin diagnostic](/gelman-rubin-diagnostic/).
5. **Bounding the mixing time.** Proofs by [coupling](/coupling-method/) and [path coupling](/path-coupling/), and proofs through the [spectral gap](/spectral-gap/), [conductance](/conductance/) and [canonical paths](/canonical-paths/).
6. **Spin systems and exact sampling.** The [Ising model](/ising-model/) and the [hard-core model](/hard-core-model/), [Glauber dynamics](/glauber-dynamics/), [Swendsen-Wang](/swendsen-wang/) cluster moves, and [coupling from the past](/coupling-from-the-past/), which returns a draw from exactly $\pi$.
7. **Samplers that follow the gradient.** [Hamiltonian Monte Carlo](/hamiltonian-monte-carlo/), the [No-U-Turn sampler](/no-u-turn-sampler/) and [MALA](/metropolis-adjusted-langevin-algorithm/).
8. **Hard targets.** [Parallel tempering](/parallel-tempering/), [Wang-Landau](/wang-landau/) and [reversible jump](/reversible-jump-mcmc/) for targets with many modes, rough energies or a varying dimension.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these twelve entries in order: [Markov chain](/markov-chain/), [stationary distribution](/stationary-distribution/), [ergodicity](/ergodicity/), [detailed balance](/detailed-balance/), [total variation distance](/total-variation-distance/), [mixing time](/mixing-time/), [Metropolis-Hastings](/metropolis-hastings/), [random-walk Metropolis](/random-walk-metropolis/), [Gibbs sampling](/gibbs-sampling/), [effective sample size](/effective-sample-size/), [burn-in](/burn-in/) and [Hamiltonian Monte Carlo](/hamiltonian-monte-carlo/). They are enough to write a correct sampler and to judge its output. Skip the fifth group at first. Its proofs are the reason some chains are known to mix fast, and you need them once you want a guarantee rather than a diagnostic. The research entries, [Wang-Landau](/wang-landau/) and [reversible jump](/reversible-jump-mcmc/), can wait until you meet a problem that needs them.

The chapter uses a few small chains again and again: the Land of Oz chain above, the two-state chain, the lazy walk on the hypercube, and Gibbs sampling of a correlated normal. Their numbers are worked out exactly in the entries, so that each theorem can be checked against a case you can compute by hand.

## Before you start

You need [conditional probability](/conditional-probability/), the [law of large numbers](/law-of-large-numbers/) and [variance](/variance/) from Volume 1. Powers of a transition matrix are [matrix powers](/matrix-power/) from Volume 11, and the spectral entries use the [eigenvalues](/eigenvalue/) of Volume 12. Irreducibility is the same as a [strongly connected](/strongly-connected-graph/) transition graph, from Volume 7, and the hard-core model is a weighted distribution on the [independent sets](/independent-set/) of a graph. The leapfrog step of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo is [Verlet integration](/verlet-integration/), and the reason to sample at all is often [Monte Carlo integration](/monte-carlo-integration/), both from Volume 12. Compare the chain-based samplers with [rejection sampling](/rejection-sampling/) and [importance sampling](/importance-sampling/) in chapter 17.1, which draw independent points but need a proposal that covers the target. The link between sampling and counting, through [approximate counting](/approximate-counting/) and the [FPRAS](/fpras/), is in Volume 14.
