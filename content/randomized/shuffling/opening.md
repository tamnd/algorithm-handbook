To shuffle is to put $n$ items in a random order so that each of the $n!$ orders has probability exactly $1/n!$. The task is short to state and easy to get wrong. A loop that swaps each item with a random partner looks right, runs in linear time, and favours some orders over others. Insertion sort with a comparator that flips a coin looks right too, and on ten items it puts the last item first with probability $1/512$ instead of $1/10$. The bias in both comes from counting: a program that makes $m$ equally likely runs can only give probabilities that are multiples of $1/m$, and when $n!$ does not divide $m$ it cannot be uniform.

The chapter has two halves. The first is about programs: the one standard shuffle, the ones that fail, and the exact methods for settings where the standard one does not fit. The second is about decks of cards. A person shuffles by repeating one random move, and the question there is how many moves bring the deck close to uniform. The answers come from the theory of Markov chains, and the most famous is that seven riffle shuffles are about enough for 52 cards.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in six groups, read in order.

1. **The problem and the standard shuffle.** The [random permutation](/random-permutation/) problem, its lower bound of $\log_2 n!$ random bits, and the [Fisher-Yates shuffle](/fisher-yates-shuffle/): one pass from the end, $n - 1$ random integers, $\Theta(n)$ time, no extra space. Its choices and the $n!$ orders are in one-to-one correspondence, and that bijection is the whole proof.
2. **Shuffles that go wrong.** The [naive shuffle](/naive-shuffle/), which draws each partner from the whole array, and the [sort-based shuffle](/sort-based-shuffle/), which sorts with a random comparator. Both are biased for $n \ge 3$, and the entries show which orders they favour.
3. **Shuffling with sorts and merges.** [Permute by sorting](/permute-by-sorting/) gives each item a random key and sorts. [Shuffling a linked list](/shuffling-a-linked-list/) splits and merges with coins weighted by the sizes of the parts. [MergeShuffle](/merge-shuffle/) uses fair coins and close to $n \log_2 n$ random bits.
4. **One cycle through all items.** A [random cyclic permutation](/random-cyclic-permutation/) is uniform over the $(n-1)!$ permutations with one cycle. [Sattolo's algorithm](/sattolo-algorithm/) draws one with a single change to Fisher-Yates.
5. **How many shuffles mix a deck.** [Card shuffling](/card-shuffling/) as a random walk on the orders of a deck. The [top-to-random shuffle](/top-to-random-shuffle/) mixes in $n \ln n$ moves, proved with a [strong uniform time](/strong-uniform-time/). The [random transposition shuffle](/random-transposition-shuffle/) mixes in $\frac12 n \ln n$.
6. **Riffles and overhand shuffles.** The [faro shuffle](/faro-shuffle/) is a perfect riffle that never mixes and returns a 52-card deck to its order after eight out-shuffles. The [Gilbert-Shannon-Reeds model](/gilbert-shannon-reeds-model/) is the random riffle, and the [Bayer-Diaconis theorem](/bayer-diaconis-theorem/) gives its exact distance from uniform. The [overhand shuffle](/overhand-shuffle-model/) is far slower, at order $n^2 \log n$ shuffles.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these nine entries in order: [the random permutation problem](/random-permutation/), [the Fisher-Yates shuffle](/fisher-yates-shuffle/), [the naive shuffle](/naive-shuffle/), [the sort-based shuffle](/sort-based-shuffle/), [permute by sorting](/permute-by-sorting/), [Sattolo's algorithm](/sattolo-algorithm/), [card shuffling](/card-shuffling/), [the top-to-random shuffle](/top-to-random-shuffle/) and [the Gilbert-Shannon-Reeds model](/gilbert-shannon-reeds-model/). The first six are about programs and need only counting and a little probability. The last three are the gentlest way into the theory of mixing. Leave the strong uniform time, the random transposition shuffle and the Bayer-Diaconis theorem for a second pass; they carry the hardest proofs of the chapter.

You need the [uniform distribution](/uniform-distribution/) and [linearity of expectation](/linearity-of-expectation/) from Volume 1. The [coupon collector problem](/coupon-collector-problem/) gives the $n H_n$ that appears in the top-to-random and random transposition shuffles, and the [birthday paradox](/birthday-paradox/) bounds the chance of a tie in permute by sorting. From the volumes on sequences and combinatorics, read [permutations](/permutation/) and their [cycles](/permutation-cycles/); [generating all permutations](/generating-all-permutations/) and [permutation unranking](/permutation-unranking/) are the deterministic side of the same bijection that Fisher-Yates uses. Merge-based shuffles build on [merge sort](/merge-sort/) from Volume 4. Every shuffle here needs a fair random integer in a range; Volume 12 shows how to draw one in [uniform random integer generation](/uniform-random-integer-generation/), why taking a remainder gives [modulo bias](/modulo-bias/), and how [Lemire's method](/lemire-bounded-integers/) avoids it cheaply.
