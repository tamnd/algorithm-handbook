## History

The standard shuffle began on paper. Ronald Fisher and Frank Yates gave it in 1938, in their book of statistical tables, as a procedure for a person with a pencil and a table of random digits: strike out a random number among those left and write it down. Richard Durstenfeld turned it into the in-place loop for computers in 1964, as Algorithm 235 of the *Communications of the ACM*, and Donald Knuth included it as Algorithm P in *The Art of Computer Programming*, which is why it is also called the Knuth shuffle. C. R. Rao in 1961 and M. Sandelius in 1962 shuffled by another route, splitting the items into groups by random digits. Donald Knuth and Andrew Yao studied in 1976 how many fair bits any procedure needs to sample a given law, the source of the $\log_2 n!$ lower bound.

Biased shuffles are as old as the correct one, and the counting argument that exposes them is folklore. A well-known case came in 2010, when a browser choice screen shown to users in Europe ordered five browsers by sorting with a random comparator, and the positions turned out to be far from uniform; the fix was a Fisher-Yates shuffle. The idea of shuffling by sorting random keys is old as well. Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest and Stein named it PERMUTE-BY-SORTING in their textbook, and the same random priorities drive the treaps of Cecilia Aragon and Raimund Seidel (1989). Sandra Sattolo published her algorithm for random cycles in 1986, and David Gries and Jinyun Xue proved it again by the methods of program verification in 1988. Axel Bacher, Olivier Bodini, Alexandros Hollender and Jérémie Lumbroso published MergeShuffle in 2015.

The mathematics of real card shuffles started with a model. Edgar Gilbert and Claude Shannon proposed the riffle model in 1955, and Jim Reeds studied it in 1981. The perfect shuffle has a separate story in magic: Alex Elmsley named the in- and out-shuffle in the 1950s, Solomon Golomb studied it in 1961, Harold Stone used it as a network for parallel computers in 1971, and Persi Diaconis, Ronald Graham and William Kantor found in 1983 the group the two shuffles generate.

The theory of mixing came in the 1980s. Persi Diaconis and Mehrdad Shahshahani found in 1981 that random transpositions mix after $\frac12 n \ln n$ moves, with Fourier analysis on the symmetric group; it was the first shuffle with a sharp mixing time. David Aldous showed in 1983 that $\frac32 \log_2 n$ riffles are needed to first order. Aldous and Diaconis introduced strong uniform times in 1986, with top-to-random as the first example, described the cutoff phenomenon, and set out the general theory in 1987. Dave Bayer and Diaconis found the exact law of the riffled deck in 1992, the source of the rule that seven shuffles are about enough for 52 cards. Robin Pemantle bounded the overhand shuffle in 1989, and Johan Jonasson proved in 2006 that order $n^2 \log n$ shuffles are needed.

## Further reading

- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 2: Seminumerical Algorithms*, third edition (1997). Section 3.4.2 gives the shuffle as Algorithm P, with sampling and its exercises.
- Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest and Clifford Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, fourth edition (2022). Section 5.3 proves Fisher-Yates and permute by sorting correct.
- David A. Levin and Yuval Peres, *Markov Chains and Mixing Times*, second edition (2017). The standard text on mixing, with top-to-random, random transpositions, riffles and strong uniform times worked out in full.
- Persi Diaconis, *Group Representations in Probability and Statistics*, IMS Lecture Notes–Monograph Series 11 (1988). The Fourier method for random walks on groups, and Broder's stopping time.
- David Aldous and Persi Diaconis, *Shuffling Cards and Stopping Times*, American Mathematical Monthly 93(5), 1986. A short, readable paper that introduced strong uniform times.
- Dave Bayer and Persi Diaconis, *Trailing the Dovetail Shuffle to Its Lair*, Annals of Applied Probability 2(2), 1992.
