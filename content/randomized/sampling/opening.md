A log file has a billion lines and you want a hundred of them, chosen fairly, after reading the file once. You do not know in advance how many lines there are, and you have room for only the hundred. Keep the first hundred lines. From then on, when line $t$ arrives, let it in with probability $100/t$ and throw out a random line already kept. At every moment the hundred lines you hold are a uniform sample of all the lines read so far. That rule is [reservoir sampling](/reservoir-sampling/), and it is a fair picture of this chapter: a short loop, a probability that has to be exactly right, and a proof that it is.

Sampling has two halves. One half chooses items from a finite collection: a subset of a list, a sample of a stream, an index picked by weight. The other half makes numbers with a given law from uniform numbers: normal heights, exponential waiting times, the faces of a loaded die. Both halves come back to the same few ideas. A random key per item turns sampling into selecting the smallest keys. Inversion maps a uniform number through a distribution function. Rejection proposes from an easy law and throws some proposals away, so the output is exact and only the running time is random. The chapter ends where sampling becomes a tool for estimation and for counting.

The chapter assumes a source of independent uniform integers or reals. Where those numbers come from, and how to draw a uniform integer in a range without bias, is the subject of [pseudorandom number generators](/pseudorandom-number-generator/) and [uniform random integer generation](/uniform-random-integer-generation/) in Volume 12. Random orders of a whole list, rather than subsets of it, are in the next chapter, which begins with the [Fisher-Yates shuffle](/fisher-yates-shuffle/).

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in five groups, read in order.

1. **Samples and designs.** [Random sampling](/random-sampling/) is the map: a sampling design is a law on subsets, and the Horvitz-Thompson estimator is unbiased for any design whose inclusion chances are known and positive. The [uniform random subset](/uniform-random-subset/) is the basic design. [Bernoulli sampling](/bernoulli-sampling/) keeps each item on its own coin, [systematic sampling](/systematic-sampling/) uses one random start, [stratified sampling](/stratified-sampling/) samples inside groups, and [PPS sampling](/probability-proportional-to-size-sampling/) favours large units.
2. **Variates from uniform numbers.** [Random variate generation](/random-variate-generation/) states the problem. [Inversion](/inverse-transform-sampling/) and [rejection](/rejection-sampling/) are the general methods. The [Box-Muller transform](/box-muller-transform/), the [polar method](/marsaglia-polar-method/) and the [ziggurat](/ziggurat-method/) make normal variates, and [ratio-of-uniforms](/ratio-of-uniforms/) and [adaptive rejection](/adaptive-rejection-sampling/) build envelopes for whole families of densities.
3. **Uniform subsets in one pass.** [Selection sampling](/selection-sampling/) scans a list of known length and keeps the input order. [Floyd's algorithm](/floyd-sampling/) takes $k$ draws for $k$ items from any range. [Stream sampling](/stream-sampling/) states the problem for streams of unknown length, reservoir sampling solves it, and [Algorithm L](/algorithm-l/) and [Vitter's skip methods](/vitter-reservoir-skip/) jump over the items that do not enter.
4. **Sampling by weight.** [Weighted sampling](/weighted-sampling/) separates the meanings of the phrase. The [alias method](/alias-method/) gives $O(1)$ draws after $O(n)$ setup. The [Gumbel-max trick](/gumbel-max-trick/) and [weighted reservoir sampling](/weighted-reservoir-sampling/) use random keys, and [priority sampling](/priority-sampling/) uses them to estimate the weight of any subset.
5. **Estimating and counting with samples.** [Latin hypercube sampling](/latin-hypercube-sampling/) stratifies every input at once, [importance sampling](/importance-sampling/) reweights draws from another law, the [Karp-Luby estimator](/karp-luby-estimator/) counts a union of large sets, and the [Jerrum-Valiant-Vazirani theorem](/jerrum-valiant-vazirani-theorem/) ties approximate counting to almost uniform sampling.

## A first reading

For a first pass, read [uniform random subset](/uniform-random-subset/), [selection sampling](/selection-sampling/), [Floyd's algorithm](/floyd-sampling/) and [reservoir sampling](/reservoir-sampling/), which give the three ways to choose $k$ of $n$ items. Then read [Bernoulli sampling](/bernoulli-sampling/) for the skip idea, [inverse transform sampling](/inverse-transform-sampling/), [rejection sampling](/rejection-sampling/) and the [Box-Muller transform](/box-muller-transform/), and finish with the [alias method](/alias-method/). Each of these entries has a small example worth working by hand. The skip methods, the weighted keys and the last group can wait for a second reading.

## Before you start

The probability is from Volume 1: [expected value](/expected-value/), [indicator random variables](/indicator-random-variable/), [linearity of expectation](/linearity-of-expectation/), [variance](/variance/), the [geometric distribution](/geometric-distribution/) and the [binomial distribution](/binomial-distribution/). The analyses of the reservoir methods use [harmonic numbers](/harmonic-series/), and the proofs of the normal generators use a [change of variables](/change-of-variables/). Volume 2's [randomized algorithms](/randomized-algorithm/) gives the vocabulary of expected running time, and rejection sampling is a [Las Vegas algorithm](/las-vegas-algorithm/) in the sense of Chapter 17.4. From Volume 3 you need [hash tables](/hash-table/) for Floyd's algorithm and the [binary heap](/binary-heap/) for the weighted reservoir. The estimators of the last group build on [Monte Carlo integration](/monte-carlo-integration/) in Volume 12, and the Karp-Luby estimator is the general form of the [Karp-Luby algorithm](/karp-luby-algorithm/) for DNF counting in Volume 14.
