## History

The sieve of Eratosthenes is credited to Eratosthenes of Cyrene, in the third century BC; his own account is lost, and the oldest surviving description is in the *Introduction to Arithmetic* of Nicomachus of Gerasa, from about 100 AD. For two thousand years the sieve was a method for making tables by hand. The other sieves of the first groups are recent. Sundaram's sieve was published in a note by V. Ramaswami Aiyar in 1934. Carter Bays and Richard Hudson described the segmented sieve in 1977. David Gries and Jayadev Misra published a linear sieve with a proof by invariants in 1978, and Paul Pritchard gave a sublinear sieve in 1981 and a family tree of the linear sieves in 1987. A. O. L. Atkin and Daniel Bernstein published their sieve based on quadratic forms in 2004.

Counting primes without listing them begins with Legendre, who gave his formula for $\pi(x)$ in 1808. In 1870 the astronomer Ernst Meissel added the correction that allows the recursion to stop at $x^{1/3}$, and he computed $\pi(10^8)$ by hand. D. H. Lehmer simplified the method in 1959 and computed $\pi(10^{10})$ on an IBM 701. Jeffrey Lagarias, Victor Miller and Andrew Odlyzko turned it into an algorithm with a proved bound of about $x^{2/3}$ steps in 1985, and Marc Deléglise and Joël Rivat improved it in 1996; Lagarias and Odlyzko also gave an analytic method with $x^{1/2 + \varepsilon}$ steps in 1987. The Lucy_Hedgehog method was posted on the Project Euler forum, as a way to sum the primes below a bound.

Sieve theory starts with Viggo Brun. In papers around 1915 to 1920 he truncated the inclusion-exclusion of Legendre's formula and proved that the sum of the reciprocals of the twin primes converges. Atle Selberg introduced his sieve with squared weights in 1947. A. A. Buchstab studied the integers without small prime factors in 1937, and N. G. de Bruijn returned to his function in 1950. The large sieve began with Linnik in 1941 and was developed by Rényi; Roth and Bombieri gave it the form of an inequality in 1965, and Bombieri used it to prove the theorem now called Bombieri-Vinogradov. Gallagher found a short proof in 1967, and Montgomery and Vaughan proved the sharp constant in 1973.

The limits of sieves became clear in the same period: Selberg pointed out the parity problem, which keeps sieves from separating primes from products of two primes. Results that go around it combine sieves with other input. Chen Jingrun proved in 1973 that every large even number is a prime plus a number with at most two prime factors, and Friedlander and Iwaniec showed in 1998 that $X^2 + Y^4$ takes infinitely many prime values. Yitang Zhang proved in 2014 that there are infinitely many pairs of primes at bounded distance, and James Maynard gave a simpler proof with a multidimensional form of Selberg's sieve in 2015.

## Further reading

- Richard Crandall and Carl Pomerance, *Prime Numbers: A Computational Perspective*, second edition. Covers practical sieving, segmented and wheel sieves, and the computation of $\pi(x)$ by combinatorial and analytic methods.
- Paul Pritchard, "Linear prime-number sieves: a family tree", *Science of Computer Programming* 9 (1987). Traces the linear sieves and their relation to the sieve of Eratosthenes.
- Marc Deléglise and Joël Rivat, "Computing $\pi(x)$: the Meissel, Lehmer, Lagarias, Miller, Odlyzko method", *Mathematics of Computation* 65 (1996). The form of the Meissel-Lehmer method that most later programs follow.
- Alina Carmen Cojocaru and M. Ram Murty, *An Introduction to Sieve Methods and Their Applications*. A short textbook on the sieves of Brun and Selberg and the large sieve, written for students.
- John Friedlander and Henryk Iwaniec, *Opera de Cribro*. The comprehensive modern treatment of sieve theory, including the parity problem.
