## History

Lattice reduction began as the reduction of quadratic forms. A basis $b_1, b_2$ of a lattice in the plane is the same thing as the positive definite binary form $\|x_1 b_1 + x_2 b_2\|^2$, and Lagrange showed in 1773 how to reduce such a form to one with small coefficients; Gauss developed the theory of reduced forms in the *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* of 1801. Hermite extended the reduction to $n$ variables in letters to Jacobi around 1850, and his bound on the shortest vector of a lattice is the origin of the constant that carries his name. Korkine and Zolotarev defined the stronger reduction now called HKZ in the 1870s and used it to find the extreme forms in dimensions 4 and 5. Minkowski's *Geometrie der Zahlen* of 1896 replaced the forms by convex bodies and lattices and proved the theorems on $\lambda_1$ and on the product of the successive minima.

The algorithmic turn came in 1982, when Arjen Lenstra, Hendrik Lenstra and László Lovász published "Factoring polynomials with rational coefficients". The reduction algorithm inside it relaxed the classical conditions just enough to run in polynomial time. Uses appeared within a few years. H. W. Lenstra solved integer programming in fixed dimension in 1983; Lagarias and Odlyzko attacked low-density knapsack systems in 1985; Babai gave the nearest plane and rounding methods for CVP in 1986; and Kannan (1983) and Fincke and Pohst (1985) gave enumeration algorithms for exact SVP. Schnorr introduced block reduction in 1987, and Schnorr and Euchner turned it into the practical BKZ algorithm, with floating point arithmetic, deep insertions and zig-zag enumeration, in 1994.

Integer relations have a separate line that rejoined this one. Ferguson and Forcade gave a generalization of the Euclidean algorithm that finds integer relations among $n$ reals in 1979, and Ferguson and Bailey's PSLQ algorithm of 1992, analyzed with Arno in 1999, became the standard tool of experimental mathematics. Coppersmith's method of 1996, in the simpler lattice form of Howgrave-Graham (1997), brought LLL into the analysis of RSA.

Complexity results arrived at the same time. Van Emde Boas proved CVP NP-hard in 1981 and asked whether the same holds for SVP in the Euclidean norm; Ajtai answered it in 1998 for randomized reductions. Two years earlier, Ajtai had shown that a random lattice problem is as hard as the worst case of an approximate one, which opened lattice-based cryptography. Ajtai, Kumar and Sivakumar gave the first sieve, in time $2^{O(n)}$, in 2001; Nguyen and Vidick (2008) and Micciancio and Voulgaris (2010) made sieving practical, and sieves with nearest neighbor search now hold the records for exact SVP. Gama and Nguyen's experiments of 2008 introduced the root Hermite factor as the measure by which attacks on lattice systems are estimated.

## Further reading

- Daniele Micciancio and Shafi Goldwasser, *Complexity of Lattice Problems: A Cryptographic Perspective*. A short book that proves the main hardness results and Ajtai's reduction with full details.
- Phong Q. Nguyen and Brigitte Vallée (editors), *The LLL Algorithm: Survey and Applications*. Survey chapters on the history of LLL, its floating point versions, BKZ, enumeration and the applications in cryptanalysis and number theory.
- Henri Cohen, *A Course in Computational Algebraic Number Theory*. Chapter 2 gives LLL, its integral version, Fincke-Pohst enumeration and integer relations as algorithms ready to program.
- Steven D. Galbraith, *Mathematics of Public Key Cryptography*. The lattice chapters cover reduction, CVP and Coppersmith's method from the point of view of attacks on public-key systems.
- J. W. S. Cassels, *An Introduction to the Geometry of Numbers*. The classical account of Minkowski's theorems, successive minima and reduction theory.
