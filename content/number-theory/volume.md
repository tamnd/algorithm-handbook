Number theory is the study of the integers: which numbers divide which, how the primes are spread, what happens to sums and products when only remainders are kept. For most of its history it was the purest part of mathematics, valued because it had no use. That changed in the 1970s. Public-key cryptography rests on number theory: a modern key generator tests large numbers for primality, an RSA key is safe only as long as its modulus cannot be factored, and Diffie-Hellman key exchange is safe only as long as discrete logarithms cannot be computed. Outside cryptography, the same algorithms compute hash functions, check identities in computer algebra, schedule calendars and fill tables of primes that analytic number theory then studies.

This volume collects the algorithms on integers and the facts they rest on. It starts with divisibility and Euclid's algorithm, the oldest algorithm still in use, and with arithmetic modulo $n$, the setting of almost everything that follows. Then come three problems that look alike and behave very differently: deciding whether a number is prime, which is easy; finding its factors, which seems hard; and computing discrete logarithms, which seems hard in a way that depends on the group. Sieves list and count primes over a whole range at once. The last chapter leaves the integers for lattices of integer vectors, whose reduction algorithms solve problems in all the other chapters.

## Why number theory gets a volume of its own

The algorithms of this volume are short, often a few lines, but their analysis uses ideas that other volumes meet only rarely. In this one volume you will meet:

- **Input size measured in bits.** A number $n$ has about $\lg n$ bits, so an algorithm that takes $\sqrt n$ steps is exponential. [Trial division](/trial-division/) is hopeless on a 1024-bit number, while the [Euclidean algorithm](/euclidean-algorithm/) and [modular exponentiation](/modular-exponentiation/) take time polynomial in $\lg n$.
- **Randomized algorithms with one-sided error.** The [Miller-Rabin test](/miller-rabin/) never calls a prime composite, and calls a composite prime with probability at most $1/4$ per round. A few rounds make the error smaller than the chance of a hardware fault.
- **Certificates.** A factor proves that a number is composite, and a [primality certificate](/primality-certificate/) proves that it is prime. Both can be checked much faster than they can be found.
- **Algorithms whose cost is a conjecture.** [Pollard's rho](/pollard-rho/), the [quadratic sieve](/quadratic-sieve/) and the [number field sieve](/general-number-field-sieve/) are fast in practice, but their running times rest on heuristics about how random numbers factor. The [smooth numbers](/smooth-number/) that they need are counted by the Dickman function.
- **Lower bounds in a model.** In the [generic group model](/generic-group-model/) every discrete logarithm algorithm needs about $\sqrt q$ group operations, and [baby-step giant-step](/baby-step-giant-step/) meets that bound. Index calculus beats it only because it looks at how elements are represented.
- **Approximation in high dimension.** The [LLL algorithm](/lll-algorithm/) finds a lattice vector at most $2^{(n-1)/2}$ times longer than the shortest. That factor is exponential, and still enough to factor polynomials and break cryptosystems.

## The chapters

**Chapter 10.1, Divisibility and GCD**, starts with [divisibility](/divisibility/) and the [greatest common divisor](/greatest-common-divisor/). It gives the Euclidean algorithm and its analysis by Lamé, the [extended Euclidean algorithm](/extended-euclidean-algorithm/) for Bézout's identity, unique factorization, the multiplicative functions such as the [Möbius function](/mobius-function/), and [continued fractions](/continued-fraction/). It ends with gcd algorithms for very large numbers and for millions of numbers at once.

**Chapter 10.2, Modular arithmetic**, works with remainders. It covers [congruences](/congruence/), fast exponentiation and the [modular inverse](/modular-inverse/), the [Chinese remainder theorem](/chinese-remainder-theorem/), the theorems of Fermat, Euler and Wilson, [primitive roots](/primitive-root/), and square roots modulo a prime through [quadratic residues](/quadratic-residue/) and reciprocity.

**Chapter 10.3, Primality**, decides whether a number is prime without factoring it. The [Fermat test](/fermat-primality-test/) is defeated by Carmichael numbers; the Miller-Rabin test is not, and it is the test that libraries use. The chapter also covers certificates from the factors of $n - 1$, the exact tests for Mersenne and Fermat numbers, and the [AKS test](/aks-primality-test/), which put primality in deterministic polynomial time.

**Chapter 10.4, Factorization**, finds the factors. It runs from trial division and [Fermat's method](/fermat-factorization/) through Pollard's rho and the methods based on smooth group orders, such as the [elliptic curve method](/elliptic-curve-factorization/), to the methods built on a [congruence of squares](/congruence-of-squares/): the quadratic sieve and the number field sieve, the fastest known.

**Chapter 10.5, Sieves**, works on a whole range of integers at once. The [sieve of Eratosthenes](/sieve-of-eratosthenes/) and the [linear sieve](/linear-sieve/) list primes and tabulate arithmetic functions; the [Meissel-Lehmer algorithm](/meissel-lehmer-algorithm/) counts the primes up to $x$ without listing them; and sieve theory, from Brun to Selberg, bounds the number of integers that survive a sieve.

**Chapter 10.6, Discrete logarithm**, inverts modular exponentiation. Baby-step giant-step, the [Pohlig-Hellman algorithm](/pohlig-hellman/) and Pollard's rho work in any group in about $\sqrt n$ steps; [index calculus](/index-calculus/) and the number field sieve work in finite fields in subexponential time; and pairings reduce the problem on some elliptic curves to a finite field.

**Chapter 10.7, Lattice reduction**, studies [lattices](/lattice/), the integer combinations of independent vectors. It defines the shortest and closest vector problems, gives Lagrange-Gauss reduction, the LLL algorithm, BKZ, enumeration and sieving, and applies them to integer relations, to small roots of polynomials by [Coppersmith's method](/coppersmith-method/), and to the hardness results behind lattice-based cryptography.

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course, read these entries in this order: the [greatest common divisor](/greatest-common-divisor/), the [Euclidean algorithm](/euclidean-algorithm/), the [extended Euclidean algorithm](/extended-euclidean-algorithm/), [congruence](/congruence/), the [modular inverse](/modular-inverse/), [modular exponentiation](/modular-exponentiation/), [Fermat's little theorem](/fermat-little-theorem/), the [Chinese remainder theorem](/chinese-remainder-theorem/), [primality testing](/primality-testing/), [Miller-Rabin](/miller-rabin/), [trial division](/trial-division/), [Pollard's rho](/pollard-rho/), the [sieve of Eratosthenes](/sieve-of-eratosthenes/), the [discrete logarithm problem](/discrete-logarithm-problem/), [baby-step giant-step](/baby-step-giant-step/), [Pohlig-Hellman](/pohlig-hellman/), the [lattice](/lattice/) and the [LLL algorithm](/lll-algorithm/). Do the exercises marked ◦ and • in the chapters you read. That is a complete first unit on computational number theory, enough to implement the arithmetic of a public-key system.

If you already know elementary number theory, start with the opening page of each chapter. It names the groups of entries in the chapter and the order to read them. The entries marked *Advanced* and *Research* lead to the subexponential algorithms, the general primality proofs and the hardness of lattice problems.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained. The chapters lean on each other more than in most volumes: the primality and discrete logarithm chapters use modular arithmetic throughout, and the factoring and logarithm chapters share smooth numbers and factor bases. Entries in other volumes are cited by number too, as [loop invariants](/loop-invariant/) in Volume 1 and [hash tables](/hash-table/) in Volume 3 are.

## What you need first

You should be able to read a short program and follow a proof by induction. From Volume 1 you need [asymptotic notation](/asymptotic-notation/), loop invariants and, for the randomized algorithms, [linearity of expectation](/linearity-of-expectation/) and the [birthday paradox](/birthday-paradox/), which sets the cost of every rho method. Hash tables from Volume 3 store the baby steps of baby-step giant-step. From Volume 11 you need [finite groups](/finite-group/) and [subgroups](/subgroup/) for orders and primitive roots, [polynomial rings](/polynomial-ring/) for the general primality proofs, and [Gaussian elimination](/gaussian-elimination/), [determinants](/determinant/) and [linear independence](/linear-independence/) for the linear algebra of the sieves and of lattices. Elliptic curves appear in several chapters; the entries say what they use of them.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
