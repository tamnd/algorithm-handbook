## History

The oldest idea in the chapter after trial division is Fermat's. In a letter of about 1643 he described how to write an odd number as a difference of two squares, and he split $2027651281 = 44021 \cdot 46061$ that way. In the 1920s Maurice Kraitchik saw that one does not need $x^2 - N$ to be a square: it is enough to find several values whose product is a square, which is the [congruence of squares](/congruence-of-squares/) behind every fast method of the twentieth century. D. H. Lehmer and R. E. Powers proposed in 1931 to take the values from the continued fraction of $\sqrt N$, and Michael Morrison and John Brillhart turned this into a computer method with linear algebra over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$, published in 1975 together with the factorization of the Fermat number $F_7$.

The 1970s also brought the methods that need little memory. John Pollard published the $p - 1$ method in 1974 and the [rho method](/pollard-rho/) in 1975, under the title "A Monte Carlo method for factorization". In 1974 Pollard, and Volker Strassen independently, gave the first deterministic method faster than $N^{1/2}$, with time about $N^{1/4}$; R. Sherman Lehman published his $N^{1/3}$ method the same year. Daniel Shanks developed SQUFOF in the same decade but never published it; Jason Gower and Samuel Wagstaff wrote down and analyzed it in 2008. Richard Brent sped up rho in 1980, and Brent and Pollard used the result to factor $F_8$, in a paper of 1981. Hugh Williams published the $p + 1$ method in 1982.

The analysis of smooth numbers made these methods comparable. Karl Dickman had found his function in 1930, and Knuth and Trabb Pardo studied the largest prime factors of random integers in 1976. John Dixon's random squares method, published in 1981, was the first with a rigorous subexponential running time. Carl Pomerance replaced the random choice by a sieve and published the [quadratic sieve](/quadratic-sieve/) in 1982; the multiple polynomial version, from work of Davis and Holdridge and of Peter Montgomery, was described by Robert Silverman in 1987. With it and a large prime variation, a volunteer project factored the 129-digit RSA challenge number in 1994. Hendrik Lenstra found the [elliptic curve method](/elliptic-curve-factorization/) in 1985, and it was published in the *Annals of Mathematics* in 1987, the same year Montgomery introduced the curves now used to run it.

Pollard proposed the number field sieve in 1988 for numbers of special form. Arjen Lenstra, Hendrik Lenstra, Mark Manasse and Pollard described it at STOC in 1990 and used it to factor $F_9$ that year, and Buhler, Lenstra and Pomerance extended it to general integers. The papers were collected in *The Development of the Number Field Sieve* in 1993. Since then the general number field sieve has set every record: RSA-130 in 1996, RSA-768 in 2009, RSA-240 in 2019 and RSA-250 in 2020. On the deterministic side, Bill Hart published his one line factoring algorithm in 2012, and David Harvey lowered the exponent of rigorous deterministic factoring to $1/5$ in 2021.

## Further reading

- Richard Crandall and Carl Pomerance, *Prime Numbers: A Computational Perspective*, second edition. Chapters 5 to 7 cover every method of this chapter, from rho to the number field sieve, with proofs and practical advice.
- Carl Pomerance, "A tale of two sieves", *Notices of the American Mathematical Society* (1996). A short and readable history of the quadratic sieve and the number field sieve by the inventor of the first.
- Arjen K. Lenstra and Hendrik W. Lenstra, Jr. (editors), *The Development of the Number Field Sieve*, Springer Lecture Notes in Mathematics (1993). The original papers on the number field sieve, collected in one volume.
- Hans Riesel, *Prime Numbers and Computer Methods for Factorization*, second edition. A practical book with worked examples of the classical methods and of continued fraction factoring.
- Samuel S. Wagstaff, Jr., *The Joy of Factoring*. An introduction to all the main methods at the level of an undergraduate course, with their history.
