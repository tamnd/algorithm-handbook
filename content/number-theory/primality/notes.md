## History

The modern tests start from a remark of Fermat. In a letter of 1640 he stated what is now [Fermat's little theorem](/fermat-little-theorem/), and its converse, read as a test, is the Fermat test. Euler found the factor $641$ of $F_5$ in 1732, which ended Fermat's belief that all the numbers $2^{2^k} + 1$ are prime. The distribution of primes was studied alongside: Chebyshev proved Bertrand's postulate in 1852, and Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin proved the prime number theorem independently in 1896. Erdős found his short proof of Bertrand's postulate in 1932.

Exact tests for numbers of special form came in the 1870s. Édouard Lucas gave tests for Mersenne numbers, with which he proved $2^{127} - 1$ prime by hand in 1876, and the converse of Fermat's theorem with an element of order $n - 1$; Pépin published his test for Fermat numbers in 1877, and Proth his theorem in 1878. Pocklington extended Lucas's idea to a partial factorization of $n - 1$ in 1914. D. H. Lehmer put Lucas's ideas in their modern form, with the prime factors of $n - 1$ in 1927 and the Mersenne test with $s_0 = 4$ in 1930, and Brillhart, Lehmer and Selfridge allowed a separate base for each prime in 1975. On the side of failures, Korselt gave his criterion in 1899, Carmichael published the example $561$ in 1910, and Alford, Granville and Pomerance proved in 1994 that there are infinitely many.

The randomized tests belong to the first years of complexity theory. Solovay and Strassen published the first test with an error bound for every input in 1977. Gary Miller gave a deterministic test in 1976 that is correct if the extended Riemann hypothesis holds; Michael Rabin turned it into the randomized test with the bound $1/4$, and Louis Monier proved the bound independently, both in papers of 1980. In the same year Pomerance, Selfridge and Wagstaff computed the pseudoprimes below $25 \cdot 10^9$, and with Baillie they proposed the combined test now called Baillie-PSW. Grantham defined Frobenius pseudoprimes and gave his random quadratic test in 1998.

Proofs of primality grew in parallel. Pratt showed in 1975 that every prime has a certificate of polynomial size, so the primes are in NP. Adleman, Pomerance and Rumely gave a deterministic test in time $(\log n)^{O(\log \log \log n)}$ in 1983, and Cohen and Lenstra made it practical with Jacobi sums. Goldwasser and Kilian brought in elliptic curves in 1986, and Atkin and Morain turned the idea into ECPP, published in 1993. In 2002 Agrawal, Kayal and Saxena found a deterministic polynomial-time test, published in 2004 as "PRIMES is in P".

## Further reading

- Richard Crandall and Carl Pomerance, *Prime Numbers: A Computational Perspective*, second edition. The standard reference for the whole chapter, from probable prime tests to ECPP and AKS, with proofs and many exercises.
- Victor Shoup, *A Computational Introduction to Number Theory and Algebra*, second edition. A careful textbook treatment of Miller-Rabin, random prime generation and the AKS test, with all the algebra they need.
- Alfred J. Menezes, Paul C. van Oorschot and Scott A. Vanstone, *Handbook of Applied Cryptography*. Chapter 4 covers probable prime tests, provable primes and prime generation as cryptographers use them.
- Andrew Granville, "It is easy to determine whether a given integer is prime", *Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society* 42 (2005). A survey of the AKS test and the ideas around it, written for a wide mathematical audience.
- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 2: Seminumerical Algorithms*, third edition. Section 4.5.4 treats factoring and primality testing together, with a careful analysis of the classical tests.
