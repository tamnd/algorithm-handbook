## History

Problems about remainders are older than any theory of them. The *Sunzi Suanjing*, a Chinese text from between the third and fifth centuries, asks for a number that leaves remainders $2$, $3$ and $2$ on division by $3$, $5$ and $7$, and gives $23$. Aryabhata described a method for such problems in sixth-century India, and Qin Jiushao gave a complete method, the *dayan* rule, in his *Mathematical Treatise in Nine Sections* of 1247. Wilson's theorem was stated by Ibn al-Haytham around 1000, published by Edward Waring in 1770 with credit to his student John Wilson, and proved by Lagrange in 1771.

Fermat stated his little theorem in a letter to Frénicle de Bessy in 1640, without proof. Euler published the first proof in 1736 and in 1763 proved the general form $a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$, introducing the totient for the purpose. Legendre introduced his symbol in 1798, and Euler and Legendre both stated the law of quadratic reciprocity. Gauss proved it in 1796, and his *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* of 1801 put the subject in the form it still has: the notation $a \equiv b \pmod n$, the Chinese remainder theorem in modern form, the existence of primitive roots, and reciprocity. Jacobi extended the symbol to composite moduli, and Kronecker in 1885 to every integer. Édouard Lucas published his theorem on binomial coefficients in 1878. Alberto Tonelli gave a square root algorithm in 1891 and Michele Cipolla another in 1907.

The algorithmic side came with computers and, above all, with public-key cryptography after 1976, when every operation became a modular exponentiation with a modulus of hundreds of digits. The binary method of exponentiation is ancient: Knuth traces it to Pingala's work on prosody, and a right-to-left form to al-Kashi in 1427. Harvey Garner described the residue number system and his conversion algorithm in 1959. Daniel Shanks gave the modern form of Tonelli's method in 1973. Michael Rabin showed in 1979 that computing square roots modulo $pq$ is as hard as factoring $pq$. Peter Montgomery published modular multiplication without trial division in 1985 and the batch inverse in 1987, and Paul Barrett presented his reduction at CRYPTO '86. Together these make the inner loop of RSA and of elliptic curve cryptography today.

Calendars are the oldest application. Joseph Scaliger proposed the Julian period in 1583, a year after Pope Gregory XIII introduced the Gregorian calendar; John Herschel made the day count a tool of astronomy in 1849. Fliegel and Van Flandern published integer formulas for the conversion in 1968, and Nachum Dershowitz and Edward Reingold built a whole system of calendars on fixed day numbers, first in 1990 and then in their book *Calendrical Calculations*.

## Further reading

- G. H. Hardy and E. M. Wright, *An Introduction to the Theory of Numbers*, sixth edition (2008). Chapters 5 to 8 cover congruences, Fermat's and Wilson's theorems, primitive roots and quadratic residues.
- Kenneth Ireland and Michael Rosen, *A Classical Introduction to Modern Number Theory*, second edition (1990). The group structure of $\mathbb{Z}_n^*$ and several proofs of quadratic reciprocity.
- Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest and Clifford Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, fourth edition (2022). Chapter 31 gives modular linear equations, the Chinese remainder theorem and repeated squaring as algorithms with proofs.
- Richard Crandall and Carl Pomerance, *Prime Numbers: A Computational Perspective*, second edition (2005). Chapters 2 and 9 treat modular arithmetic as it is done on big numbers, with Montgomery and Barrett reduction and square roots.
- Alfred J. Menezes, Paul C. van Oorschot and Scott A. Vanstone, *Handbook of Applied Cryptography* (1996). Chapter 14 is a practical account of modular multiplication, reduction and exponentiation.
- Nachum Dershowitz and Edward M. Reingold, *Calendrical Calculations*. The standard reference for calendar algorithms, built on day counts.
