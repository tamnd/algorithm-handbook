## History

Euclid's algorithm is among the oldest algorithms still in daily use. Book VII of Euclid's *Elements*, from about 300 BC, opens with the method of finding the greatest common measure of two numbers by repeated subtraction (Propositions 1 and 2), and Proposition 30 of the same book is Euclid's lemma. Indian mathematicians, starting with Aryabhata around 499, solved linear equations in integers by the *kuttaka*, a method that carries the Euclidean steps backward, much as the extended algorithm does. In Europe, Claude Gaspard Bachet de Méziriac gave the same method in the second edition of his *Problèmes plaisans et délectables* (1624). Étienne Bézout proved the analogous statement for polynomials in 1779, and the identity carries his name.

Euclid had the lemma but did not state unique factorization in its modern form. The first clear statement with a proof of uniqueness is usually credited to Gauss, in Article 16 of the *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801). The arithmetic functions came in the same century: August Möbius introduced his function in 1832, and Dirichlet proved in 1849, with what is now called the hyperbola method, that $\sum_{k \le n} d(k) = n \ln n + (2\gamma - 1)n + O(\sqrt n)$. How small the error term really is became Dirichlet's divisor problem. Voronoi lowered the exponent to $1/3$ in 1903, Hardy showed in 1916 that it cannot go below $1/4$, and the best established exponent is Huxley's $131/416$ from 2003.

The analysis of Euclid's algorithm is one of the first analyses of an algorithm. Gabriel Lamé proved in 1844 that the number of division steps is at most five times the number of decimal digits of the smaller input, using the Fibonacci numbers; Jeffrey Shallit's survey of 1994 traces earlier bounds, among them one by Finck in 1841. Fractions arranged by the mediant rule have a history of their own: John Farey stated the mediant property in a letter of 1816, after Haros had found the sequences in 1802, and Cauchy gave a proof. Moritz Stern described the Stern-Brocot tree in 1858, and Achille Brocot, a clockmaker, found it again in 1861 while designing gear trains. Neil Calkin and Herbert Wilf published the Calkin-Wilf tree in 2000.

The computer brought the gcd of long numbers. D. H. Lehmer showed in 1938 how to do most steps of Euclid's algorithm on the leading digits. Josef Stein published binary GCD in 1967. Knuth gave the first subquadratic gcd in 1970, and Arnold Schönhage improved it in 1971 to $O(n \log^2 n \log \log n)$ bit operations. The half-GCD for integers is delicate, and published versions contained errors; Thull and Yap gave a careful version in 1990, and Niels Möller a simpler one in 2008 that the GMP library adopted. Daniel J. Bernstein described batch GCD with product and remainder trees around 2004. In 2012 two groups, one led by Nadia Heninger and J. Alex Halderman and one by Arjen Lenstra, used it on millions of RSA keys collected from the internet and factored many of them, because faulty random number generators had given different keys a common prime.

## Further reading

- G. H. Hardy and E. M. Wright, *An Introduction to the Theory of Numbers*, sixth edition (2008). The classic account of divisibility, unique factorization, arithmetic functions, continued fractions and Farey sequences.
- Tom M. Apostol, *Introduction to Analytic Number Theory* (1976). Chapters 1 to 3 treat the gcd, Dirichlet convolution, Möbius inversion and averages of arithmetic functions, including the divisor problem, with full proofs.
- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 2: Seminumerical Algorithms*, third edition (1997). Sections 4.5.2 and 4.5.3 give Euclid's algorithm, binary GCD, Lehmer's method and their analysis, with the history.
- Ronald L. Graham, Donald E. Knuth and Oren Patashnik, *Concrete Mathematics*, second edition (1994). Chapter 4 covers divisibility, the Stern-Brocot tree, Legendre's formula and the Möbius function in the style of this chapter.
- Richard P. Brent and Paul Zimmermann, *Modern Computer Arithmetic* (2010). How big-number libraries compute gcds, from Lehmer's method to the subquadratic algorithms.
- Jeffrey Shallit, *Origins of the analysis of the Euclidean algorithm*, Historia Mathematica 21(4), 1994. The story of the first bounds on Euclid's algorithm.
