Number theory on a computer starts with one relation: $a$ divides $b$. From it come the divisors of a number, the greatest common divisor of two numbers, and the primes. The greatest common divisor is the first quantity in the subject that has a fast algorithm. Euclid's algorithm finds $\gcd(a, b)$ by repeated division with remainder, in a number of steps proportional to the number of digits, and it never factors anything. That is a striking fact, since no fast method is known to factor a number.

The gcd is everywhere once you look. Exact rational arithmetic divides out the gcd after every operation to keep fractions in lowest terms. A modular inverse, needed by RSA and by every hash or code that works modulo a prime, comes out of the extended Euclidean algorithm. In 2012 researchers computed the gcd of every pair among millions of RSA public keys found on the internet, and broke tens of thousands of them that shared a prime by accident.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups, read in order.

1. **Divisors and common divisors.** The definitions: [divisibility](/divisibility/), [divisors](/divisor/), the [greatest common divisor](/greatest-common-divisor/), [coprime integers](/coprime-integers/) and the [least common multiple](/least-common-multiple/), with the digit rules for divisibility by 3, 9 and 11.
2. **Euclid's algorithm.** The [GCD recursion theorem](/gcd-recursion-theorem/), the [Euclidean algorithm](/euclidean-algorithm/) and [Lamé's theorem](/lames-theorem/), which bounds its steps by about $\log_\varphi$ of the smaller input. [Binary GCD](/binary-gcd/) does the same job with shifts and subtractions.
3. **Bézout's identity and linear equations.** [Bézout's identity](/bezouts-identity/) writes the gcd as $ax + by$; the [extended Euclidean algorithm](/extended-euclidean-algorithm/) finds $x$ and $y$. This solves the [linear Diophantine equation](/linear-diophantine-equation/) $ax + by = c$ completely.
4. **Unique factorization.** [Euclid's lemma](/euclids-lemma/) and the [fundamental theorem of arithmetic](/fundamental-theorem-of-arithmetic/), with [Legendre's formula](/legendre-formula/) for the primes in $n!$ and the [Euclidean domains](/euclidean-domain/) where the same theory works.
5. **Arithmetic functions.** [Multiplicative functions](/multiplicative-function/), the [divisor function](/divisor-function/), the [Möbius function](/mobius-function/), [Möbius inversion](/mobius-inversion-formula/) and [Dirichlet convolution](/dirichlet-convolution/).
6. **Summing over divisors.** The [divisor summatory function](/divisor-summatory-function/) and the [hyperbola method](/dirichlet-hyperbola-method/) that computes it in $O(\sqrt n)$ steps.
7. **Continued fractions and trees of fractions.** [Continued fractions](/continued-fraction/) record the quotients of Euclid's algorithm; their [convergents](/continued-fraction-convergents/) are the best rational approximations. The [Farey sequence](/farey-sequence/), the [Stern-Brocot tree](/stern-brocot-tree/) and the [Calkin-Wilf tree](/calkin-wilf-tree/) list the fractions in lowest terms.
8. **The gcd of big numbers and of many numbers.** [Lehmer's GCD](/lehmers-gcd/), the subquadratic [half-GCD](/half-gcd/), and [batch GCD](/batch-gcd/) for millions of inputs at once.

Together they are the toolkit that the rest of the volume draws on. Groups 6 and 8, and the trees of fractions in group 7, can wait until you need them; they are marked *Advanced* or *Research*.

## Before you start

You need [division with remainder](/division-with-remainder/) and [floor division](/floor-division/) from Volume 11, and the [Fibonacci numbers](/fibonacci-numbers/) for Lamé's theorem. From Volume 1 you need [asymptotic notation](/asymptotic-notation/), [loop invariants](/loop-invariant/) and the [well-ordering principle](/well-ordering-principle/), which proves Bézout's identity. A few entries lean forward on later chapters of this volume: the Möbius function and the Farey sequence use [prime numbers](/prime-number/) and [Euler's totient](/euler-totient/), defined in Chapters 10.3 and 10.2. The big-number methods of the last group assume [integer multiplication](/integer-multiplication/) and the [subproduct tree](/subproduct-tree/) from Volume 11.
