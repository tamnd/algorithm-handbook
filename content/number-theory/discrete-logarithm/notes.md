## History

Discrete logarithms are older than cryptography. Gauss used them, under the name of indices, in the *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* of 1801, and Jacobi published a large table of indices in his *Canon Arithmeticus* of 1839. Maurice Kraitchik described the idea of index calculus, relations among small primes solved as linear equations, in the 1920s, and tables of indices were still being computed this way in the 1960s.

The problem became central in 1976, when Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman based their key exchange on it in "New directions in cryptography". Algorithms followed quickly. Daniel Shanks had described baby-step giant-step in 1971, for class groups. Stephen Pohlig and Martin Hellman published their reduction to prime order in 1978, which showed that $p - 1$ must have a large prime factor. In the same year John Pollard gave the rho and kangaroo methods in "Monte Carlo methods for index computation (mod p)". Leonard Adleman described index calculus for prime fields in 1979 with a subexponential running time.

The 1980s brought the methods of the second family to their first maturity. Don Coppersmith found the first $L(1/3)$ algorithm, for fields of characteristic 2, in 1984; Coppersmith, Odlyzko and Schroeppel gave $L(1/2, 1)$ methods for prime fields in 1986, and Pomerance proved a rigorous $L(1/2, \sqrt 2)$ bound in 1987. Andrew Odlyzko's survey of 1985 set out what these methods meant for key sizes. Elliptic curves were proposed for cryptography by Neal Koblitz and Victor Miller independently in the mid-1980s, in part because no index calculus was known for them. Daniel Gordon adapted the number field sieve to prime fields in 1993, Oliver Schirokauer improved it in the same year, and Adleman introduced the function field sieve in 1994.

The limits were explored from both sides. Nechaev in 1994 and Victor Shoup in 1997 proved that generic algorithms need about $\sqrt q$ operations, and Paul van Oorschot and Michael Wiener showed in 1994 how distinguished points let rho run on many machines. For curves, Menezes, Okamoto and Vanstone presented their pairing reduction in 1991, Frey and Rück generalized it in 1994, Frey proposed Weil descent in 1998, and Semaev introduced summation polynomials in 2004. In 2013 Antoine Joux found an $L(1/4)$ algorithm for small characteristic, and Barbulescu, Gaudry, Joux and Thomé published a heuristic quasi-polynomial algorithm in 2014. In 2015 the Logjam attack used the precomputation of the number field sieve against 512-bit Diffie-Hellman groups still offered by servers.

## Further reading

- Steven D. Galbraith, *Mathematics of Public Key Cryptography*. The standard reference for the square root methods, index calculus and the attacks on elliptic curves, with careful analyses and many exercises.
- Alfred J. Menezes, Paul C. van Oorschot and Scott A. Vanstone, *Handbook of Applied Cryptography*. Chapter 3 gives baby-step giant-step, rho, Pohlig-Hellman and index calculus as algorithms ready to implement.
- Andrew M. Odlyzko, "Discrete logarithms in finite fields and their cryptographic significance", *Advances in Cryptology: EUROCRYPT '84*. The classic survey of index calculus and its consequences, still a clear first account.
- Victor Shoup, "Lower bounds for discrete logarithms and related problems", *Advances in Cryptology: EUROCRYPT '97*. The short paper that proves the generic lower bound with random encodings.
