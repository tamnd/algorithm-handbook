A heater warms a metal block. In suitable units the temperature $x$ obeys $\dot x = -2x + u$, where $u$ is the heater power. A computer sets $u$ every $h = 0.5$ s and holds it until the next update. At the sampling instants the model is then exactly $x_{k+1} = e^{-1} x_k + \tfrac12 (1 - e^{-1}) u_k$, about $x_{k+1} = 0.368\, x_k + 0.316\, u_k$. Its step response, $0.316$, $0.432$, $0.475$, $\ldots$, is the continuous step response $\tfrac12(1 - e^{-2t})$ read at $t = 0.5, 1, 1.5$. Going from the equation to the discrete model is modeling.

Identification goes the other way, from records to a model. Take a plant with the unknown model $y(k) - 0.8\, y(k-1) = u(k-1) + v(k)$, where the disturbance $v(k) = e(k) + 0.5\, e(k-1)$ is colored. Drive it with a random $\pm 1$ input and fit $y(k) + a\, y(k-1) = b\, u(k-1)$ by least squares. With $N = 20000$ samples and seeds 1 to 5, the estimated pole $-\hat a$ came out between $0.8585$ and $0.8654$. A short calculation gives the limit $0.8590$, not $0.8$. The arithmetic is right. The method assumes white equation noise, and here that assumption is false. Instrumental variables built from delayed inputs gave poles between $0.7932$ and $0.8132$ on the same data. They scatter around the true value.

This chapter covers both directions. It describes linear and nonlinear dynamical systems by transfer functions, diagrams and state equations, and it solves and samples the linear ones exactly. Then it estimates models from data. Each identification method comes with the conditions under which it is consistent: the input must excite the system enough, and the noise must match what the method assumes. Outside those conditions a method can return a confident wrong answer, as least squares did above.

## A first reading

For a first pass, read the [Laplace transform](/laplace-transform/) and [poles and zeros](/poles-and-zeros/), then the [state-space representation](/state-space-representation/) and [linearization about an equilibrium](/jacobian-linearization/). Then read the [matrix exponential](/matrix-exponential/) and [zero-order hold discretization](/zero-order-hold-discretization/), which turn a continuous model into the discrete one a computer uses. For identification, start with the [system identification](/system-identification/) problem and the [process reaction curve](/process-reaction-curve/). Then read [persistent excitation](/persistent-excitation/), the [ARX model](/arx-model/) and its [least-squares fit](/least-squares-arx-identification/), and the [prediction error method](/prediction-error-method/), which covers the other structures. End with the [Ho-Kalman algorithm](/ho-kalman-algorithm/) to see how a state model is read off an impulse response. The rest of the chapter refines these ideas: more inputs and outputs, noise that is not white, feedback in the data, and models with little structure.
