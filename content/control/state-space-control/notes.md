## History

State-space control began around 1960 with Rudolf Kalman. In 1960 he introduced controllability and observability as properties of the matrices of a linear system and gave the rank test, and with Ho and Narendra (1963) he stated it in the form used today. Gilbert (1963) gave the modal tests for systems with distinct eigenvalues. Kalman's papers of 1962 and 1963 split a system into its four parts and showed that only the controllable and observable part shows in the transfer function, which settled what a minimal realization is. McMillan (1952) had already defined the degree of a rational matrix, and Ho and Kalman (1966) gave the first algorithm that builds a minimal realization from Markov parameters. The eigenvalue form of the rank test is named after Popov (1966), Belevitch (1968) and Hautus (1969).

Pole placement for one input was known in the early 1960s and is often credited to Rissanen (1960) and to Popov (1964). Wonham (1967) proved the theorem for several inputs: the eigenvalues of $A - BK$ can be placed at will exactly when the pair is controllable. Heymann (1968) reduced the multi-input case to one input, and Hautus (1977) gave the short proof of that lemma. Ackermann (1972) published the formula that carries his name. Kautsky, Nichols and Van Dooren (1985) showed that the choice of eigenvectors decides how sensitive the placed poles are, and gave the methods behind the pole placement routines of numerical libraries.

Luenberger introduced the observer in 1964. His 1966 paper treated systems with several outputs and the use of observers in feedback, where the eigenvalues of the controller and of the observer separate, and his tutorial of 1971 described both the full-order and the reduced-order observer. Gopinath (1971) gave the form of the reduced-order observer used in many textbooks. The Kalman-Bucy filter of 1961 has the same structure, with a gain chosen from noise statistics. Doyle (1978) showed with a two-state example that an LQG controller, which joins two designs with good margins, can have arbitrarily small gain margins.

The geometric approach began with Basile and Marro (1969) and with Wonham and Morse (1970), who solved the disturbance decoupling problem with the largest controlled invariant subspace and the algorithm that computes it. Francis and Wonham (1975) proved the internal model principle, Davison (1976) solved the robust servomechanism problem, and Francis (1977) showed that two linear matrix equations decide the output regulation problem.

Model order reduction by balancing is due to Moore (1981). Pernebo and Silverman (1982) proved that the truncated model is asymptotically stable, and Enns (1984) and Glover (1984) proved the error bound of twice the sum of the deleted Hankel singular values. Meier and Luenberger (1967) had found the first-order conditions for optimal reduction in the $H_2$ norm, and Gugercin, Antoulas and Beattie (2008) turned them into the iterative rational Krylov algorithm.

## Further reading

- Thomas Kailath, *Linear Systems*, Prentice-Hall, 1980. A long and careful treatment of state-space models, canonical forms, realization, and observers, with many worked examples.
- W. Murray Wonham, *Linear Multivariable Control: A Geometric Approach*, third edition, Springer, 1985. The standard source for controlled invariant subspaces, disturbance decoupling and regulation.
- Harry L. Trentelman, Anton A. Stoorvogel and Malo Hautus, *Control Theory for Linear Systems*, Springer, 2001. A modern account of the geometric approach, with stabilizability subspaces and the regulator problem.
- Kemin Zhou, John C. Doyle and Keith Glover, *Robust and Optimal Control*, Prentice Hall, 1996. Covers balanced realizations, the error bound of balanced truncation and Hankel-norm approximation, alongside LQG and robust control.
- Athanasios C. Antoulas, *Approximation of Large-Scale Dynamical Systems*, SIAM, 2005. A survey of model order reduction, from balanced truncation to Krylov and interpolation methods.
