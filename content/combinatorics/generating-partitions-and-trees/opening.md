Permutations and subsets are not the only objects a program may need to list. A test generator for a parser wants every binary tree with ten nodes. A scheduler that groups jobs wants every way to split twelve jobs into teams. A chemist wants every tree-shaped molecule with a given number of carbon atoms, each once up to symmetry. A graph theorist checks a conjecture on every graph with ten vertices. This chapter lists such objects: partitions of numbers and of sets, Young tableaux, trees of several kinds, and graphs.

The counts grow fast, but at very different rates. The number 100 has $190\,569\,292$ partitions, while it has $2^{99}$ compositions, the sums in which the order of the parts matters. A 10-set has $115\,975$ partitions into blocks. There are $16\,796$ binary trees with 10 nodes, $719$ rooted unlabeled trees and $106$ free trees, but $10^8$ labeled trees on 10 vertices. These counts decide, before a run starts, whether a full list is feasible.

One idea runs through the chapter: give each object a code, a sequence with simple local rules, and list the codes. A partition is a non-increasing sequence, a set partition is a restricted growth string, a binary tree is a string of balanced parentheses, a rooted tree is the sequence of its depths in preorder, and a labeled tree is its Prüfer code. When the objects are unlabeled, the code must be canonical, one chosen code for each class. For trees a cheap canonical code exists; for graphs the canonicity test is the main cost.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in nine groups, read in order.

1. **Partitions and their diagrams.** [Integer partitions](/integer-partitions/) and [compositions](/integer-compositions/), the [Ferrers diagram](/ferrers-diagram/) and its transpose, the [conjugate partition](/conjugate-partition/), then [Euler's partition theorem](/euler-partition-theorem/) and the [Durfee square](/durfee-square/), two identities proved with the diagram.
2. **How many partitions.** The [partition function](/integer-partition-function/) $p(n)$, its growth by the [Hardy-Ramanujan theorem](/hardy-ramanujan-theorem/), and the exact series of [Rademacher's formula](/rademacher-formula/).
3. **Generating partitions and compositions.** What [output-sensitive generation](/output-sensitive-generation/) means, then [Algorithm P](/integer-partition-generation/) for partitions and the [composition successor](/composition-generation/).
4. **Young tableaux.** [Young tableaux](/young-tableau/), the [generation of standard tableaux](/standard-young-tableaux/) of a given shape, and [Young's lattice](/youngs-lattice/) of all diagrams.
5. **Set partitions.** [Set partitions](/set-partitions/), their code as [restricted growth strings](/restricted-growth-string/), the [Stirling numbers](/stirling-numbers/), the [lexicographic generator](/set-partition-generation/) and the [partition lattice](/partition-lattice/).
6. **Balanced parentheses, ranking and unranking.** [Dyck words](/dyck-word/), their [generation](/balanced-parentheses-generation/) and [successor rule](/next-balanced-bracket-sequence/), and the technique of [ranking and unranking](/ranking-unranking/) applied to [rank](/balanced-bracket-sequence-ranking/) and [unrank](/kth-balanced-bracket-sequence/) the words.
7. **Binary, ordered and labeled trees.** [Binary trees](/binary-tree-generation/) and [ordered trees](/ordered-tree-generation/) through their Dyck words, [Zaks' algorithm](/zaks-tree-generation/) for $t$-ary trees, and [labeled trees](/labeled-tree-generation/) through [Prüfer decoding](/pruefer-decoding/).
8. **Unlabeled rooted and free trees.** The [level sequence](/level-sequence/), [rooted tree generation](/rooted-tree-generation/) and the [Beyer-Hedetniemi algorithm](/beyer-hedetniemi/), then [free tree generation](/free-tree-generation/) and the [Wright-Richmond-Odlyzko-McKay algorithm](/wright-richmond-odlyzko-mckay/).
9. **Graphs up to isomorphism.** The problem of [generating graphs](/graph-generation/), then [orderly generation](/orderly-generation/) and [canonical augmentation](/canonical-augmentation/), the two methods that list one graph per class without storing the others.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these thirteen entries in order: integer partitions, the Ferrers diagram, the conjugate partition, the partition function, output-sensitive generation, Algorithm P, set partitions, restricted growth strings, the set partition generator, Dyck words, the successor rule for balanced brackets, ranking and unranking, and Prüfer decoding. Then read the level sequence and the Beyer-Hedetniemi algorithm, which are advanced but short.

Most of the theory can wait for a second pass: the Durfee square, Young's lattice, the partition lattice and the Hardy-Ramanujan theorem. Rademacher's formula and canonical augmentation are research-level entries. Read them last, and only if you need an exact value of $p(n)$ or a graph generator of your own.
