Combinatorial algorithms work on finite objects that can be listed: permutations, subsets, partitions, trees, bit strings. A test suite tries a function on every ordering of its arguments. A scheduler tries every assignment of a few jobs to a few machines. A puzzle solver tries every placement of its pieces. A statistician who wants the exact distribution of a test statistic lists every way the data could have been labelled. Behind each of these sits one of two questions: how do I visit every object of a class exactly once, and how many objects are there?

This volume collects the algorithms that answer both. It starts with the generation of permutations and combinations, goes on to partitions, trees and Gray codes, then turns from listing to counting, and ends with exhaustive search, where the objects are visited only to find the few that matter.

## Why combinatorial generation gets a volume of its own

The objects are simple and the number of them is huge: there are $10! = 3{,}628{,}800$ orderings of ten items and more than six billion of thirteen. So a generator must spend little time per object, and the questions of the field are about that time and about the order in which the objects appear. In this volume you will meet:

- **Orders on objects.** [Lexicographic order](/lexicographic-order/) is the order of a dictionary. The [colexicographic order](/colexicographic-order/) compares from the other end. A [minimal-change order](/minimal-change-permutation-order/) makes neighbours differ as little as possible, for example by one swap of adjacent items.
- **Successor functions.** Given one object, compute the next. [Next permutation](/next-permutation/) does this for permutations in lexicographic order with a scan, a swap and a reversal.
- **Recursive generators.** Fix the first choice and generate the rest. [Heap's algorithm](/heaps-algorithm/) and the [recursive generation of combinations](/recursive-combination-generation/) are built this way.
- **Ranking and unranking.** A bijection between the objects and the integers $0, 1, \dots, N - 1$. With it you can jump to the millionth permutation, store a subset as one number or pick a random object. See [permutation ranking](/permutation-ranking/) and the [combinatorial number system](/combinatorial-number-system/).
- **Canonical forms.** When two objects count as the same, as unlabeled trees or graphs do, the generator must list one of each class. [Level sequences](/level-sequence/) give each rooted tree one code, and [orderly generation](/orderly-generation/) and [canonical augmentation](/canonical-augmentation/) do the same for graphs.
- **Counting without listing.** Often the number is wanted and the objects are not. A recurrence, a [generating function](/generating-functions/) or [Burnside's lemma](/burnside-lemma/) gives it in time polynomial in $n$ while the objects number exponentially many; for some classes, such as the perfect matchings of a graph, even counting is hard, and [approximation](/fpras/) is the best one can do.
- **Cost per object.** The total time is at least the number of objects, so the measure is the time between two outputs. [Constant amortized time](/constant-amortized-time-generation/) bounds the average; [loopless generation](/loopless-generation/) bounds every single step.

## The chapters

**Chapter 14.1, Generating permutations**, starts with the [factorial](/factorial/) and the problem of [generating all permutations](/generating-all-permutations/). It gives the [lexicographic successor](/lexicographic-permutation-successor/) and the next-permutation algorithm, which takes $\Theta(n)$ time in the worst case but $\Theta(1)$ amortized. It then lists permutations by one change at a time: Heap's algorithm makes one swap per step, and the [Steinhaus–Johnson–Trotter algorithm](/steinhaus-johnson-trotter/) one swap of adjacent items. The chapter also covers the [cycle decomposition](/permutation-cycle-decomposition/) and the [sign](/permutation-sign/) of a permutation, [permutations of a multiset](/multiset-permutations/), ranking and [unranking](/permutation-unranking/) in $O(n \log n)$ time, and [involutions](/generating-involutions/).

**Chapter 14.2, Generating combinations**, states the task of [combinatorial generation](/combinatorial-generation/) and applies it to [$k$-subsets](/k-subset-enumeration/), [fixed-weight binary strings](/fixed-weight-binary-strings/), the [power set](/power-set-enumeration/) and [combinations with repetition](/combinations-with-repetition/). It gives [lexicographic generation](/lexicographic-combinations/) and the [power set by binary counting](/power-set-by-recursion/), the colexicographic order and the combinatorial number system, and [ranking and unranking](/combinatorial-ranking-and-unranking/) of combinations. It ends with generators that spend $O(1)$ time on every step: [Ehrlich's loopless method](/ehrlich-loopless-combinations/) and the [cool-lex order](/cool-lex-order/).

**Chapter 14.3, Generating partitions and trees**, starts with [integer partitions](/integer-partitions/) and the [Ferrers diagram](/ferrers-diagram/), how fast the [number of partitions](/integer-partition-function/) grows, and the generators of [partitions](/integer-partition-generation/) and compositions. It goes on to [Young tableaux](/young-tableau/), [set partitions](/set-partitions/) coded as [restricted growth strings](/restricted-growth-string/), and [Dyck words](/dyck-word/), where the general method of [ranking and unranking](/ranking-unranking/) appears. It then generates trees: [binary](/binary-tree-generation/) and ordered trees, [labeled trees](/labeled-tree-generation/) through Prüfer codes, and [rooted](/rooted-tree-generation/) and [free](/free-tree-generation/) unlabeled trees through level sequences. It ends with [graphs up to isomorphism](/graph-generation/), by orderly generation and canonical augmentation.

**Chapter 14.4, Gray codes**, lists objects so that each differs from the one before in a single small change. It builds the [binary reflected Gray code](/binary-reflected-gray-code/), its [ruler sequence](/ruler-sequence/) and the [conversions](/gray-code-conversion/) between ranks and strings, and sees [cyclic Gray codes](/cyclic-gray-code/) as Hamiltonian cycles of the cube, among them the [balanced](/balanced-gray-code/) ones. [Loopless generation](/loopless-gray-code-generation/) with focus pointers extends to [$k$-ary](/k-ary-gray-code/) and mixed-radix strings. It ends with Gray codes for combinations, in order of how strict a step is: the [revolving door](/revolving-door-ordering/), [Chase's sequence](/chase-sequence/) and the [Eades–McKay order](/eades-mckay-algorithm/).

**Chapter 14.5, Counting algorithms**, turns from listing to counting. It starts with the [rules of counting](/rule-of-product/) and [binomial coefficients](/binomial-coefficient/): Pascal's triangle, the product formula and [their computation](/binomial-coefficient-computation/) modulo a prime. It builds the classical tools: [inclusion-exclusion](/inclusion-exclusion-principle/) and Möbius inversion, [lattice paths](/counting-lattice-paths/) with the [Catalan numbers](/catalan-numbers/) and the hook length formula, the [Stirling](/stirling-numbers-second-kind/), [Bell](/bell-numbers/) and [Eulerian](/eulerian-numbers/) numbers, [generating functions](/generating-functions/) and [counting partitions](/integer-partition-counting/). It ends with labelled structures such as [Cayley's formula](/cayley-formula/), counting up to symmetry with [Burnside's lemma](/burnside-lemma/) and [Pólya's theorem](/polya-enumeration-theorem/), and the hard counts: the [permanent](/permanent-computation/), [Kasteleyn's method](/kasteleyn-method/) for planar matchings, the [Karp–Luby estimator](/karp-luby-algorithm/) and [Barvinok's algorithm](/barvinok-algorithm/) for lattice points.

**Chapter 14.6, Exhaustive search**, visits the objects to find the few that matter. It starts with [search spaces](/search-space/), [brute force](/brute-force-search/), [generate and test](/generate-and-test/) and [exact exponential-time algorithms](/exact-exponential-algorithms/), then works two classic searches, the [$n$-queens problem](/n-queens-problem/) and [Golomb rulers](/golomb-ruler-problem/). It solves [exact cover](/exact-cover/) by [Algorithm X](/algorithm-x/) with [dancing links](/dancing-links/), lists all solutions with [output-sensitive enumeration](/output-sensitive-enumeration/) and [reverse search](/reverse-search/), counts [polyominoes](/polyomino-enumeration/) by [Redelmeier's method](/redelmeier-algorithm/), and ends with [maximal independent sets](/maximal-independent-set-enumeration/), minimal transversals and the [Fredman–Khachiyan algorithm](/fredman-khachiyan-algorithm/).

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course, read these entries in this order. From Chapter 14.1: [factorial](/factorial/), [lexicographic order](/lexicographic-order/), [generating all permutations](/generating-all-permutations/), [lexicographic successor](/lexicographic-permutation-successor/), [next permutation](/next-permutation/), [Heap's algorithm](/heaps-algorithm/), [the Steinhaus–Johnson–Trotter algorithm](/steinhaus-johnson-trotter/), [permutation ranking](/permutation-ranking/) and [unranking](/permutation-unranking/). From Chapter 14.2: [combinatorial generation](/combinatorial-generation/), [enumerating $k$-subsets](/k-subset-enumeration/), [the power set by binary counting](/power-set-by-recursion/), [recursive generation of combinations](/recursive-combination-generation/), [lexicographic generation of combinations](/lexicographic-combinations/), [colexicographic order](/colexicographic-order/), [the combinatorial number system](/combinatorial-number-system/) and [constant amortized time](/constant-amortized-time-generation/). From Chapter 14.4: [the binary reflected Gray code](/binary-reflected-gray-code/) and [Gray code conversion](/gray-code-conversion/). From Chapter 14.5: [binomial coefficients](/binomial-coefficient/), [inclusion-exclusion](/inclusion-exclusion-principle/), [Catalan numbers](/catalan-numbers/) and [generating functions](/generating-functions/). From Chapter 14.6: [brute-force search](/brute-force-search/) and [the $n$-queens problem](/n-queens-problem/). Do the exercises marked ◦ and • at the end of Chapters 14.1, 14.2 and 14.5. That is a complete first unit on combinatorial generation and counting; Chapters 14.3 and 14.6 and the rest of 14.4 are the second.

If you already know the basics, start with the opening page of each chapter. It names the groups of the chapter and the order to read them. The entries marked *Advanced* and *Research* are the place to go deeper.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained.

## What you need first

You should be able to read a short recursive program and follow a proof by induction. You should know what the binomial coefficient $\binom{n}{k}$ counts and how Pascal's rule $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n-1}{k-1} + \binom{n-1}{k}$ builds it; Chapter 14.5 proves both, but the earlier chapters use them from the start. From Volume 1 you need [asymptotic notation](/asymptotic-notation/), [recursion](/recursion/), [loop invariants](/loop-invariant/) and [amortized cost](/amortized-cost/). From Volume 2 you need the [generic backtracking algorithm](/generic-backtracking-algorithm/) and [bit manipulation](/bit-manipulation/). From Volume 6 you need [permutations](/permutation/), [inversions](/inversion/), the [Lehmer code](/lehmer-code/) and the [factorial number system](/factorial-number-system/). Ranking permutations fast uses a [Fenwick tree](/fenwick-tree/) from Volume 3.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
