## History

The reflected code is older than its name. In a booklet published in Lyon in 1872, Louis Gros linked the moves of the Chinese rings puzzle to binary numbers, and the states of the puzzle follow the reflected code; Knuth calls him the true inventor. Émile Baudot used the 5-bit code in a telegraph machine that he showed in 1878, and George Stibitz used it in a patent of 1943. Frank Gray of Bell Labs filed a patent for a pulse code tube in 1947, and the patent was issued in 1953 as US 2,632,058, Pulse code communication. It defined the code by reflection, and the name Gray code comes from it.

Programs that list the code came with the study of generation in general. Gideon Ehrlich's paper of 1973 in the Journal of the ACM defined loopless algorithms, in which the work between two visits is bounded in advance. James Bitner, Ehrlich and Edward Reingold applied the idea to the reflected code in 1976 in the Communications of the ACM, with the focus pointers of this chapter, and used them for combinations as well. Knuth's Section 7.2.1.1 presents the parity rule as Algorithm G, the focus pointers as Algorithm L, and their extension to mixed radices, with one direction flag per digit, as Algorithm H.

Combinations have their own line of work. D. T. Tang and C. N. Liu showed in 1973 that the strings of weight $k$ in the reflected code form a cyclic revolving door list, and W. H. Payne and F. M. Ives published a fast generator for it in 1979. The search for stronger rules began earlier. Éva Tőrők published a homogeneous scheme in Matematikai Lapok in 1968, Phillip J. Chase published the homogeneous generator TWIDDLE as Algorithm 382 of the Communications of the ACM in 1970, and Peter Eades and Brendan McKay gave their recursive homogeneous list in Information Processing Letters in 1984. Knuth reports that Chase found his near-perfect lists in 1976, and Chase published them in Congressus Numerantium in 1989. The strongest rule, adjacent moves only, was settled in 1984 by Buck and Wiedemann and by Eades, Hickey and Read, and Hough and Ruskey gave an efficient algorithm for the possible cases in 1988.

The view of a cyclic Gray code as a Hamiltonian cycle of the $n$-cube led to questions about the many other cycles. Tibor Bakos had a construction of balanced codes about 1955, published in a book by Ádám in 1968 and later forgotten. Wagner and West proved in 1991 that balanced codes exist when $n$ is a power of 2, and Girish Bhat and Carla Savage proved it for all $n$ in 1996. Haanpää and Östergård counted the cycles for $n = 6$ in 2014. Savage's survey of 1997 and Torsten Mütze's updated survey of 2023 collect these results and many open problems.

## Further reading

- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 4A: Combinatorial Algorithms, Part 1*, Addison-Wesley, 2011. Sections 7.2.1.1 and 7.2.1.3: binary and mixed radix Gray codes, the revolving door, Chase's sequence and the Eades-McKay order.
- Frank Ruskey, *Combinatorial Generation*, working version, University of Victoria, 1995 to 2003. Chapter 5 builds the reflected code and the Gray codes for combinations as recursions.
- Carla Savage, A survey of combinatorial Gray codes, *SIAM Review* 39(4), 605 to 629, 1997. Gray codes for strings, combinations, permutations and partitions.
- Torsten Mütze, Combinatorial Gray codes, an updated survey, *Electronic Journal of Combinatorics*, Dynamic Survey DS26, 2023. Transition counts, balanced codes, counts of Gray cycles and open problems.
- James R. Bitner, Gideon Ehrlich and Edward M. Reingold, Efficient generation of the binary reflected Gray code and its applications, *Communications of the ACM* 19(9), 517 to 521, 1976. The focus pointer method and its use for combinations.
- Girish S. Bhat and Carla D. Savage, Balanced Gray codes, *Electronic Journal of Combinatorics* 3(1), Research Paper 25, 1996. A construction of a balanced cyclic Gray code for every $n$.
