## History

The eight queens puzzle was posed by the chess player Max Bezzel in 1848, and Franz Nauck published the 92 solutions in 1850. Gauss took an interest in the problem around the same time. A general construction for every $n \ge 4$ goes back to Pauls (1874), and the survey of Jordan Bell and Brett Stevens (2009) collects the later results, from Pólya's toroidal theorem to the counts. The puzzle became the standard example of backtracking once computers could run such searches. Solomon Golomb and Leonard Baumert described the method in general terms in their paper "Backtrack programming" in the Journal of the ACM in 1965. Babcock's work of 1953 on radio channels led to the rulers that now carry Golomb's name.

The cost of the method was clear early. The Lighthill report of 1973 to the British Science Research Council named the combinatorial explosion as a main reason why the search programs of artificial intelligence did not scale. Karp's list of 21 NP-complete problems (1972) includes exact cover, so a general fast method was not to be expected. Exact algorithms with a smaller base of the exponent go back to the dynamic program of Held and Karp (1962) for the traveling salesman problem. Woeginger's survey (2003) and the book of Fomin and Kratsch (2010) made "exact exponential algorithms" a field of its own.

Exact cover became a practical tool through a small trick. Hitotsumatsu and Noshita (1979) undid deletions from doubly linked lists in a queens program. Knuth named the trick dancing links in his paper of 2000 and built Algorithm X on it. He returned to the subject in Section 7.2.2.1 of Volume 4B of The Art of Computer Programming (2022). Redelmeier (1981) counted polyominoes up to 24 cells with a method that stores no polyomino. Klarner (1967) proved that the growth constant exists, and Hammersley and Morton (1954) did the same for self-avoiding walks.

The theory of listing all solutions starts with the algorithm of Tsukiyama, Ide, Ariyoshi and Shirakawa (1977) for maximal independent sets, whose number had been bounded by Moon and Moser (1965). Johnson, Yannakakis and Papadimitriou (1988) gave the definitions of polynomial delay and incremental polynomial time that the field still uses. Avis and Fukuda introduced reverse search for the vertices of arrangements and polyhedra in 1992 and stated it in general in 1996, and McKay's canonical augmentation (1998) adapted the parent rule to objects up to isomorphism. For hypergraph duality, Fredman and Khachiyan (1996) gave the quasi-polynomial algorithm, and Eiter and Gottlob (1995) showed how many problems reduce to it. Whether it has a polynomial algorithm is still open.

## Further reading

- Donald E. Knuth, The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 4B: Combinatorial Algorithms, Part 2, Addison-Wesley, 2022. Section 7.2.2 treats backtrack programming with the queens as main example, and Section 7.2.2.1 treats dancing links and exact cover with many puzzles.
- Donald L. Kreher and Douglas R. Stinson, Combinatorial Algorithms: Generation, Enumeration, and Search, CRC Press, 1999. A textbook account of backtracking, pruning and exhaustive generation, with code.
- Fedor V. Fomin and Dieter Kratsch, Exact Exponential Algorithms, Springer, 2010. Branching, measure and conquer, dynamic programming over subsets and inclusion-exclusion, with their analysis.
- Jordan Bell and Brett Stevens, A survey of known results and research areas for n-queens, Discrete Mathematics 309(1), 2009. The history, the constructions and the variants of the queens problem.
- David Avis and Komei Fukuda, Reverse search for enumeration, Discrete Applied Mathematics 65, 1996. The general method, with polytopes, triangulations and spanning trees as examples.
- Yann Strozecki, Enumeration complexity, Bulletin of the EATCS 129, 2019. A short survey of the complexity classes of enumeration and of the methods that reach them.
