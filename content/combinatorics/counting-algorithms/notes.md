## History

The oldest tools of the chapter are the binomial coefficients. The arithmetic triangle was known in Indian, Persian and Chinese mathematics long before Blaise Pascal, whose treatise on it was published in 1665 and gave it his name. Zhu Shijie had the convolution identity in 1303, and Vandermonde published it in 1772. Euler found the pentagonal number theorem by expanding a product by hand in the 1740s and proved it around 1750, which gave the recurrence for the partition numbers. Édouard Lucas published his rule for binomial coefficients modulo a prime in 1878.

Many counts of paths and trees come from the late nineteenth century. Borchardt proved the formula $n^{n-2}$ for labelled trees in 1860 with determinants, Cayley stated it in 1889, and Prüfer gave the bijection with sequences in 1918. Joseph Bertrand posed the ballot problem in 1887, and Désiré André answered it in the same year with a short combinatorial argument; the reflection of a path across a line came in papers by Aebly and by Mirimanoff in 1923. Dvoretzky and Motzkin introduced the cycle lemma in 1947. Frame, Robinson and Thrall found the hook length formula in 1954, Greene, Nijenhuis and Wilf gave the hook walk proof in 1979, and Lindström (1973) and Gessel and Viennot (1985) gave the determinant for paths that do not touch.

Counting up to symmetry has a tangled history. The lemma named after Burnside was known to Cauchy in 1845 and published by Frobenius in 1887; Burnside put it in his book on finite groups in 1897. Redfield introduced the cycle index in 1927, and the paper went almost unnoticed until Pólya found the method on his own and published it in 1937, with chemical compounds, trees and graphs as examples. In the theory of partitions, MacMahon tabulated $p(n)$ for $n \le 200$ with Euler's recurrence, and Hardy and Ramanujan, in their paper of 1918, introduced the circle method and found that a few terms of their series gave $p(200)$ exactly. Rademacher made the series convergent in 1937.

The computer brought the question of cost. Ryser gave his inclusion-exclusion formula for the permanent in his book Combinatorial Mathematics in 1963, and Nijenhuis and Wilf ran it in Gray code order. In 1961 Kasteleyn, and independently Temperley and Fisher, counted the domino tilings of a rectangle, and in 1963 Kasteleyn showed that a Pfaffian counts the perfect matchings of any planar graph. Valiant proved in 1979 that the permanent is #P-complete, which explained why no polynomial method had been found. Approximation followed: Karp and Luby gave their estimator for DNF formulas in 1983, Jerrum, Valiant and Vazirani tied approximate counting to random generation in 1986, and Jerrum, Sinclair and Vigoda gave an FPRAS for the permanent of a nonnegative matrix in 2004. For lattice points, Barvinok gave a polynomial algorithm in each fixed dimension in 1994.

## Further reading

- Ronald L. Graham, Donald E. Knuth and Oren Patashnik, Concrete Mathematics: A Foundation for Computer Science, second edition, Addison-Wesley, 1994. Binomial identities, Stirling and Eulerian numbers, and generating functions, worked for the analysis of algorithms.
- Richard P. Stanley, Enumerative Combinatorics, Volume 1, second edition, Cambridge University Press, 2012. The standard reference for the twelvefold way, sieve methods and the counts of permutations by cycles and descents.
- Herbert S. Wilf, generatingfunctionology, third edition, A K Peters, 2006. A short and readable account of ordinary and exponential generating functions as a working tool.
- Philippe Flajolet and Robert Sedgewick, Analytic Combinatorics, Cambridge University Press, 2009. The symbolic method, which turns a description of a class into series equations, and the asymptotics read off those series.
- Frank Harary and Edgar M. Palmer, Graphical Enumeration, Academic Press, 1973. Labelled and unlabelled graphs counted with exponential generating functions and the Pólya enumeration theorem.
- Mark Jerrum, Counting, Sampling and Integrating: Algorithms and Complexity, Lectures in Mathematics ETH Zürich, Birkhäuser, 2003. Approximation schemes, the link between approximate counting and random sampling, and the Markov chain methods, coupling and canonical paths, that make sampling fast.
