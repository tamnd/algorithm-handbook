An automaton is a machine with a fixed amount of memory that reads its input one symbol at a time. A grammar is a set of rules that generates the strings of a language. A rewrite system is a set of rules that turns one term into another. This volume studies the three together, because they answer the same question from different sides: how a program can recognize, take apart and transform structured text.

The ideas run through much of everyday computing. The lexer of every compiler is a finite automaton, and its parser is built from a grammar, often by a parser generator. Text editors, `grep` and the validation code of web forms run regular expressions millions of times a day. Network protocols and hardware controllers are specified as state machines, and model checkers verify them by building automata. Natural language processing parses sentences with probabilistic grammars. Proof assistants, computer algebra systems and optimizing compilers simplify expressions with rewrite rules, and lazy functional languages are implemented by graph reduction.

The volume starts with finite automata and regular expressions, which describe the same class of languages. It moves up to context-free grammars and the parsers that real compilers use, then to the generators that build those parsers from a grammar. It ends with term rewriting, where the rules act on trees instead of strings. Entries in other volumes are cited by number, so a prerequisite such as [breadth-first search](/breadth-first-search/) from Volume 7 can be found at once.

## Why automata and parsing get a volume of their own

The machines here are simple enough that almost every question about them has an exact answer, and the answers draw a clear map of what can be computed with little memory. In this one volume you will meet:

- **State as all you remember.** A [deterministic finite automaton](/deterministic-finite-automaton/) keeps nothing but its current state. The [Myhill-Nerode theorem](/myhill-nerode-theorem/) says exactly how many states a language needs, and [Hopcroft's algorithm](/hopcroft-minimization/) finds that smallest machine in $O(\sigma n \log n)$ time.
- **What nondeterminism costs.** A [nondeterministic automaton](/nondeterministic-finite-automaton/) accepts no more languages than a deterministic one, but the [subset construction](/subset-construction/) may need $2^n$ states to remove the guessing. Simulating the guesses instead, as [Thompson's NFA simulation](/thompson-nfa-simulation/) does, keeps the time at $O(mn)$.
- **Two ways to match a pattern.** Automaton engines run in time linear in the text. [Backtracking matching](/backtracking-regex-matching/) supports more features but can take exponential time, which is the root of [ReDoS](/redos/) attacks.
- **Grammar classes chosen for their parsers.** The [LL(1)](/ll1-parsing/) and [LALR](/lalr-parsing/) conditions are exactly what a table-driven parser needs to decide each move from one symbol of lookahead, and so to run in linear time.
- **General parsing at cubic cost.** The [CYK algorithm](/cyk-algorithm/) and the [Earley parser](/earley-parser/) accept every context-free grammar, ambiguous ones included, in $O(n^3)$ time.
- **Where decidability ends.** Equivalence of DFAs takes almost linear time; for NFAs it is PSPACE-complete; for context-free grammars it is [undecidable](/cfg-equivalence-undecidable/), and so is [termination](/undecidability-of-termination/) of rewrite systems.
- **Equations turned into algorithms.** A rewrite system that is terminating and [confluent](/confluence/) decides its equational theory by computing normal forms, and [Knuth-Bendix completion](/knuth-bendix-completion/) tries to build such a system from the equations.

## The chapters

**Chapter 9.1, Finite automata**, defines the deterministic and nondeterministic machines and converts between them. It covers the [product construction](/product-construction/) and the closure properties it proves, the [pumping lemma](/pumping-lemma-for-regular-languages/), the decision problems for DFAs, and minimization. Later groups add automata with output, such as [Mealy machines](/mealy-machine/) and [transducers](/finite-state-transducer/), the [Büchi automaton](/buchi-automaton/) on infinite words, the [syntactic monoid](/syntactic-monoid/), and learning a DFA from queries with the [L* algorithm](/lstar-algorithm/).

**Chapter 9.2, Regular expressions**, is about the notation people actually type. It converts expressions to automata with [Thompson's construction](/thompson-construction/) and the [Glushkov automaton](/glushkov-automaton/), and back with [state elimination](/state-elimination/). It compares the matching engines: NFA simulation, the [lazy DFA](/lazy-dfa-construction/), [Brzozowski derivatives](/brzozowski-derivatives/) and backtracking. It explains which match a matcher reports and how it extracts submatches, and it ends with the hard questions: [equivalence](/regex-equivalence/), [minimization](/regex-minimization/) and [Kleene algebra](/kleene-algebra/).

**Chapter 9.3, Context-free parsing**, starts from the [context-free grammar](/context-free-grammar/), the [parse tree](/parse-tree/) and [ambiguity](/ambiguity/). It gives the [pushdown automaton](/pushdown-automaton/) and the theory of context-free languages, then the parsers: [recursive descent](/recursive-descent-parsing/) and LL(1) from the top, [shift-reduce](/shift-reduce-parsing/) and the LR family from the bottom, and the general algorithms CYK, Earley and [GLR](/glr-parsing/). It closes with probabilistic grammars, [attribute grammars](/attribute-grammar/) and [parsing expression grammars](/parsing-expression-grammar/).

**Chapter 9.4, Parser generators**, looks at the tools that build parsers from a grammar. It computes [FIRST and FOLLOW sets](/first-and-follow-sets/), builds the [LR item sets](/lr-item-sets/) and the [parsing table](/parsing-table/), and computes LALR lookaheads efficiently. [Yacc](/yacc/) is the model tool. The chapter then covers [error recovery](/parser-error-recovery/), [incremental reparsing](/incremental-reparsing/) for editors, [parser combinators](/parser-combinators/) and [GLL parsing](/gll-parsing/).

**Chapter 9.5, Term rewriting**, moves from strings to trees. It defines [terms](/term/), [rewrite rules](/rewrite-rule/) and [normal forms](/normal-form/), and the two properties that matter most, termination and confluence, with [Newman's lemma](/newman-lemma/) and [critical pairs](/critical-pair/) linking them. It covers reduction strategies, the lambda calculus and [combinatory logic](/combinatory-logic/), the orderings that prove termination, completion and [narrowing](/narrowing/), and the automata that match many patterns at once.

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course, read these entries in this order: [deterministic finite automaton](/deterministic-finite-automaton/), [nondeterministic finite automaton](/nondeterministic-finite-automaton/), [subset construction](/subset-construction/), [Kleene's theorem](/kleene-theorem/) and [Hopcroft's algorithm](/hopcroft-minimization/) in Chapter 9.1; [regular expression](/regular-expression/), [Thompson's construction](/thompson-construction/), [Thompson's NFA simulation](/thompson-nfa-simulation/) and [backtracking matching](/backtracking-regex-matching/) in Chapter 9.2; [context-free grammar](/context-free-grammar/), [pushdown automaton](/pushdown-automaton/), [CYK algorithm](/cyk-algorithm/), [recursive descent](/recursive-descent-parsing/), [LL(1) parsing](/ll1-parsing/) and [LALR parsing](/lalr-parsing/) in Chapter 9.3; [FIRST and FOLLOW sets](/first-and-follow-sets/) and [yacc](/yacc/) in Chapter 9.4; and [term rewriting system](/term-rewriting-system/), [critical pair](/critical-pair/) and Knuth-Bendix completion in Chapter 9.5. Read FIRST and FOLLOW sets before LL(1) parsing. Do the exercises marked ◦ and • at the end of each of these chapters. That is a complete first unit on automata and parsing.

If you know the basic automata already, start with the opening page of each chapter. It names the groups in the chapter and the order to read them. The entries marked *Advanced* and *Research* are the place to go deeper.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained.

## What you need first

You should be able to read a short program and follow a proof by induction on the length of a string or the size of a tree. From Volume 1 you need [asymptotic notation](/asymptotic-notation/), [structural induction](/structural-induction/) and, for termination proofs, [well-founded relations](/well-founded-relation/). From Volume 3 you need [equivalence relations](/equivalence-relation/), the [disjoint-set](/disjoint-set/) structure and [monoids](/monoid/). From Volume 7 you need [directed graphs](/directed-graph/), [graph reachability](/graph-reachability/), breadth-first and [depth-first search](/depth-first-search/), and [partition refinement](/partition-refinement/) for minimization. Strings and alphabets are defined in [alphabet](/alphabet/) in Volume 8. The hardness and undecidability results use the [Turing machine](/turing-machine/) from Volume 1, and [PSPACE](/pspace/) and the [halting problem](/halting-problem/) from the volume on complexity; you can take them on trust at first.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
