## History

Finite automata came from three directions at once in the 1940s and 1950s: models of neurons, the design of switching circuits, and logic. Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts described nets of idealized neurons in 1943. Stephen Kleene asked which sets of input sequences such nets can recognize, and in a RAND report of 1951, published in the volume *Automata Studies* in 1956, he answered with the "regular events" and proved what is now [Kleene's theorem](/kleene-theorem/). In the same years, engineers built sequential circuits as finite-state machines: David Huffman's work on switching circuits appeared in 1954, George Mealy's method for synthesizing sequential circuits in 1955, and Edward Moore's "Gedanken-experiments on sequential machines", in *Automata Studies*, in 1956. Moore's paper already contains the idea of telling states apart by experiments, the basis of [Moore's minimization algorithm](/moore-minimization/).

The theory took its modern form in 1957 to 1959. John Myhill (1957) and Anil Nerode (1958) characterized the regular languages by right congruences of finite index, which gives the [Myhill-Nerode theorem](/myhill-nerode-theorem/) and the minimal DFA. Michael Rabin and Dana Scott, in "Finite automata and their decision problems" (1959), introduced nondeterministic automata, proved by the [subset construction](/subset-construction/) that they accept only regular sets, defined the product of two automata, treated two-way automata, and studied decision problems; John Shepherdson showed the same year that two-way automata accept only regular languages too. Rabin and Scott received the Turing Award in 1976 for this paper. Robert McNaughton and Hisao Yamada gave the conversion from automata to expressions in 1960, and Janusz Brzozowski stated his double-reversal minimization in 1962.

The 1960s widened the model. Marcel-Paul Schützenberger introduced weighted automata in 1961, J. Richard Büchi defined automata on infinite words in his work on the decidability of a monadic second-order theory (1962), and Rabin defined probabilistic automata in 1963. In 1965 Kenneth Krohn and John Rhodes published their decomposition theorem, and Schützenberger characterized the star-free languages by aperiodic monoids; with the work of McNaughton and Papert (1971) on first-order logic, this began the algebraic theory of regular languages.

Algorithms and complexity came next. John Hopcroft gave the $O(n \log n)$ minimization algorithm in 1971, and Hopcroft and Richard Karp the near-linear equivalence test in a report of the same year. Albert Meyer and Larry Stockmeyer showed in 1972 and 1973 that equivalence of regular expressions, and so of NFAs, is hard, which places NFA universality and equivalence among the PSPACE-complete problems. Ashok Chandra, Dexter Kozen and Stockmeyer set out the theory of alternation in a paper of 1981, which includes alternating finite automata. Tao Jiang and B. Ravikumar proved in 1993 that NFA minimization is PSPACE-complete, and Sheng Yu, Qingyu Zhuang and Kai Salomaa started the systematic study of [state complexity](/state-complexity/) in 1994.

Learning came from another direction. E. Mark Gold showed in 1978 that finding a smallest DFA consistent with given examples is NP-hard, and Dana Angluin's L* algorithm (1987) showed that with membership and equivalence queries the minimal DFA can be learned in polynomial time. L* and its successors are now used to build models of software and protocols that are available only as black boxes.

## Further reading

- John E. Hopcroft, Rajeev Motwani and Jeffrey D. Ullman, *Introduction to Automata Theory, Languages, and Computation*, third edition. Chapters 2 to 4 cover DFAs, NFAs, the closure and decision properties, and minimization, with many worked examples.
- Michael Sipser, *Introduction to the Theory of Computation*, third edition. Chapter 1 is a short, clear first course on finite automata, nondeterminism and the pumping lemma.
- Dexter C. Kozen, *Automata and Computability* (1997). Short lectures, each on one topic; the lectures on the subset construction, the Myhill-Nerode theorem and minimization are models of careful proof.
- Jacques Sakarovitch, *Elements of Automata Theory* (2009). A deep treatment of automata, transducers and weighted automata, for the advanced groups of the chapter.
- Wolfgang Thomas, *Automata on Infinite Objects*, in the *Handbook of Theoretical Computer Science*, Volume B (1990). The standard survey of Büchi automata and their relatives.
