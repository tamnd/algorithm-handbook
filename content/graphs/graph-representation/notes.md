## History

Graph theory starts with a counting argument. In 1736 Leonhard Euler asked whether a walk through Königsberg could cross each of its seven bridges once. He counted, for each land region, the bridges that end there, and used the fact that these counts add up to twice the number of bridges, because each bridge has two ends. That is the [handshake lemma](/handshake-lemma/), and its parity form, that the regions with an odd count come in pairs, is what settles the question. Two other results of this chapter come from the nineteenth century. Gustav Kirchhoff introduced the matrix now called the [Laplacian](/laplacian-matrix/) in 1847, in his study of electrical networks, and showed that its cofactors count spanning trees. Arthur Cayley stated in 1889 that there are $n^{n-2}$ labelled trees on $n$ vertices, and Heinz Prüfer gave the bijective proof by [sequences](/prufer-sequence/) in 1918.

The twentieth century added the structural vocabulary. Hassler Whitney proved in 1932 that a connected graph is determined by its [line graph](/line-graph/), with the single exception of the triangle and the star with three leaves. The first book devoted to graphs, by Dénes Kőnig, appeared in 1936, and the language of vertices, edges, degrees and subgraphs settled into the form the textbooks of West, Diestel and Bondy and Murty use today.

The question of how to store a graph in a computer came with the first graph algorithms. Kruskal's minimum spanning tree method of 1956 and the shortest path methods of the 1950s read the graph as a list of edges. The [adjacency list](/adjacency-list/) became the standard form through the work of John Hopcroft and Robert Tarjan in the early 1970s: Tarjan's 1972 paper on depth-first search showed that with adjacency lists, depth-first search and the algorithms built on it run in time linear in $n + m$. Parent pointers, as used by Bernard Galler and Michael Fischer in 1964 for equivalence classes, and the correspondence between forests and binary trees that Knuth describes in *The Art of Computer Programming*, gave the standard forms for trees.

The packed forms come from numerical computing and from operations research. The compressed sparse row format is the Yale format of the Yale Sparse Matrix Package of the 1970s, and the [forward star](/forward-star/) is the same layout as it appears in codes for network flow problems. The hash-based form spread with libraries such as NetworkX, described by Aric Hagberg, Daniel Schult and Pieter Swart in 2008. The bit-parallel matrix goes back to the method of Arlazarov, Dinic, Kronrod and Faradzev in 1970 for Boolean matrix products.

The study of graphs in few bits began with György Turán's 1984 encoding of planar graphs in $O(n)$ bits and Guy Jacobson's 1989 paper on rank and select, which made such encodings queryable. At web scale, the Link Database built at Compaq around 2001 already coded the links of a crawl by gaps and by copying nearby lists, and Paolo Boldi and Sebastiano Vigna's WebGraph framework of 2004 refined these ideas into the standard tool, at about three bits per link.

## Further reading

- Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest and Clifford Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, fourth edition (2022). Appendix B.4 fixes the definitions, and Section 20.1 compares the list and the matrix; its exercises include the universal sink and the incidence matrix.
- Robert Sedgewick and Kevin Wayne, *Algorithms*, fourth edition (2011). Sections 4.1 and 4.2 build the graph type, the adjacency list and the symbol graph as working code.
- Kurt Mehlhorn and Peter Sanders, *Algorithms and Data Structures: The Basic Toolbox* (2008). Chapter 8 sorts the representations by the operations they support and makes the case for the adjacency array.
- Douglas B. West, *Introduction to Graph Theory*, second edition (2001). The first chapter is a careful introduction to the vocabulary, with degree sequences, line graphs and Prüfer codes.
- Ravindra K. Ahuja, Thomas L. Magnanti and James B. Orlin, *Network Flows: Theory, Algorithms, and Applications* (1993). Section 2.3 presents the forward and reverse star used throughout network optimization.
- Gonzalo Navarro, *Compact Data Structures: A Practical Approach* (2016). Its chapter on graphs covers succinct and compressed representations, including the web graph methods.
