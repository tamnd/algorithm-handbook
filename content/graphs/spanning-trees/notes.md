## History

The minimum spanning tree problem is older than computing. In 1926 the Czech mathematician Otakar Borůvka, asked how to lay out an economical electrical network in southern Moravia, published the method in which every fragment takes its cheapest outgoing edge. Vojtěch Jarník answered in 1930 with the method that grows a single tree from one vertex. Both papers were in Czech, and the second method became known in the West only when Robert Prim found it again at Bell Laboratories in 1957, and Edsger Dijkstra in 1959 in the paper that also gave his shortest-path algorithm. Joseph Kruskal's three-page paper of 1956 gave the method of taking edges in order of length, and also the reverse method of deleting the longest edges on cycles. It proved that the tree is unique when the lengths are distinct.

The theory that unites these methods came later. Robert Tarjan's 1983 monograph *Data Structures and Network Algorithms* stated the blue rule, which colours the lightest edge of a cut, and the red rule, which colours the heaviest edge of a cycle. It showed that any order of applying them gives a minimum tree. The deeper reason is that forests form a matroid, the setting in which Jack Edmonds characterized in 1971 exactly when the greedy algorithm is optimal. Ronald Graham and Pavol Hell's 1985 survey traced the history and restored the credit to Borůvka and Jarník.

From the 1970s the question was how fast the problem can be solved. Andrew Yao reached $O(m \log \log n)$ in 1975 by combining Borůvka's rounds with linear-time selection. Fredman and Tarjan's Fibonacci heaps gave $O(m \, \beta(m, n))$ in 1987, and Gabow, Galil, Spencer and Tarjan lowered it to $O(m \log \beta(m, n))$. Karger, Klein and Tarjan published a randomized linear-time algorithm in 1995, built on random sampling and the linear-time verification of Dixon, Rauch and Tarjan, which uses Komlós's bound on comparisons. Bernard Chazelle's soft heap gave a deterministic $O(m \, \alpha(m, n))$ algorithm in 2000, and Pettie and Ramachandran described in 2002 an algorithm whose running time is optimal, although no one knows what that time is.

For directed graphs, Yoeng-Jin Chu and Tseng-Hong Liu in 1965 and Jack Edmonds in 1967 independently gave the algorithm that contracts cycles of cheapest incoming arcs; Tarjan's 1977 implementation and the 1986 work of Gabow, Galil, Spencer and Tarjan made it faster. Counting goes back to Gustav Kirchhoff's 1847 paper on electrical networks, in which the matrix-tree theorem is implicit, and to Arthur Cayley's formula $n^{n-2}$ of 1889. Nash-Williams and Tutte both proved the tree packing theorem in 1961, and Nash-Williams the covering theorem in 1964. Random spanning trees were first sampled by random walks in the work of Broder (1989) and Aldous (1990), and David Wilson's loop-erased walks of 1996 gave a faster method.

## Further reading

- Robert E. Tarjan, *Data Structures and Network Algorithms* (1983), Chapter 6. The blue and red rules and the classical algorithms as instances of them, in a few pages.
- Ronald L. Graham and Pavol Hell, "On the history of the minimum spanning tree problem", *Annals of the History of Computing* (1985). How the early papers relate and who found what first.
- Jaroslav Nešetřil, Eva Milková and Helena Nešetřilová, "Otakar Borůvka on minimum spanning tree problem" (2001). English translations of Borůvka's two papers of 1926, with commentary.
- Jon Kleinberg and Éva Tardos, *Algorithm Design* (2005), Section 4.5 and the clustering section that follows it. The cut and cycle properties, reverse-delete, and an application to clustering.
- Alexander Schrijver, *Combinatorial Optimization* (2003). Spanning trees, arborescences, tree packing and covering, treated through matroids and polyhedra.
