## History

Tracking came to vision from radar and sonar. Kalman (1960) gave the linear filter that still predicts most tracks, and Kuhn (1955) the Hungarian method for the assignment problem. Reid (1979) wrote the hypothesis-oriented algorithm of multiple hypothesis tracking, and Fortmann, Bar-Shalom and Scheffe (1983) extended probabilistic data association from one target to several for sonar tracking in clutter. Radar texts of the 1980s, among them Blackman's book of 1986, solved the association of one scan as an optimal assignment, which Blackman later called global nearest neighbor. Bar-Shalom and Fortmann collected these methods in 1988. Cox and Hingorani (1996) made MHT practical with Murty's k-best assignments, and Mahler (2003) derived the PHD filter from finite set statistics; Vo, Singh and Doucet (2005) gave a particle form and Vo and Ma (2006) the Gaussian mixture form.

In vision, point tracking began with the registration step of Lucas and Kanade (1981). Tomasi and Kanade (1991) added a rule for choosing the windows, Shi and Tomasi (1994) justified it, and Baker and Matthews (2001, 2004) gave the inverse compositional algorithm and a framework for the variants. Isard and Blake presented CONDENSATION in 1996, a sample-based tracker of contours in clutter in the line of the particle filter of Gordon, Salmond and Smith (1993). Bradski (1998) built CAMShift to track faces at Intel, and Comaniciu, Ramesh and Meer (2000, 2003) tracked with mean shift on kernel-weighted histograms. For fixed cameras, Wren, Azarbayejani, Darrell and Pentland (1997) used one Gaussian per pixel in Pfinder, Stauffer and Grimson (1999) an adaptive mixture, and Barnich and Van Droogenbroeck (2009, 2011) the random sample sets of ViBe.

The 2010s brought fast learned models. Bolme, Beveridge, Draper and Lui (2010) fitted a correlation filter in closed form and tracked at several hundred frames per second. Henriques, Caseiro, Martins and Batista showed the circulant structure of all cyclic shifts (2012) and extended it to kernels (2015). Kalal, Mikolajczyk and Matas proposed the forward-backward error in 2010 and tracking-learning-detection in 2012. Bertinetto, Valmadre, Henriques, Vedaldi and Torr (2016) trained a fully convolutional Siamese network offline and tracked with no update.

Tracking many objects moved to detection. Zhang, Li and Nevatia (2008) linked all detections of a video by min-cost flow, and Pirsiavash, Ramanan and Fowlkes and Berclaz, Fleuret, Türetken and Fua (2011) gave faster shortest path solvers. Bewley, Ge, Ott, Ramos and Upcroft (2016) showed with SORT that a good detector, a Kalman filter and the Hungarian algorithm make a fast online tracker. Wojke, Bewley and Paulus (2017) added an appearance metric, and Zhang and co-authors (2022) used the low-score boxes in ByteTrack. The scores changed with the trackers: Bernardin and Stiefelhagen defined the CLEAR MOT metrics in 2008, Ristani and co-authors IDF1 in 2016, and Luiten and co-authors HOTA in 2021.

## Further reading

- Yaakov Bar-Shalom and Thomas E. Fortmann, *Tracking and Data Association*, Academic Press, 1988. The classical treatment of gating, PDA and JPDA, from radar and sonar.
- Alper Yilmaz, Omar Javed and Mubarak Shah, "Object tracking: a survey", *ACM Computing Surveys* 38(4), 2006. A map of single-target methods before learned trackers.
- Arnold W. M. Smeulders and co-authors, "Visual tracking: an experimental survey", *IEEE Transactions on Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence* 36(7), 2014. Many trackers compared on one set of videos.
- Wenhan Luo and co-authors, "Multiple object tracking: a literature review", *Artificial Intelligence* 293, 2021. Multi-object tracking by detection, its components and its benchmarks.
- Jonathon Luiten and co-authors, "HOTA: a higher order metric for evaluating multi-object tracking", *International Journal of Computer Vision* 129(2), 2021. Compares MOTA, IDF1 and HOTA on examples.
