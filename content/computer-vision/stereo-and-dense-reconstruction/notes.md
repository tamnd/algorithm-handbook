## History

Two-view stereo grew out of photogrammetry, which resampled image pairs to epipolar geometry long before computer vision did. In computer vision, Ohta and Kanade (1985) matched scanlines by dynamic programming, and Cox, Hingorani, Rao and Maggs (1996) gave the pixel-based version with a fixed price per occluded pixel. Zabih and Woodfill (1994) proposed the rank and census transforms as matching costs that ignore changes of brightness. Scharstein and Szeliski (2002) split dense stereo into matching cost, aggregation, optimization and refinement, and measured many methods on one benchmark; their taxonomy is still the usual way to describe a stereo method. Yoon and Kweon gave adaptive support weights in 2005, and Hirschmüller presented semi-global matching at CVPR 2005, with a journal version in 2008. SGM was built into hardware and became one of the most used stereo methods in practice. Barnes, Shechtman, Finkelstein and Goldman introduced PatchMatch in 2009 for image editing, and Bleyer, Rhemann and Rother turned it into PatchMatch stereo with slanted windows in 2011.

The energy view came from statistics. Besag (1986) proposed iterated conditional modes for the restoration of images, and Greig, Porteous and Seheult (1989) showed that a binary image can be restored exactly by a maximum flow. Roy and Cox (1998) solved stereo over a whole image with one maximum flow. Boykov, Veksler and Zabih introduced swap and expansion moves, with the bound $2c$, in their journal paper of 2001, and Ishikawa (2003) gave an exact cut for convex priors. Kolmogorov and Zabih described graph cut stereo with occlusions in 2001 and the general condition, regularity, for energies a cut can represent in 2004. Boykov and Kolmogorov (2004) gave the max-flow algorithm that made these cuts fast on image grids. QPBO rests on the roof duality of Hammer, Hansen and Simeone (1984); Kolmogorov and Rother (2007) reviewed its use in vision.

Light and focus give depth without a second camera. Horn's thesis of 1970 posed shape from shading, and Woodham (1980) introduced photometric stereo. Frankot and Chellappa (1988) enforced integrability when integrating normals, and Belhumeur, Kriegman and Yuille (1999) described the bas-relief ambiguity of unknown lights. Posdamer and Altschuler (1982) coded projector columns by binary patterns, the start of structured light scanning. Krotkov (1988) compared focus measures for automatic focusing, and Nayar and Nakagawa (1994) made shape from focus a dense depth method.

For many views, Laurentini (1994) defined the visual hull. Collins (1996) introduced the space sweep, which became plane sweep stereo. Seitz and Dyer (1997, journal version 1999) gave voxel coloring, and Kutulakos and Seitz (2000) gave space carving and the photo hull. Curless and Levoy (1996) fused range scans by averaging signed distances, and Newcombe, Izadi and co-authors (2011) ran the same update in real time in KinectFusion. Furukawa and Ponce presented PMVS in 2007, and Seitz, Curless, Diebel, Scharstein and Szeliski (2006) gave multi-view stereo its benchmark. Mildenhall and co-authors introduced neural radiance fields at ECCV 2020, and Kerbl, Kopanas, Leimkühler and Drettakis introduced 3D Gaussian splatting in 2023.

## Further reading

- Richard Szeliski, *Computer Vision: Algorithms and Applications*, second edition, Springer, 2022. The chapters on stereo correspondence and 3D reconstruction cover most of this chapter at textbook level.
- Daniel Scharstein and Richard Szeliski, "A taxonomy and evaluation of dense two-frame stereo correspondence algorithms", *International Journal of Computer Vision* 47, 2002. The best single paper for how two-view stereo methods are built and compared.
- Richard Hartley and Andrew Zisserman, *Multiple View Geometry in Computer Vision*, second edition, Cambridge University Press, 2004. The geometry behind rectification, plane-induced homographies and triangulation.
- Andrew Blake, Pushmeet Kohli and Carsten Rother (editors), *Markov Random Fields for Vision and Image Processing*, MIT Press, 2011. Graph cuts, move algorithms, QPBO and their use in vision, by the people who developed them.
- Yasutaka Furukawa and Carlos Hernández, "Multi-view stereo: a tutorial", *Foundations and Trends in Computer Graphics and Vision* 9, 2015. A survey of photo-consistency, depth map and patch methods and of fusion.
