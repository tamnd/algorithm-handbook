One unit of traffic goes from a town to a city by one of two roads. The first road takes one hour whatever the traffic. On the second road the travel time equals the fraction $x$ of drivers who use it. Every driver takes the second road, since it is never slower, and each trip takes an hour. A planner who sent half the drivers each way would make the average trip $3/4$ of an hour. The ratio $4/3$ between the two is the price of anarchy of this network. For latencies that are affine in the flow no network is worse: that is a theorem of Roughgarden and Tardos. The example asks the questions of this volume. What do self-interested agents do, can their choice be computed, how much does it cost compared with a plan, and how should rules such as auctions, elections and markets be designed so that what the agents choose is good.

This volume collects the algorithms and theorems of algorithmic game theory: equilibria and how to compute them, the cost of selfish behaviour, mechanisms and auctions, voting, fair division, matching and markets, and cooperative games. It has nine chapters and 292 entries.

## Why algorithmic game theory gets a volume of its own

Game theory and economics study what self-interested agents do. Computer science adds questions that change the answers.

- **Existence is not an algorithm.** Every finite game has a Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies, by a fixed-point theorem. Finding one is PPAD-complete already for two players. This is evidence that no polynomial algorithm exists, not a proof. A two-player zero-sum game is solved by one linear program, and so is the search for a correlated equilibrium.
- **Simple dynamics reach weaker equilibria.** When every player has no regret, the time average of play converges to the set of coarse correlated equilibria. That is a weaker notion than Nash, but a cheap one to reach.
- **Selfish behaviour has a worst case.** The price of anarchy bounds the ratio of the equilibrium cost to the optimum over a whole class of games, like an approximation ratio. The bound depends on the class of cost functions and on the kind of equilibrium.
- **Incentives constrain algorithms.** A mechanism that asks agents for private data must make honesty pay. When the best outcome is NP-hard to compute, an approximation can break truthfulness, and the design must handle both.
- **Some answers are impossibility theorems.** Arrow's theorem and the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem show that no rule for three or more alternatives has all of a short list of properties, so the question becomes which one to give up. Others say that a fair division need not exist, or exists only with an enormous protocol.

## The chapters

**Chapter 42.1, Games and solution concepts** (29 entries), defines the [normal-form game](/normal-form-game/), [mixed strategies](/mixed-strategy/) and the [Nash equilibrium](/nash-equilibrium/). It proves the [minimax theorem](/minimax-theorem/) and the [Nash existence theorem](/nash-existence-theorem/), from the [Brouwer fixed-point theorem](/brouwer-fixed-point-theorem/). It also covers [correlated](/correlated-equilibrium/) and [coarse correlated](/coarse-correlated-equilibrium/) equilibria, [Bayesian games](/bayesian-game/), [extensive-form games](/extensive-form-game/) with [subgame perfect equilibria](/subgame-perfect-equilibrium/), and refinements such as the [evolutionarily stable strategy](/evolutionarily-stable-strategy/).

**Chapter 42.2, Computing equilibria** (32 entries), solves [zero-sum games by linear programming](/zero-sum-game-linear-programming/) and finds correlated equilibria with [one LP](/correlated-equilibrium-linear-program/). For general bimatrix games it gives [support enumeration](/support-enumeration/) and the [Lemke-Howson algorithm](/lemke-howson-algorithm/), whose paths can be exponentially long ([Savani and von Stengel](/savani-von-stengel-theorem/)). It proves [PPAD-completeness](/ppad-completeness-of-nash/) through the [end-of-the-line problem](/end-of-the-line-problem/), and gives approximations such as the [Lipton-Markakis-Mehta algorithm](/lipton-markakis-mehta-algorithm/). Among the dynamics, [fictitious play converges in zero-sum games](/robinson-fictitious-play-theorem/) but not in general, and [no-regret dynamics](/no-regret-dynamics/) reach [coarse correlated equilibria](/no-regret-coarse-correlated-theorem/).

**Chapter 42.3, Price of anarchy and selfish routing** (27 entries), defines the [price of anarchy](/price-of-anarchy/) and [potential games](/potential-game/). In these games [best-response dynamics](/best-response-dynamics/) stops at a pure equilibrium, perhaps after exponentially many steps. Finding a pure equilibrium of a congestion game is [PLS-complete](/pls-completeness-of-congestion-games/). In nonatomic routing with affine latencies the [Wardrop equilibrium](/wardrop-equilibrium/) costs at most [4/3 times the optimum](/roughgarden-tardos-four-thirds-bound/). For atomic unweighted affine congestion games the pure price of anarchy is [5/2](/atomic-linear-congestion-price-of-anarchy/). The chapter also covers [Braess's paradox](/braess-paradox/), [tolls](/marginal-cost-tolls/) and the [smoothness framework](/smoothness-framework/).

**Chapter 42.4, Mechanism design** (30 entries), starts from [quasilinear utility](/quasilinear-utility/), the [revelation principle](/revelation-principle/) and [dominant-strategy incentive compatibility](/dominant-strategy-incentive-compatibility/). The [VCG mechanism](/vcg-mechanism/) is truthful and efficient, but not budget balanced, and its revenue can be low or zero. [Myerson's lemma](/myerson-lemma/) characterizes truthful single-parameter mechanisms. The impossibility results, the [Myerson-Satterthwaite](/myerson-satterthwaite-theorem/), [Green-Laffont](/green-laffont-theorem/) and [Roberts](/roberts-theorem/) theorems, hold under their exact hypotheses. The chapter ends with [truthful scheduling](/truthful-scheduling-unrelated-machines/) and the [Nisan-Ronen conjecture](/nisan-ronen-conjecture/), proved in 2023.

**Chapter 42.5, Auctions** (33 entries), covers the [Vickrey auction](/vickrey-auction/), which is truthful in dominant strategies, and the [first-price auction](/first-price-auction/), which is not; its [equilibrium bids](/first-price-auction-equilibrium/) hold in the symmetric model with independent private values. It covers [revenue equivalence](/revenue-equivalence-theorem/) under its conditions and [Myerson's optimal auction](/myerson-optimal-auction/) for independent private values, with [ironing](/ironing/) when the distribution is not [regular](/regular-distribution/). Then come the [Bulow-Klemperer theorem](/bulow-klemperer-theorem/), the [generalized second-price auction](/generalized-second-price-auction/) of sponsored search, which is not truthful, and [combinatorial auctions](/combinatorial-auction/), whose [winner determination](/winner-determination-problem/) is NP-hard.

**Chapter 42.6, Social choice and voting** (41 entries), covers [preference profiles](/preference-profile/), [Condorcet winners](/condorcet-winner/) and the common rules, from [plurality](/plurality-voting/) and the [Borda count](/borda-count/) to [Schulze](/schulze-method/) and [ranked pairs](/ranked-pairs/). [Arrow's theorem](/arrow-impossibility-theorem/) needs at least three alternatives, an unrestricted domain, Pareto and independence of irrelevant alternatives. [Gibbard-Satterthwaite](/gibbard-satterthwaite-theorem/) needs at least three possible winners. [Manipulation](/voting-manipulation-problem/) is NP-hard for some rules, but only in the worst case. [Kemeny aggregation](/kemeny-rank-aggregation/) is NP-hard and the winner of the [Dodgson rule](/dodgson-rule/) is $\Theta_2^p$-complete to decide. The chapter also treats [single-peaked preferences](/single-peaked-preferences/), [apportionment](/apportionment-problem/) and committee rules with [justified representation](/justified-representation/).

**Chapter 42.7, Fair division** (32 entries), goes from [cake cutting](/cake-cutting-problem/) with [divide and choose](/divide-and-choose/) and [Even-Paz](/even-paz-algorithm/) to the bounded envy-free [Aziz-Mackenzie protocol](/aziz-mackenzie-protocol/) for any number of agents, whose bound is a tower of exponentials. For indivisible goods, [EF1](/envy-freeness-up-to-one-good/) allocations always exist. [EFX](/envy-freeness-up-to-any-good/) is known for two agents and for three additive agents and is open beyond. [Maximin share](/maximin-share/) allocations [can fail to exist](/mms-nonexistence-theorem/), but a [constant fraction](/approximate-mms-allocation/) is always possible.

**Chapter 42.8, Matching markets and market equilibria** (36 entries), proves that [deferred acceptance](/deferred-acceptance-strategyproofness/) is strategy-proof for the proposing side only. By [Roth's theorem](/roth-impossibility-theorem/) no stable mechanism is strategy-proof for both sides. [Top trading cycles](/top-trading-cycles/) is strategy-proof and picks the unique strict core allocation. For markets, the [Eisenberg-Gale program](/eisenberg-gale-program/) solves linear [Fisher markets](/fisher-market/). [Arrow-Debreu equilibria](/arrow-debreu-existence-theorem/) exist under stated conditions, and finding one is [PPAD-hard](/ppad-hardness-of-arrow-debreu/) for additively separable piecewise-linear concave utilities. [Kidney exchange](/kidney-exchange-problem/) and [school choice](/school-choice-problem/) are the applications.

**Chapter 42.9, Cooperative games and cost sharing** (32 entries), studies [transferable utility games](/transferable-utility-game/). The [core](/core-of-a-cooperative-game/) can be empty, and the [Bondareva-Shapley theorem](/bondareva-shapley-theorem/) says exactly when. The [Shapley value](/shapley-value/) is the [unique](/shapley-uniqueness-theorem/) value with its axioms. Power indices are [#P-hard](/power-index-hardness/) for weighted voting games, but they have a [pseudo-polynomial dynamic program](/power-index-dynamic-programming/). The chapter also covers the [nucleolus](/nucleolus/), [bankruptcy rules](/bankruptcy-problem/), [Nash bargaining](/nash-bargaining-solution/) and the [Moulin mechanism](/moulin-mechanism/) for cost sharing.

## How to read this volume

Chapter 42.1 is the base for 42.2 and 42.3. Chapter 42.4 is the base for 42.5, and parts of 42.8 and 42.9 use its language of incentives. Chapters 42.6 and 42.7 can be read almost on their own. Chapters 42.8 and 42.9 lean on linear and convex programming more than on the earlier chapters.

For a first course, take these entries in order: [Nash equilibrium](/nash-equilibrium/), [minimax theorem](/minimax-theorem/), [correlated equilibrium](/correlated-equilibrium/), [zero-sum games by LP](/zero-sum-game-linear-programming/), [Lemke-Howson](/lemke-howson-algorithm/), [no-regret dynamics](/no-regret-dynamics/), [Pigou's example](/pigou-example/), [the 4/3 bound](/roughgarden-tardos-four-thirds-bound/), [VCG](/vcg-mechanism/), [Myerson's lemma](/myerson-lemma/), [Vickrey auction](/vickrey-auction/), [Myerson's optimal auction](/myerson-optimal-auction/), [Arrow's theorem](/arrow-impossibility-theorem/), [Gibbard-Satterthwaite](/gibbard-satterthwaite-theorem/), [cake cutting](/cake-cutting-problem/), [EF1](/envy-freeness-up-to-one-good/), [top trading cycles](/top-trading-cycles/), the [Eisenberg-Gale program](/eisenberg-gale-program/) and the [Shapley value](/shapley-value/). A second course can add PPAD-completeness of Nash, the smoothness framework, revenue equivalence, the Bulow-Klemperer theorem, Kemeny aggregation, the Aziz-Mackenzie protocol, Roth's theorem, the nucleolus and the Moulin mechanism. The learning path *Algorithmic game theory* goes through the volume step by step.

Each entry stands on its own; its facts box lists what it needs, by entry number.
