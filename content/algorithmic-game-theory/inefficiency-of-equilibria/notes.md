## History

The question is older than game theory. Pigou (1920) used a two-road example to argue that a tax on the crowded road could raise total welfare, and Knight (1924) answered that the same result follows when the road has an owner who charges for it. Wardrop (1952) stated the two principles of traffic assignment: at equilibrium no driver can lower their own travel time, and at the optimum the average travel time is least. Beckmann, McGuire and Winsten (1956) wrote the equilibrium as the solution of a convex program, which gave existence, the uniqueness of the edge latencies and the marginal cost form of the optimum. Braess (1968) found that a new road can slow every driver. Dafermos and Sparrow (1969) and later LeBlanc, Morlok and Pierskalla (1975) turned the convex program into algorithms for road networks, and Smith (1979) gave the variational inequality form of the equilibrium.

For atomic players, Rosenthal (1973) showed that every congestion game has a pure equilibrium, by the potential that now carries his name. Monderer and Shapley (1996) defined potential games in general, characterized the finite improvement property, and showed that every finite exact potential game is isomorphic to a congestion game.

The worst-case view came from computer science. Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou (1999) compared the worst equilibrium of a load balancing game on parallel links with the optimum, and called the ratio the coordination ratio; Papadimitriou (2001) named it the price of anarchy. Czumaj and Vöcking (2002) and Koutsoupias, Mavronicolas and Spirakis found the tight bounds for mixed equilibria. Roughgarden and Tardos (2002) proved the bound $4/3$ for nonatomic routing with affine latencies. Roughgarden (2003) showed that the worst case for a class of latencies is reached on two links, and Correa, Schulz and Stier-Moses (2004) gave the short proof without convexity. Roughgarden (2001, journal 2006) proved that detecting Braess's paradox is hard, and studied Stackelberg strategies in the same year.

Equilibrium computation and atomic bounds followed. Fabrikant, Papadimitriou and Talwar (2004) proved that finding a pure equilibrium of a congestion game is PLS-complete and solved symmetric network games by min-cost flow. Christodoulou and Koutsoupias, and independently Awerbuch, Azar and Epstein, proved the bound $5/2$ for atomic affine congestion games in 2005. Anshelevich, Dasgupta, Kleinberg, Tardos, Wexler and Roughgarden (2004) introduced the price of stability with the fair cost sharing game and its bound $H(n)$, and Fabrikant, Luthra, Maneva, Papadimitriou and Shenker (2003) introduced the network creation game. Roughgarden (2009) showed that most of these proofs use one smoothness inequality, which extends them to coarse correlated equilibria and to no-regret play. Koch and Skutella (2009) characterized Nash flows over time by thin flows.

## Further reading

- Noam Nisan, Tim Roughgarden, Éva Tardos and Vijay V. Vazirani (eds.), *Algorithmic Game Theory* (Cambridge University Press, 2007). Chapters 17 to 20 cover the price of anarchy, selfish routing, network formation games and load balancing, with notes on the literature.
- Tim Roughgarden, *Selfish Routing and the Price of Anarchy* (MIT Press, 2005). The nonatomic model in full: Wardrop equilibria, the Pigou bound, Braess's paradox and its hardness, Stackelberg strategies.
- Tim Roughgarden, *Twenty Lectures on Algorithmic Game Theory* (Cambridge University Press, 2016). Short lectures on selfish routing, smoothness, potential games and the complexity of equilibria.
- Martin Beckmann, C. B. McGuire and Christopher B. Winsten, *Studies in the Economics of Transportation* (Yale University Press, 1956). The convex program for traffic equilibrium and the marginal cost tolls.
- Tim Roughgarden, Intrinsic robustness of the price of anarchy, *Journal of the ACM* 62(5) (2015). The smoothness framework and its extension to correlated, coarse correlated and no-regret outcomes.
