## History

The subject began with Vickrey (1961), who showed that a sealed-bid auction in which the winner pays the second highest bid makes truthful bidding a dominant strategy. Hurwicz framed the general question in the 1960s and early 1970s: a designer who does not know the agents' private information chooses the rules of a game, and asks which outcomes can be reached when each agent follows its own interest. For public decisions, Clarke (1971) and Groves (1973) found the payments that make truth telling dominant and the efficient choice the result. Green and Laffont (1977) proved that on unrestricted domains these are the only efficient dominant-strategy mechanisms, and later showed that none of them balances the budget.

The revelation principle took shape in the same decade. Gibbard (1973) stated it for dominant strategies, on the way to his impossibility theorem for voting, and Myerson (1979) and others stated it for Bayes-Nash equilibrium. It turned the design of arbitrary games into the study of truthful direct mechanisms. d'Aspremont and Gerard-Varet (1979) and Arrow (1979) showed that Bayesian truthfulness allows efficiency with a balanced budget. Myerson (1981) characterized the incentive compatible auctions of one item with independent values and derived the revenue-optimal one, and Myerson and Satterthwaite (1983) proved that efficient bilateral trade needs a subsidy. Roberts (1979) showed that on unrestricted domains only affine maximizers are truthful, Rochet (1987) gave the cycle condition for general domains, Cremer and McLean (1985, 1988) showed what correlation allows, and Border (1991) characterized feasible interim rules. Maskin's 1977 paper on Nash implementation, published in 1999, opened implementation with complete information. Hurwicz, Maskin and Myerson shared the 2007 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for this body of work.

Computer scientists entered with Nisan and Ronen (1999), who asked for mechanisms that are truthful and computationally efficient at once. Their scheduling problem showed that the two goals can conflict, and their conjecture that VCG is the best deterministic truthful mechanism for unrelated machines was proved by Christodoulou, Koutsoupias and Kovacs at STOC 2023. Combinatorial auctions, where VCG needs an NP-hard winner determination, led to maximal-in-range and truthful-in-expectation mechanisms. Procaccia and Tennenholtz (2009) studied approximation without money, with facility location as the main example, building on Moulin's (1980) work on single-peaked preferences.

Computation also changed how mechanisms are found. Conitzer and Sandholm (2002) wrote the design problem for a concrete prior as a linear program. Kamenica and Gentzkow (2011) gave the concavification view of Bayesian persuasion, which leads to a similar linear program for the design of information. Dütting, Feng, Narasimhan, Parkes and Ravindranath (2019) trained neural networks as auctions, with incentive compatibility as a penalty on regret.

## Further reading

- Noam Nisan, Tim Roughgarden, Eva Tardos and Vijay V. Vazirani (eds.), *Algorithmic Game Theory*, Cambridge University Press, 2007. Chapters 9 to 13 cover mechanism design with and without money, combinatorial auctions, truthful approximation and revenue.
- Tim Roughgarden, *Twenty Lectures on Algorithmic Game Theory*, Cambridge University Press, 2016. A short course that starts with Myerson's lemma and VCG and keeps the proofs simple.
- Andreu Mas-Colell, Michael D. Whinston and Jerry R. Green, *Microeconomic Theory*, Oxford University Press, 1995. Chapter 23 is the standard economics treatment, including AGV and the participation constraints.
- Rakesh V. Vohra, *Mechanism Design: A Linear Programming Approach*, Cambridge University Press, 2011. Incentive constraints as networks and linear programs, with cycle monotonicity and Border's theorem.
- Noam Nisan and Amir Ronen, Algorithmic mechanism design, *Games and Economic Behavior* 35 (2001), 166-196. The paper that started the algorithmic side, with the scheduling problem.
