## History

The theory of strategic games began with two-player zero-sum games. Émile Borel wrote about mixed strategies in the early 1920s, and John von Neumann proved the minimax theorem in *Zur Theorie der Gesellschaftsspiele* (1928). Jean Ville gave a more elementary proof in 1938, in a note in Borel's treatise. Von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern then set out normal and extensive forms, and much of the language of the subject, in *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior* (1944).

John Nash extended the idea of a solution to any finite game with any number of players. His note *Equilibrium points in n-person games* (1950) proved existence with the fixed-point theorem that Shizuo Kakutani had published in 1941, and his paper *Non-cooperative games* (1951) gave a second proof with the theorem of L. E. J. Brouwer from 1911–1912. In the same years Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher ran the experiment at RAND that became the prisoner's dilemma, and Albert Tucker gave it its story. Much later, Robert Axelrod's tournaments made tit for tat famous, and Axelrod and William Hamilton wrote on the evolution of cooperation in 1981.

The 1950s to the 1970s widened the class of games and refined the solution concept. Harold Kuhn's *Extensive games and the problem of information* (1953) introduced information sets and proved that, with perfect recall, behaviour strategies are as good as mixed ones. John Harsanyi's three papers of 1967–1968 turned games with private information into Bayesian games. Reinhard Selten defined subgame perfection in 1965 and trembling-hand perfection in 1975. Robert Aumann defined correlated equilibrium in 1974, and Hervé Moulin and Jean-Philippe Vial the coarser notion in 1978. Nash, Harsanyi and Selten shared the Nobel prize in economics in 1994. The leader-follower model is older still: Heinrich von Stackelberg described it in *Marktform und Gleichgewicht* (1934).

Biology brought another reading of equilibrium. John Maynard Smith and George Price defined evolutionarily stable strategies in *The logic of animal conflict* (1973), and Peter Taylor and Leo Jonker linked them to the replicator equation in 1978. Richard McKelvey and Thomas Palfrey defined quantal response equilibrium in 1995, as a model of players who make mistakes that cost them little more often than mistakes that cost them much.

Computer science entered when the questions became ones of size and cost. Michael Kearns, Michael Littman and Satinder Singh introduced graphical games in 2001, to describe games with many players compactly. The complexity results of the next decade, which Chapter 42.2 covers, showed that the existence proofs of Nash and Kakutani give no efficient method, while correlated equilibria can be found by linear programming.

## Further reading

- Martin J. Osborne and Ariel Rubinstein, *A Course in Game Theory*, MIT Press, 1994. A short and exact textbook on strategic, Bayesian and extensive games, with clean definitions of every concept in this chapter.
- Michael Maschler, Eilon Solan and Shmuel Zamir, *Game Theory*, Cambridge University Press, 2013. A long textbook with full proofs, including the fixed-point theorems and Kuhn's theorem.
- Yoav Shoham and Kevin Leyton-Brown, *Multiagent Systems: Algorithmic, Game-Theoretic, and Logical Foundations*, Cambridge University Press, 2009. Covers the same concepts with an eye to computation, and leads into the next chapter.
- Noam Nisan, Tim Roughgarden, Éva Tardos and Vijay V. Vazirani (eds.), *Algorithmic Game Theory*, Cambridge University Press, 2007. The first chapters introduce the solution concepts and graphical games for a computer science reader.
- Josef Hofbauer and Karl Sigmund, *Evolutionary Games and Population Dynamics*, Cambridge University Press, 1998. The standard account of evolutionarily stable strategies and the replicator equation.
