## History

Émile Borel wrote about mixed strategies in a series of notes from 1921 and solved small symmetric games, but he did not prove a general theorem. John von Neumann proved the minimax theorem in 1928, with a topological argument, in the paper that started the theory of games. Jean Ville gave the first proof that uses only convexity in 1938, in a note in Borel's treatise, and von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern built their book *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior* (1944) on a proof of this kind.

Shizuo Kakutani proved his fixed point theorem for set-valued maps in 1941, to give a short proof of von Neumann's results. John Nash used it in his one-page note of 1950 to prove that every finite game with any number of players has an equilibrium, and his 1951 paper in the Annals of Mathematics gave the proof from Brouwer's theorem with the explicit improvement map. Von Neumann and Morgenstern had treated games with more than two players through coalitions. Nash's view, in which each player acts alone, became the basis of most later game theory, and he shared the 1994 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with John Harsanyi and Reinhard Selten.

The link between games and linear programming came in 1947, when von Neumann saw duality in George Dantzig's problem. Dantzig published the equivalence of the two problems in 1951, and Ilan Adler completed the reduction from linear programs to zero-sum games in 2013. Maurice Sion gave the general minimax theorem for quasi-concave and quasi-convex functions in 1958.

Robert Aumann introduced correlated equilibria in 1974. Unlike Nash equilibria, they are described by linear inequalities, a fact that matters for computation, as Chapter 42.2 shows.

## Further reading

- John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern, *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior*, Princeton University Press, 1944 (60th anniversary edition 2007). The founding book, with the theory of zero-sum games in full.
- Martin J. Osborne and Ariel Rubinstein, *A Course in Game Theory*, MIT Press, 1994. A short and rigorous course on strategic games, equilibria and extensive games.
- Yoav Shoham and Kevin Leyton-Brown, *Multiagent Systems: Algorithmic, Game-Theoretic, and Logical Foundations*, Cambridge University Press, 2009. Chapters on normal-form games and solution concepts written for computer scientists.
- Noam Nisan, Tim Roughgarden, Éva Tardos and Vijay V. Vazirani (eds.), *Algorithmic Game Theory*, Cambridge University Press, 2007. The standard reference for the algorithmic side of the whole volume; Chapter 1 introduces the solution concepts.
