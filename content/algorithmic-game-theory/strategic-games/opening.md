Two firms set prices, two drivers approach a narrow bridge, a bidder decides how much to offer, a poker player decides whether to bet. In each case the result for one side depends on what the others do, and each side knows this. Game theory gives such situations a precise form and asks which choices are stable: which profiles of choices no one wants to leave on their own. This chapter sets up that language. It defines games, strategies and the main solution concepts, from dominance and Nash equilibrium to correlated, Bayesian and subgame perfect equilibria, and it proves that equilibria exist. How to compute them is the subject of Chapter 42.2.

The chapter is mostly definitions and theorems, but each definition has a computational meaning that later chapters use. A dominated strategy can be found with one linear program. A Nash equilibrium of a two-player zero-sum game is a solution of a linear program, while for general games the existence proof uses a fixed-point theorem and gives no fast method. Correlated equilibria are again the solutions of linear inequalities. These differences decide what can be computed, and they explain why the rest of the volume uses some concepts more than others.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups, read in order.

1. **Games, strategies and best responses.** The [normal-form game](/normal-form-game/) and its two-player case, the [bimatrix game](/bimatrix-game/). A [mixed strategy](/mixed-strategy/) is a lottery over pure strategies, and a [best response](/best-response/) is optimal against fixed choices of the others.
2. **Dominance.** A [dominant strategy](/dominant-strategy/) is best whatever the others do. [Iterated elimination of dominated strategies](/iterated-elimination-of-dominated-strategies/) removes bad strategies in rounds; with strict dominance the order does not matter and no Nash equilibrium is lost.
3. **Nash equilibrium.** The [Nash equilibrium](/nash-equilibrium/), its [pure](/pure-nash-equilibrium/) form, the [prisoner's dilemma](/prisoners-dilemma/) as the standard example of an equilibrium that is bad for everyone, the [minimax theorem](/minimax-theorem/) for zero-sum games, and the [$\varepsilon$-Nash equilibrium](/epsilon-nash-equilibrium/).
4. **Why equilibria exist.** The [Brouwer](/brouwer-fixed-point-theorem/) and [Kakutani](/kakutani-fixed-point-theorem/) fixed-point theorems, and [Nash's theorem](/nash-existence-theorem/) that every finite game has a mixed equilibrium.
5. **Correlated equilibria.** The [correlated equilibrium](/correlated-equilibrium/) of Aumann, in which a mediator draws a profile, and the larger set of [coarse correlated equilibria](/coarse-correlated-equilibrium/).
6. **Private information.** [Bayesian games](/bayesian-game/), in which players have private types, and the [Bayes-Nash equilibrium](/bayes-nash-equilibrium/).
7. **Games in extensive form.** The [extensive-form game](/extensive-form-game/) with [information sets](/information-set/) and [perfect recall](/perfect-recall/); the [subgame perfect equilibrium](/subgame-perfect-equilibrium/), the [Stackelberg equilibrium](/stackelberg-equilibrium/) of a leader who commits, [Kuhn's theorem](/kuhn-theorem-behavioral-strategies/) on behaviour strategies, and the [trembling-hand perfect equilibrium](/trembling-hand-perfect-equilibrium/).
8. **Evolution, noise and large games.** The [evolutionarily stable strategy](/evolutionarily-stable-strategy/) and the [replicator dynamics](/replicator-dynamics/), the [quantal response equilibrium](/quantal-response-equilibrium/), and [graphical games](/graphical-game/) for many players with local interactions.

The equilibrium concepts nest. For a finite game, every pure Nash equilibrium is a mixed Nash equilibrium, every mixed Nash equilibrium is a correlated equilibrium, and every correlated equilibrium is a coarse correlated equilibrium. The larger sets are easier to compute and to reach by learning, and the smaller ones say more about play.

You need [expected values](/expected-value/), [probability distributions](/probability-distribution/) and [conditional probability](/conditional-probability/) from Volume 1, and [convex sets](/convex-set/) from Volume 13 for the fixed-point theorems.
