Nine voters rank three candidates. Four rank $a$ first, then $b$, then $c$; three rank $c$, $b$, $a$; two rank $b$, $c$, $a$. Under plurality $a$ wins with four first places. Under instant-runoff voting $b$ is eliminated first, its two ballots go to $c$, and $c$ wins five to four. The Borda count elects $b$, and so does every rule that elects the [Condorcet winner](/condorcet-winner/), since $b$ beats $a$ five to four and $c$ six to three in head-to-head votes. The ballots are the same in all four cases; only the rule differs. Social choice is the study of such rules: what they do, which properties they can have together, and how hard they are to compute and to manipulate.

The same questions arise wherever rankings or approvals must be combined: elections to a parliament or a club, the seats each state gets in a legislature, a committee chosen to represent a town, a budget split between projects, or one ranking of web pages built from the rankings of several search engines. The chapter mixes two traditions. The axiomatic one, from Condorcet and Borda to Arrow, asks which properties a rule can satisfy at once. The computational one asks for the running time of a rule, and finds that some natural rules, such as Kemeny's and Dodgson's, are NP-hard or harder to evaluate, while for others the manipulation problem is NP-hard in the worst case.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in nine groups.

1. **Preferences and majorities.** The [preference profile](/preference-profile/), [social welfare functions](/social-welfare-function/), the Condorcet winner, and the [Condorcet paradox](/condorcet-paradox/): majority preferences can form a cycle.
2. **Simple voting rules.** [Plurality](/plurality-voting/), the [Borda count](/borda-count/), [approval voting](/approval-voting/) and [instant-runoff voting](/instant-runoff-voting/), all fast to count; plurality and approval voting take time linear in the size of the ballots.
3. **Axioms and impossibility theorems.** [May's theorem](/may-theorem/) for two candidates, [Arrow's theorem](/arrow-impossibility-theorem/) and the [Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem](/gibbard-satterthwaite-theorem/) for three or more, and the [Condorcet jury theorem](/condorcet-jury-theorem/).
4. **Rules based on pairwise majorities.** The [Smith set](/smith-set/), [Copeland's method](/copeland-method/), [ranked pairs](/ranked-pairs/) and the [Schulze method](/schulze-method/), all polynomial and all Condorcet consistent.
5. **Consensus rankings and distances.** [Kemeny rank aggregation](/kemeny-rank-aggregation/), [Slater's rule](/slater-rule/), [KwikSort](/kwiksort-rank-aggregation/) and [Dodgson's rule](/dodgson-rule/): exact rules that are hard to compute, and an approximation.
6. **Single-peaked preferences.** The domain on which majority voting behaves well: [single-peaked preferences](/single-peaked-preferences/), the [median voter theorem](/median-voter-theorem/), [recognition](/single-peaked-recognition/) of such profiles, and the strategyproof [generalized median voter rules](/generalized-median-voter-rule/).
7. **The computational difficulty of strategic behaviour.** [Manipulation](/voting-manipulation-problem/), [control and bribery](/election-control-problem/), and the [metric distortion](/metric-distortion/) of voting rules.
8. **Apportionment.** The [apportionment problem](/apportionment-problem/), [Hamilton's method](/hamilton-method/) and the [Alabama paradox](/alabama-paradox/), the divisor methods of [D'Hondt](/dhondt-method/), [Sainte-Lague](/sainte-lague-method/) and [Huntington-Hill](/huntington-hill-method/), and the [Balinski-Young theorem](/balinski-young-theorem/).
9. **Committees and proportional representation.** [Single transferable vote](/single-transferable-vote/), [justified representation](/justified-representation/), the [Chamberlin-Courant rule](/chamberlin-courant-rule/), [proportional approval voting](/proportional-approval-voting/), [Phragmen's sequential method](/phragmen-sequential-method/), the [method of equal shares](/method-of-equal-shares/) and [participatory budgeting](/participatory-budgeting/).

## A first reading

Read these fourteen entries in order: [preference profile](/preference-profile/), [Condorcet winner](/condorcet-winner/), [Condorcet paradox](/condorcet-paradox/), [plurality voting](/plurality-voting/), [Borda count](/borda-count/), [instant-runoff voting](/instant-runoff-voting/), [May's theorem](/may-theorem/), [Arrow's impossibility theorem](/arrow-impossibility-theorem/), [Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem](/gibbard-satterthwaite-theorem/), [Copeland's method](/copeland-method/), [Schulze method](/schulze-method/), [single-peaked preferences](/single-peaked-preferences/), [median voter theorem](/median-voter-theorem/) and [D'Hondt method](/dhondt-method/). They give the main rules, the two impossibility theorems and the way around them by a restricted domain. On a first pass, skip the consensus rules of group 5, the complexity results of group 7 and the committee rules of group 9 except single transferable vote; the apportionment group can be read on its own at any time.

## Before you start

The rules are short algorithms on small matrices, and the chapter assumes the tools to analyse them. From Volume 6 you need [permutations](/permutation/) and the [Kendall tau distance](/kendall-tau-distance/). Several Condorcet methods are graph algorithms from Volume 7: [strongly connected components](/strongly-connected-components/) for the Smith set, [transitive closure](/transitive-closure/) for ranked pairs, the [widest path](/widest-path/) and [Floyd-Warshall](/floyd-warshall/) for the Schulze method, and the [feedback arc set problem](/feedback-arc-set-problem/) for Kemeny's and Slater's rules. The hardness results use [NP-completeness](/np-complete/) from Volume 39, the jury theorem uses the [binomial distribution](/binomial-distribution/) and [Hoeffding's inequality](/hoeffding-inequality/) of Volume 1, and the divisor methods use a [priority queue](/priority-queue/) (Volume 3). The Borda count and Kemeny's rule are also methods of [rank aggregation](/rank-aggregation/) in Volume 35. Strategyproofness is the [dominant-strategy incentive compatibility](/dominant-strategy-incentive-compatibility/) of Chapter 42.4.
