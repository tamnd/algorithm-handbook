## History

The theory of voting began in the French Academy of Sciences. Borda read a paper on elections there in 1770, and his memoir on the rule that now bears his name appeared in 1784. Condorcet answered in his Essai of 1785. He showed that majorities between pairs can form a cycle, proposed electing the candidate who beats every rival, and proved the jury theorem on the accuracy of majority decisions. In 1876 Charles Dodgson, the Lewis Carroll of the Alice books, proposed electing the candidate who needs the fewest swaps to become a Condorcet winner. Thomas Hare published his single transferable vote in 1857, and Henry Droop argued for a smaller quota in 1881. In the 1890s Thiele in Denmark and Phragmen in Sweden designed proportional rules for approval ballots, the rules now called PAV and Phragmen's method.

Apportionment has its own history, mostly in the United States. Congress passed Hamilton's method in 1792, and Washington vetoed it, so Jefferson's method was used for the first House. Webster proposed rounding at one half in 1832. Hamilton's method returned in 1850, and the Alabama paradox appeared in 1880. Hill proposed the method of equal proportions in 1911, Huntington built its theory, and Congress adopted it in 1941. D'Hondt proposed his rule for party lists in Belgium and Sainte-Lague derived his in 1910. Balinski and Young turned the subject into an axiomatic theory in the 1970s, beginning with the quota method of 1975, and proved their impossibility theorem in Fair Representation (1982).

The modern theory starts with Duncan Black's paper of 1948 on single-peaked preferences and the median voter, and with Kenneth Arrow. Arrow stated his impossibility theorem in 1950 and gave the full treatment in Social Choice and Individual Values (1951); he shared the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1972. Kenneth May characterized majority rule for two candidates in 1952. Kemeny proposed his consensus ranking in 1959. Gibbard (1973) and Satterthwaite (1975) proved that every non-dictatorial rule with at least three possible outcomes can be manipulated, and Moulin (1980) described the strategyproof rules on single-peaked preferences.

Computation entered in 1989. Bartholdi, Tovey and Trick showed that Dodgson and Kemeny winners are NP-hard to find, and that manipulating some rules is NP-hard, which suggested that hardness could protect an election. Bartholdi and Orlin showed in 1991 that manipulating single transferable vote is NP-complete, and Bartholdi, Tovey and Trick studied control in 1992. Hemaspaandra, Hemaspaandra and Rothe placed the Dodgson winner problem in $\Theta_2^p$ in 1997. Dwork, Kumar, Naor and Sivakumar brought Kemeny aggregation to web search in 2001, and Ailon, Charikar and Newman gave KwikSort in 2005.

Committee elections became a field of their own from 2015. Aziz, Brill, Conitzer, Elkind, Freeman and Walsh defined justified representation and its extensions and showed that PAV satisfies EJR. Brill, Freeman, Janson and Lackner proved PJR for Phragmen's sequential method in 2017. Peters and Skowron introduced the rule now called the method of equal shares in 2020, and Wieliczka in Poland used it for its participatory budget in 2023.

## Further reading

- Felix Brandt, Vincent Conitzer, Ulle Endriss, Jérôme Lang and Ariel D. Procaccia (eds.), *Handbook of Computational Social Choice*, Cambridge University Press, 2016. The standard reference for the computational side: tournament solutions, Kemeny and Dodgson, manipulation, control and bribery.
- Kenneth J. Arrow, *Social Choice and Individual Values*, Wiley, 1951; second edition, Yale University Press, 1963. The source of the impossibility theorem, and still readable.
- Michel L. Balinski and H. Peyton Young, *Fair Representation: Meeting the Ideal of One Man, One Vote*, 2nd edition, Brookings Institution Press, 2001. The history of apportionment in the United States and the axiomatic theory behind the divisor methods.
- Martin Lackner and Piotr Skowron, *Multi-Winner Voting with Approval Preferences*, Springer, 2023. A short book on approval-based committee rules and the proportionality axioms, with tables of which rule meets which axiom.
- Edith Elkind, Martin Lackner and Dominik Peters, Preference restrictions in computational social choice: a survey. Single-peaked, single-crossing and other structured domains, with their recognition algorithms.
