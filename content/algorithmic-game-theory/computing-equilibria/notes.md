## History

The first algorithms in the chapter came soon after the minimax theorem. George Dantzig published a proof that zero-sum games and linear programs are equivalent in 1951, in the Cowles Commission volume *Activity Analysis of Production and Allocation*. In the same year George Brown proposed fictitious play as a way to compute the value of a game, and Julia Robinson proved that it converges in every zero-sum game. Lloyd Shapley showed in 1964 that it can cycle in a $3 \times 3$ game that is not zero-sum.

Path following came next. Carlton Lemke and Joseph Howson published their algorithm for bimatrix games in 1964, and Lemke extended it to general linear complementarity problems in 1965. The method also gave a constructive proof that a nondegenerate game has an odd number of equilibria. Herbert Scarf used a similar path on simplicial subdivisions in 1967 to compute approximate fixed points, building on Sperner's lemma of 1928, and Harold Kuhn and others simplified the method. For zero-sum games in extensive form, Daphne Koller, Nimrod Megiddo and Bernhard von Stengel developed the sequence form in the 1990s, which made such games solvable by an LP of size linear in the tree.

Complexity theory asked whether these paths had to be long. Michael Hirsch, Christos Papadimitriou and Stephen Vavasis proved in 1989 that black-box methods for Brouwer fixed points need exponentially many queries. Papadimitriou defined the class PPAD in 1994, with END OF THE LINE as its complete problem. Itzhak Gilboa and Eitan Zemel had shown in 1989 that questions about equilibria with given properties are NP-hard, and Vincent Conitzer and Tuomas Sandholm extended this list in 2003 and 2008. Savani and von Stengel built games with exponentially long Lemke-Howson paths in 2004. The main result came in 2006: Constantinos Daskalakis, Paul Goldberg and Papadimitriou proved PPAD-completeness for games with 3 or more players, and Xi Chen, Xiaotie Deng and Shang-Hua Teng for two players; the journal versions appeared in 2009. Papadimitriou and Tim Roughgarden gave the ellipsoid against hope for correlated equilibria of succinct games in 2008, and Albert Jiang and Kevin Leyton-Brown made it exact in 2015.

Approximation and learning grew alongside. Richard Lipton, Evangelos Markakis and Aranyak Mehta showed in 2003 that small supports suffice for $\varepsilon$-equilibria, which gives a quasi-polynomial algorithm. Constantinos Daskalakis, Aranyak Mehta and Christos Papadimitriou gave the $1/2$ algorithm in 2006, a line of later work brought the constant down to about $1/3$, and Aviad Rubinstein proved the quasi-polynomial lower bound under an exponential time hypothesis for PPAD in 2016. On the learning side, David Blackwell's approachability theorem of 1956 and James Hannan's work of 1957 started no-regret learning. Sergiu Hart and Andreu Mas-Colell introduced regret matching in 2000, Avrim Blum and Yishay Mansour gave their reduction from swap regret to external regret in 2007, and Daskalakis, Maxwell Fishelson and Noah Golowich proved polylogarithmic regret for optimistic Hedge in games in 2021. Conitzer and Sandholm showed in 2006 that optimal commitments are found by linear programs, and Christopher Kiekintveld, Milind Tambe and their colleagues scaled the method to large security games in 2009.

## Further reading

- Noam Nisan, Tim Roughgarden, Éva Tardos and Vijay V. Vazirani (eds.), *Algorithmic Game Theory*, Cambridge University Press, 2007. Chapters 2 to 4 cover the complexity of equilibria, the Lemke-Howson algorithm and learning, each written by authors of the results.
- Tim Roughgarden, *Twenty Lectures on Algorithmic Game Theory*, Cambridge University Press, 2016. Short lectures on no-regret dynamics, the hierarchy of equilibria and PPAD, for a first course.
- Yoav Shoham and Kevin Leyton-Brown, *Multiagent Systems: Algorithmic, Game-Theoretic, and Logical Foundations*, Cambridge University Press, 2009. Covers support enumeration, Lemke-Howson, the sequence form and the CE LP with worked examples.
- Nicolò Cesa-Bianchi and Gábor Lugosi, *Prediction, Learning, and Games*, Cambridge University Press, 2006. The reference for regret, approachability and their use in games.
- Richard W. Cottle, Jong-Shi Pang and Richard E. Stone, *The Linear Complementarity Problem*, Academic Press, 1992. The standard account of LCPs, Lemke's method and the matrix classes it handles.
