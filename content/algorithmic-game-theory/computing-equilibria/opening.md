A poker program needs a strategy that no opponent can exploit. A security agency must decide how to spread a few patrols over many targets, knowing that an attacker will watch. An economist who builds a model of a market wants to know what the traders will do in it. In each case the solution concept of Chapter 42.1 is known, and the question is how to find a point that satisfies it. Nash's theorem guarantees that an equilibrium exists, but its proof uses a fixed-point theorem and gives no method. This chapter is about the methods, and about the evidence that for general games no fast method exists.

The answers fall into a clear pattern. Two-player zero-sum games, correlated equilibria and the leader's best commitment are linear programs, so they take polynomial time. A Nash equilibrium of a general two-player game is found by path-following algorithms, which work well in practice but take exponential time on some games, and the problem is complete for the class PPAD. Approximate equilibria sit in between. Learning dynamics give a different route: simple rules of repeated play whose averages approach the easier equilibrium sets.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in six groups, read in order.

1. **The problem and first methods.** [Nash equilibrium computation](/nash-equilibrium-computation/) states the problem and its variants. The [indifference principle](/indifference-principle/) turns supports into linear equations, [support enumeration](/support-enumeration/) tries all supports, and [best-response dynamics](/best-response-dynamics/) searches for a pure equilibrium.
2. **Equilibria that linear programs find.** [Zero-sum games by linear programming](/zero-sum-game-linear-programming/), the [linear program for correlated equilibria](/correlated-equilibrium-linear-program/), the [Stackelberg commitment algorithm](/stackelberg-commitment-algorithm/) and its use in [security games](/security-game/), the [sequence form](/sequence-form/) and its [linear program](/sequence-form-linear-program/) for game trees, and the [ellipsoid against hope](/ellipsoid-against-hope/) for succinct games.
3. **Complementarity and path following.** The [linear complementarity problem](/linear-complementarity-problem/), the [best-response polytopes](/best-response-polytope/), the [Lemke-Howson algorithm](/lemke-howson-algorithm/), [Lemke's algorithm](/lemke-algorithm/) for general LCPs, and the [exponential paths](/savani-von-stengel-theorem/) of Savani and von Stengel.
4. **Fixed points, PPAD and NP-hardness.** [Sperner's lemma](/sperner-lemma/), the [END OF THE LINE](/end-of-the-line-problem/) problem, [Scarf's algorithm](/scarf-algorithm/) for approximate fixed points, the [PPAD-completeness of Nash equilibria](/ppad-completeness-of-nash/), and the [NP-hardness of optimal equilibria](/hardness-of-optimal-nash-equilibria/).
5. **Approximate equilibria.** The [DMP algorithm](/dmp-half-approximate-nash/) for a $1/2$-approximate equilibrium, the quasi-polynomial [Lipton-Markakis-Mehta algorithm](/lipton-markakis-mehta-algorithm/), and [Rubinstein's lower bound](/rubinstein-approximate-nash-lower-bound/), which shows that it is nearly optimal under a hardness hypothesis.
6. **Learning dynamics.** [No-regret dynamics](/no-regret-dynamics/) and the theorems that their averages are [coarse correlated equilibria](/no-regret-coarse-correlated-theorem/) and, in [zero-sum games](/no-regret-zero-sum-theorem/), optimal strategies; [regret matching](/regret-matching/) and the [Blum-Mansour reduction](/blum-mansour-reduction/) for correlated equilibria; [Blackwell approachability](/blackwell-approachability/); [Robinson's theorem](/robinson-fictitious-play-theorem/) on fictitious play; and [optimistic Hedge](/optimistic-hedge/).

The results line up with the nesting of Chapter 42.1. Coarse correlated and correlated equilibria are found exactly by linear programs, in time polynomial in the payoff table, and approximately by learning. So are the Nash equilibria of zero-sum games. Nash equilibria of general games are PPAD-complete.
