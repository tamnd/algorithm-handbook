## History

Auctions are old: the Romans used them, and the London auction houses of the eighteenth century ran English auctions much like those of today. Their theory begins with William Vickrey's paper *Counterspeculation, auctions, and competitive sealed tenders* (1961). Vickrey proposed the second-price sealed-bid auction, showed that bidding one's value is a dominant strategy in it, solved the first-price auction with uniform values, and observed that in his examples the standard formats earn the same expected revenue. He shared the Nobel prize in economics in 1996.

The general theory came about twenty years later. Roger Myerson's *Optimal auction design* (1981) and, independently, John Riley and William Samuelson (1981) proved revenue equivalence in general and found the auctions that maximize expected revenue. Myerson's paper introduced the function now called the virtual valuation, and ironing for irregular distributions; Jeremy Bulow and John Roberts (1989) read them as marginal revenues, which is the form used in the entries. Myerson and Mark Satterthwaite (1983) showed that efficient bilateral trade is impossible under the natural constraints, and Preston McAfee (1992) gave a truthful double auction that loses at most one trade. Jeremy Bulow and Paul Klemperer (1996) showed that one extra bidder is worth more than the optimal choice of mechanism.

Common values have their own line. Robert Wilson studied auctions in which all bidders value the item the same but see different signals, in 1969 and 1977. Edward Capen, Robert Clapp and William Campbell (1971) used the winner's curse to explain the low returns of oil companies on offshore leases. Paul Milgrom and Robert Weber, in *A theory of auctions and competitive bidding* (1982), introduced affiliated values and the linkage principle, which puts the expected revenue of the English auction at or above that of the sealed-bid formats. Milgrom and Wilson, with Preston McAfee, then designed the simultaneous ascending auction that the US Federal Communications Commission first used for spectrum in 1994, and Milgrom and Wilson received the Nobel prize in 2020.

Computer science entered with combinatorial auctions. Michael Rothkopf, Aleksandar Pekeč and Ronald Harstad (1998) studied which bundle structures make winner determination tractable, Noam Nisan (2000) compared bidding languages, and Daniel Lehmann, Liadan O'Callaghan and Yoav Shoham (1999, journal version 2002) gave the greedy truthful mechanism for single-minded bidders. Search advertising raised new questions: Google adopted the generalized second-price auction in 2002, and Benjamin Edelman, Michael Ostrovsky and Michael Schwarz (2007) and Hal Varian (2007) found its envy-free equilibria.

Since then the algorithmic work has asked how well simple mechanisms do. Shuchi Chawla, Jason Hartline, David Malec and Balasubramanian Sivan (2010) analyzed sequential posted pricing. Sergiu Hart and Noam Nisan, and later Moshe Babaioff, Nicole Immorlica, Brendan Lucier and Matthew Weinberg, compared selling items separately and as a bundle with the optimum. For first-price auctions, Vasilis Syrgkanis and Éva Tardos (2013) proved that every Bayes-Nash equilibrium reaches $1 - 1/e$ of the optimal welfare, and Yaonan Jin and Pinyan Lu (2022) found the exact bound $1 - 1/e^2$ for independent values.

## Further reading

- Vijay Krishna, *Auction Theory*, 2nd edition (2010). The standard graduate text on single-item auctions, revenue equivalence, common values and linkage, with full proofs.
- Paul Milgrom, *Putting Auction Theory to Work* (2004). The theory as used in the design of spectrum auctions, by one of their designers.
- Tim Roughgarden, *Twenty Lectures on Algorithmic Game Theory* (2016). Short lectures on Myerson's lemma, revenue maximization, Bulow-Klemperer, combinatorial auctions and the price of anarchy of auctions.
- Peter Cramton, Yoav Shoham and Richard Steinberg (eds.), *Combinatorial Auctions* (2006). Surveys of package bidding, bidding languages, winner determination and practice.
- Noam Nisan, Tim Roughgarden, Éva Tardos and Vijay V. Vazirani (eds.), *Algorithmic Game Theory* (2007). Chapters on mechanism design, combinatorial auctions and sponsored search written for computer scientists.
