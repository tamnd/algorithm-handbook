Three bidders want one item, and each value is drawn independently and uniformly from $[0, 1]$. In a [Vickrey auction](/vickrey-auction/) each bids its value and the winner pays the second-highest value, so the seller expects $1/2$. In a [first-price auction](/first-price-auction/) each bids two thirds of its value in equilibrium and the winner pays its own bid, and the seller again expects $1/2$. The [revenue equivalence theorem](/revenue-equivalence-theorem/) says that this is no coincidence. A [reserve price](/reserve-price/) of $1/2$ raises the expected revenue to $17/32$, and [Myerson's optimal auction](/myerson-optimal-auction/) shows that no auction in which the bidders report truthfully and never lose by taking part does better with these three bidders. Yet a Vickrey auction with a fourth bidder and no reserve expects $3/5$, more than the best auction with three: this is the [Bulow-Klemperer theorem](/bulow-klemperer-theorem/).

Auctions are where the mechanism design of Chapter 42.4 meets practice. Governments have sold radio spectrum by the [simultaneous ascending auction](/simultaneous-ascending-auction/) since 1994, exchanges match buyers and sellers in [double auctions](/double-auction/), and search engines run an auction for the advertisements beside the results of each query. The theory of the chapter answers three kinds of question. How should a bidder bid, and what does an auction earn in equilibrium? Which auction earns the most, or gives the items to those who value them most? And, when there are many items and bidders want bundles, can the winners even be computed? The last question is where algorithms come in: choosing the winners of a [combinatorial auction](/combinatorial-auction/) is NP-hard, and the truthful mechanisms that run in polynomial time are approximations.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups.

1. **Selling one item.** The [Vickrey](/vickrey-auction/), [English](/english-auction/), first-price and [Dutch](/dutch-auction/) auctions, and the [independent private values](/independent-private-values/) model in which bidders know the distributions but not the values of the others.
2. **Equilibrium bidding and revenue equivalence.** The [equilibrium of the symmetric first-price auction](/first-price-auction-equilibrium/), the revenue equivalence theorem, the [all-pay auction](/all-pay-auction/), and the [price of anarchy of first-price auctions](/first-price-auction-price-of-anarchy/) when bidders are not symmetric.
3. **Auctions that maximize revenue.** [Virtual valuations](/virtual-valuation/), [regular distributions](/regular-distribution/), reserve prices, Myerson's optimal auction, [ironing](/ironing/) and the Bulow-Klemperer theorem.
4. **Simple, approximate and learned mechanisms.** [Sequential posted pricing](/sequential-posted-pricing/), [selling separately or as a bundle](/simple-versus-optimal-multi-item/), and the [sample complexity of revenue maximization](/sample-complexity-of-revenue-maximization/).
5. **Interdependent values.** [Common value auctions](/common-value-auction/), the winner's curse and the [linkage principle](/linkage-principle/).
6. **Many units and two-sided markets.** The [uniform price auction](/uniform-price-auction/), double auctions and [McAfee's trade reduction mechanism](/mcafee-trade-reduction/).
7. **Combinatorial auctions.** The [winner determination problem](/winner-determination-problem/), [bidding languages](/bidding-languages/), [demand queries](/demand-query/), the [Lehmann-O'Callaghan-Shoham mechanism](/single-minded-bidder-mechanism/) for single-minded bidders and the simultaneous ascending auction.
8. **Advertising auctions.** The [position auction](/position-auction/), the [generalized second-price auction](/generalized-second-price-auction/) and the [envy-free equilibrium](/gsp-envy-free-equilibrium/) that gives the VCG outcome, and [pacing equilibria](/pacing-equilibrium/) of budget-limited bidders.
