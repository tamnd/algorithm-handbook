Two buyers each have one dollar to spend on two goods, one unit of each. Buyer 1 values the goods at 2 and 1, buyer 2 at 1 and 2. At prices of one dollar each, buyer 1 spends her dollar on good 1, buyer 2 spends hers on good 2, and both goods are sold. Nobody wants to change anything, so these prices are an equilibrium. Raise the price of good 1 to two dollars and the market no longer clears: two dollars of budget cannot pay for goods that now cost three. Prices that clear a market are a kind of solution, and finding them is a computational problem.

Many markets cannot use prices at all. Doctors are matched to hospitals, pupils to schools, and kidney patients to donors, and in each case money may not change hands. There the solution is a matching that respects the rankings: one that no doctor and hospital would both rather leave, or that no group of patients could improve by trading among themselves. The two kinds of market turn out to be close. A housing market without money has an allocation that some prices support, an ascending auction for unit-demand bidders ends at the same prices as the [VCG mechanism](/vcg-mechanism/), and a market with equal budgets of fake money divides goods fairly.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups, read in order.

1. **Two-sided matching.** The structure of stable matchings beyond the algorithm: the [lattice of stable matchings](/lattice-of-stable-matchings/), the [rural hospitals theorem](/rural-hospitals-theorem/), the [strategyproofness of deferred acceptance](/deferred-acceptance-strategyproofness/) for the proposing side and [Roth's impossibility theorem](/roth-impossibility-theorem/) for both sides, the [egalitarian stable matching](/egalitarian-stable-matching/), and [stable matching with couples](/matching-with-couples/), where a stable matching may not exist.
2. **School choice.** The [school choice problem](/school-choice-problem/) and the [Boston mechanism](/boston-mechanism/), which Boston used until 2005, when it changed to deferred acceptance.
3. **Allocation without money.** The [house allocation problem](/house-allocation-problem/), [serial dictatorship](/serial-dictatorship/), [random serial dictatorship](/random-serial-dictatorship/), [probabilistic serial](/probabilistic-serial/) and [popular matchings](/popular-matching/).
4. **Prices and equilibrium.** The [competitive equilibrium](/competitive-equilibrium/), the [first welfare theorem](/first-welfare-theorem/), the [Arrow-Debreu market](/arrow-debreu-market/) with its [existence theorem](/arrow-debreu-existence-theorem/), and the [Fisher market](/fisher-market/).
5. **Housing markets and kidney exchange.** The [Shapley-Scarf housing market](/shapley-scarf-housing-market/), [top trading cycles](/top-trading-cycles/) and the [Roth-Postlewaite theorem](/roth-postlewaite-theorem/), then the [kidney exchange problem](/kidney-exchange-problem/), its [cycle formulation](/kidney-exchange-cycle-formulation/) and [top trading cycles and chains](/top-trading-cycles-and-chains/).
6. **Computing market equilibria.** The [Eisenberg-Gale program](/eisenberg-gale-program/), the [DPSV algorithm](/dpsv-algorithm/), [Orlin's strongly polynomial algorithm](/orlin-fisher-market-algorithm/), [proportional response dynamics](/proportional-response-dynamics/), [tatonnement](/tatonnement/), and the [PPAD-hardness of market equilibria](/ppad-hardness-of-arrow-debreu/).
7. **Indivisible goods with prices.** [Gross substitutes](/gross-substitutes/), the [Demange-Gale-Sotomayor auction](/demange-gale-sotomayor-auction/) and the [Kelso-Crawford process](/kelso-crawford-process/).
8. **Markets for fair division.** [Competitive equilibrium from equal incomes](/competitive-equilibrium-from-equal-incomes/), the [Hylland-Zeckhauser mechanism](/hylland-zeckhauser-mechanism/) and [approximate CEEI](/approximate-ceei/) for course allocation.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these fourteen entries in order: the lattice of stable matchings, the strategyproofness of deferred acceptance, Roth's impossibility theorem, the school choice problem, the house allocation problem, serial dictatorship, probabilistic serial, competitive equilibrium, the first welfare theorem, the Fisher market, the Shapley-Scarf housing market, top trading cycles, the Eisenberg-Gale program and the Demange-Gale-Sotomayor auction. They give the main objects of both halves and the one convex program that ties prices to welfare.

Leave the research entries for later: Orlin's algorithm, the PPAD-hardness proof and approximate CEEI. The DPSV algorithm is worth reading after the Eisenberg-Gale program, but its analysis is long. The Arrow-Debreu existence theorem uses a fixed-point argument that is easier after the [Brouwer fixed-point theorem](/brouwer-fixed-point-theorem/) of chapter 42.1.
