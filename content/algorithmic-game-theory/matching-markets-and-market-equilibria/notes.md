## History

The market side is older. Irving Fisher built a hydraulic machine in his 1891 thesis that found the equilibrium prices of a market of buyers with budgets. Kenneth Arrow and Gerard Debreu gave the modern proofs of the welfare theorems in 1951, and in 1954 Arrow and Debreu, and Lionel McKenzie in the same year, proved that equilibria exist under convexity, with fixed-point theorems. Edmund Eisenberg and David Gale found the convex program for the Fisher market in 1959, in a paper on the pari-mutuel method of betting. Herbert Scarf gave an economy in 1960 in which tatonnement cycles forever, and in 1967 the first general algorithm for computing an equilibrium.

David Gale and Lloyd Shapley introduced stable matching in 1962. The lattice of stable matchings, with a proof of John Conway, appeared in Donald Knuth's lectures of 1976. Lester Dubins and David Freedman (1981) and Alvin Roth (1982) proved that deferred acceptance is safe for the proposers, and Roth proved that no stable mechanism is safe for both sides. Roth (1984) pointed out that couples in the American residency match can make stable matchings fail to exist, and the design of Roth and Elliott Peranson, adopted in 1998, handles couples explicitly.

Matching without money also starts with Shapley. Shapley and Scarf (1974) defined the housing market and proved with Gale's top trading cycles that its core is not empty, and Roth and Andrew Postlewaite (1977) showed that the strict core has a single allocation. Aanund Hylland and Richard Zeckhauser (1979) proposed a market with fake money for assigning positions. Atila Abdulkadiroglu and Tayfun Sonmez (1998) showed that random serial dictatorship equals the core from random endowments, and Anna Bogomolnaia and Herve Moulin (2001) introduced probabilistic serial. On the side of prices, Alexander Kelso and Vincent Crawford (1982) defined gross substitutes, and Gabrielle Demange, Gale and Marilda Sotomayor (1986) gave the exact ascending auction for unit-demand bidders.

Practice followed theory in the 2000s. Abdulkadiroglu and Sonmez (2003) framed school choice as mechanism design, and New York and Boston changed to deferred acceptance. Roth, Sonmez and Utku Unver designed kidney exchange in 2004 and 2005, and David Abraham, Avrim Blum and Tuomas Sandholm (2007) solved the cycle formulation for pools with thousands of pairs by branch and price. Eric Budish (2011) gave approximate CEEI for course allocation.

Computer science took up equilibrium computation around 2002, when Nikhil Devanur, Christos Papadimitriou, Amin Saberi and Vijay Vazirani gave a combinatorial polynomial algorithm for linear Fisher markets. James Orlin made it strongly polynomial in 2010, and Benjamin Birnbaum, Devanur and Lin Xiao (2011) analyzed proportional response as mirror descent. On the hardness side, Xi Chen, Decheng Dai, Ye Du and Shang-Hua Teng (2009) proved that finding an equilibrium of an exchange market with separable piecewise-linear concave utilities is PPAD-complete.

## Further reading

- Alvin E. Roth and Marilda A. Oliveira Sotomayor, *Two-Sided Matching: A Study in Game-Theoretic Modeling and Analysis*, Cambridge University Press, 1990. The standard account of stable matching theory, its incentives and the residency match.
- Dan Gusfield and Robert W. Irving, *The Stable Marriage Problem: Structure and Algorithms*, MIT Press, 1989. Rotations, the lattice and the algorithms for optimal stable matchings.
- David F. Manlove, *Algorithmics of Matching Under Preferences*, World Scientific, 2013. A survey of the algorithms and complexity results for two-sided and one-sided matching, including house allocation and popular matchings.
- Noam Nisan, Tim Roughgarden, Éva Tardos and Vijay V. Vazirani (eds.), *Algorithmic Game Theory*, Cambridge University Press, 2007. Its chapters on market equilibria cover Fisher markets, the Eisenberg-Gale program and the combinatorial algorithms.
- Andreu Mas-Colell, Michael D. Whinston and Jerry R. Green, *Microeconomic Theory*, Oxford University Press, 1995. The economic theory of competitive equilibrium, its existence and the welfare theorems.
