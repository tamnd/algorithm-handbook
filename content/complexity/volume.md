A dispatcher must split a day's parcels between two vans so that both carry exactly the same weight: the [partition problem](/partition-problem/). A dynamic program over the loads one van can reach takes time proportional to the number of parcels times the total weight. That is fast for weights in kilograms and hopeless for 60-bit numbers, because the time grows with the values, not with their length. No algorithm polynomial in the length of the input is known, and the problem is [NP-complete](/np-complete/): such an algorithm would give one for every problem in NP and prove P = NP, which is open. Other questions from the same office belong to other parts of the theory: whether routes can be fixed fast when only $k$ stops may change, whether two logs of $n$ lines can be compared by edit distance in much less than $n^2$ steps, how many bits two depots must exchange to check that their copies of a file agree, and whether one tool can decide, for every program, whether it halts. The last answer is no, and that is a theorem, not a conjecture.

This volume is about what problems need, not about what one algorithm achieves: classes of problems, the reductions that compare them, and the lower bounds, proved or conditional, that say no algorithm can do better. It has seven chapters. In this edition one of them is written: Chapter 39.2, NP completeness, with 20 entries. The other six are not written yet; each has an opening page that says what it will cover and what to read meanwhile.

## Why complexity gets a volume of its own

A few ideas run through the whole volume:

- **A statement about every algorithm.** A hardness result or a lower bound speaks of all algorithms for a problem in a fixed model, usually the [multitape Turing machine](/multitape-turing-machine/) of Volume 1, and the problems are [decision problems](/decision-problem/): sets of bit strings under a fixed, reasonable encoding.
- **Reductions compare problems.** A Karp reduction maps the instances of one problem to instances of another in polynomial time and keeps the answer. An algorithm for the second problem then gives one for the first, and hardness passes the other way. Every NP-completeness proof is a chain of such maps that starts at [SAT](/sat/) with the [Cook-Levin theorem](/cook-levin-theorem/). Parameterized and fine-grained reductions also keep a parameter or an exponent.
- **Most separations are conditional.** An NP-hard problem has no polynomial-time algorithm only if P ≠ NP, which is open. Fine-grained lower bounds rest on hypotheses such as the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis, and parameterized ones on FPT ≠ W[1]. Unconditional bounds hold in restricted models, such as the [comparison sorting lower bound](/comparison-sort-lower-bound/); undecidability needs no hypothesis.
- **The encoding of numbers matters.** Subset sum is NP-complete with its numbers in binary and solvable in polynomial time with them in [unary](/unary-encoding/). [Pseudo-polynomial time](/pseudo-polynomial-time/) and [strong NP-hardness](/strong-np-hardness/) make this precise: a strongly NP-hard problem has no pseudo-polynomial algorithm unless P = NP.
- **The worst case is not the typical case.** NP-hardness speaks of the hardest instances of each size. [CDCL](/cdcl/) solvers decide many industrial formulas with millions of clauses; that is a measurement on the instances tried, not a guarantee.

## The chapters

**Chapter 39.1, Complexity classes**, is not written yet. It will cover the classes defined by resources: P, NP and coNP, space classes from L to PSPACE, exponential time, randomized classes such as BPP, circuit classes, the polynomial hierarchy, and interactive and probabilistically checkable proofs. It will give the theorems that relate them, such as the hierarchy theorems, Savitch's theorem and IP = PSPACE, and the barriers that known proof methods meet. Meanwhile, the machine models are in Volume 1: the [nondeterministic](/nondeterministic-turing-machine/), [probabilistic](/probabilistic-turing-machine/) and [alternating](/alternating-turing-machine/) Turing machines and the [Boolean circuit](/boolean-circuit/). Complete problems for PSPACE are written in other volumes: [QBF](/qbf/) in Volume 16, [regular expression equivalence](/regex-equivalence-pspace-completeness/) in Volume 9 and [STRIPS plan existence](/strips-plan-existence-pspace-complete/) in Volume 29.

**Chapter 39.2, NP completeness**, is written. It starts from the [decision problem](/decision-problem/), the [certificate](/certificate/) and the [polynomial-time verifier](/polynomial-time-verifier/) that define NP, and defines [NP-hard](/np-hard/), NP-complete and [coNP-complete](/co-np-complete/) problems. The Cook-Levin theorem makes SAT NP-complete, and [Karp's 21 problems](/karps-21-np-complete-problems/) and the [Garey-Johnson compendium](/garey-johnson-compendium/) grow the list. [Three-dimensional matching](/three-dimensional-matching/), [exact cover by 3-sets](/exact-cover-by-3-sets/), [subset sum](/subset-sum-problem/) and partition start many later proofs. Unary encoding, pseudo-polynomial time, [weak](/weak-np-hardness/) and strong NP-hardness set apart the problems that are hard only when their numbers are large, with [scheduling with release times and deadlines](/scheduling-with-release-times-and-deadlines/) as a strongly NP-complete example. The chapter ends with two structural theorems: [Ladner's theorem](/ladners-theorem/) gives problems in NP that are neither in P nor NP-complete, if P ≠ NP, and [Mahaney's theorem](/mahaneys-theorem/) shows that a sparse set is NP-hard only if P = NP.

**Chapter 39.3, Reductions catalogue**, is not written yet. It will cover the kinds of reduction (Karp, Cook, Levin and parsimonious), search-to-decision reductions and self-reducibility, gadgets, and a catalogue of standard proofs, from circuit satisfiability and 3-coloring to the Hamiltonian cycle and 3-partition. Meanwhile, the proof page of Karp's 21 problems gives five reductions in full. The general [reduction](/reduction/) and [polynomial-time equivalence](/polynomial-time-equivalence/) are in Volume 2, and reductions that keep approximation ratios, such as the [gap-preserving reduction](/gap-preserving-reduction/) and the [L-reduction](/l-reduction/), are in Volume 18.

**Chapter 39.4, Parameterized complexity**, is not written yet. It measures time in the input size $n$ and a parameter $k$, such as the solution size, and asks which problems take time $f(k) \cdot n^{O(1)}$. It will cover kernels and their lower bounds, branching, color coding, Courcelle's theorem and the W-hierarchy, in which $k$-clique is W[1]-complete. Meanwhile, read [treewidth](/treewidth/), [tree decompositions](/tree-decomposition/), [treewidth computation](/treewidth-computation/), which is fixed-parameter tractable in $k$, and [dynamic programming on tree decompositions](/dp-on-tree-decompositions/) in Volume 7. The [longest path problem](/longest-path-problem/) and the [feedback vertex set problem](/feedback-vertex-set-problem/) in Volume 7 give fixed-parameter algorithms, and [parameterized inapproximability](/parameterized-inapproximability/) in Volume 18 gives hardness under W[1] ≠ FPT and Gap-ETH.

**Chapter 39.5, Fine grained complexity**, is not written yet. It asks whether the exponents of polynomial algorithms can be lowered, for example whether edit distance has an $O(n^{2-\varepsilon})$ algorithm. It will cover the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH), the orthogonal vectors hypothesis, the 3SUM and APSP conjectures, fine-grained reductions, and dynamic lower bounds. Meanwhile, [edit distance hardness](/edit-distance-hardness/) in Volume 8 and the [SETH lower bound for LCS](/backurs-indyk-lcs-lower-bound/) in Volume 6 rule out strongly subquadratic algorithms unless SETH fails. The quadratic [3SUM scan](/three-sum-two-pointer-scan/) is in Volume 2, and [collinear points](/collinear-points/) in Volume 13 is the founding example of 3SUM-hardness. [Min-plus matrix multiplication](/min-plus-matrix-multiplication/) in Volume 11 is equivalent to all-pairs shortest paths, and no $n^{3-\varepsilon}$ algorithm for it is known.

**Chapter 39.6, Communication and query complexity**, is not written yet. Its models count the bits that players exchange when each holds part of the input, or the input bits an algorithm reads, and in them lower bounds need no hypotheses. It will cover deterministic and randomized protocols, the fooling set, rank and discrepancy methods, equality and set disjointness, the log-rank conjecture, decision tree complexity, sensitivity, approximate degree and quantum queries. Meanwhile, the [decision tree model](/decision-tree-model/) and [Yao's minimax principle](/yao-minimax-principle/) are in Volume 1. [String equality testing](/string-equality-testing/) in Volume 8 shows the gap between deterministic and randomized protocols. The [index problem lower bound](/index-problem-lower-bound/) in Volume 19 turns one-way communication bounds into streaming space bounds, and [Grover optimality](/grover-optimality/) in Volume 30 is a quantum query lower bound.

**Chapter 39.7, Computability**, is not written yet. It asks which problems can be solved at all. It will cover the Church-Turing thesis, recursive functions, the universal Turing machine, the halting problem, Rice's theorem, undecidable problems such as Post's correspondence problem and Hilbert's tenth problem, Gödel's incompleteness theorems, Turing degrees and Kolmogorov complexity. Meanwhile, the [lambda calculus](/lambda-calculus/) and the [register machine](/register-machine/), equivalent in power to Turing machines, are in Volume 1. Volume 9 proves [ambiguity](/cfg-ambiguity-undecidable/) and [equivalence](/cfg-equivalence-undecidable/) of context-free grammars undecidable, and [higher-order unification](/higher-order-unification-undecidability/) in Volume 16 is undecidable by a reduction from Post's correspondence problem.

## How to read this volume

There is no course through the volume yet. Read Chapter 39.2 in the order its opening page gives, and do the exercises marked ◦ at its end. For each other chapter, its opening page lists written entries elsewhere that cover part of its ground.

Each entry stands on its own; its facts box lists what it needs, by entry number.

## What you need first

From Volume 1 you need the [Turing machine](/turing-machine/) and its multitape form, [input size](/input-size/), [polynomial time](/polynomial-time/) and [asymptotic notation](/asymptotic-notation/). Chapter 39.2 uses [3-SAT](/3-sat/) and [conjunctive normal form](/conjunctive-normal-form/) from Volume 16.

## The exercises

Chapter 39.2 ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
