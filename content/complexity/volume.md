A dispatcher must split a day's parcels between two vans so that both carry exactly the same weight: the [partition problem](/partition-problem/). A dynamic program over the loads one van can reach takes time proportional to the number of parcels times the total weight. That is fast for weights in kilograms and hopeless for 60-bit numbers, because the time grows with the values, not with their length. No algorithm polynomial in the length of the input is known, and the problem is [NP-complete](/np-complete/): such an algorithm would give one for every problem in NP and prove P = NP, which is open. Other questions from the same office belong to other parts of the theory: whether routes can be fixed fast when only $k$ stops may change, whether two logs of $n$ lines can be compared by edit distance in much less than $n^2$ steps, how many bits two depots must exchange to check that their copies of a file agree, and whether one tool can decide, for every program, whether it halts. The last answer is no, and that is a theorem, not a conjecture.

This volume is about what problems need, not about what one algorithm achieves: classes of problems, the reductions that compare them, and the lower bounds, proved or conditional, that say no algorithm can do better. It has seven chapters, and all of them are written.

## Why complexity gets a volume of its own

A few ideas run through the whole volume:

- **A statement about every algorithm.** A hardness result or a lower bound speaks of all algorithms for a problem in a fixed model, usually the [multitape Turing machine](/multitape-turing-machine/) of Volume 1, and the problems are [decision problems](/decision-problem/): sets of bit strings under a fixed, reasonable encoding.
- **Reductions compare problems.** A [polynomial-time reduction](/polynomial-time-reduction/) maps the instances of one problem to instances of another and keeps the answer. An algorithm for the second problem then gives one for the first, and hardness passes the other way. Parameterized and fine-grained reductions also keep a parameter or an exponent, and logspace reductions work inside P.
- **Most separations are conditional.** An NP-hard problem has no polynomial-time algorithm only if P ≠ NP, which is open, and so are P versus PSPACE, L versus P and BPP versus P. Fine-grained lower bounds rest on hypotheses such as the [Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis](/strong-exponential-time-hypothesis/), and parameterized ones on FPT ≠ W[1]. The proved separations come from the hierarchy theorems, from restricted models such as constant-depth circuits, communication protocols and decision trees, and from computability.
- **The encoding of numbers matters.** Subset sum is NP-complete with its numbers in binary and solvable in polynomial time with them in [unary](/unary-encoding/). [Strong NP-hardness](/strong-np-hardness/) marks the problems that stay hard with small numbers.
- **The worst case is not the typical case.** NP-hardness speaks of the hardest instances of each size. [CDCL](/cdcl/) solvers decide many industrial formulas with millions of clauses; that is a measurement on the instances tried, not a guarantee.

## The chapters

**Chapter 39.1, Complexity classes**, defines the classes by model, resource and bound: [P](/p/), [NP](/np/) and [coNP](/co-np/), space classes from [L](/logspace/) to [PSPACE](/pspace/), exponential time, the randomized classes [BPP](/bpp/), RP and ZPP, circuit classes such as [AC0](/ac0/) and [P/poly](/p-poly/), the [polynomial hierarchy](/polynomial-hierarchy/), search classes such as [PPAD](/ppad/), and interactive and probabilistically checkable proofs. It proves the relations that are known, among them the [time hierarchy theorem](/time-hierarchy-theorem/), [Savitch's theorem](/savitch-theorem/) and [IP = PSPACE](/ip-equals-pspace/), and ends with the barriers, such as [relativization](/relativization/), that known proof methods meet.

**Chapter 39.2, NP completeness**, starts from the [certificate](/certificate/) and the [polynomial-time verifier](/polynomial-time-verifier/) that define NP. The [Cook-Levin theorem](/cook-levin-theorem/) makes SAT NP-complete, and [Karp's 21 problems](/karps-21-np-complete-problems/) and the [Garey-Johnson compendium](/garey-johnson-compendium/) grow the list. [Pseudo-polynomial time](/pseudo-polynomial-time/), weak and strong NP-hardness set apart the problems that are hard only when their numbers are large. [Ladner's theorem](/ladners-theorem/) and [Mahaney's theorem](/mahaneys-theorem/) describe the structure of NP if P ≠ NP.

**Chapter 39.3, Reductions catalogue**, treats reductions as objects of their own: Karp and [Turing reductions](/turing-reduction/), [transitivity](/transitivity-of-reductions/), the parts of an [NP-completeness proof](/np-completeness-proof/) and the [gadgets](/gadget-reduction/) that build it. It gives the standard reductions from [circuit satisfiability](/circuit-sat/) to [3-coloring](/three-coloring/), [Hamiltonian cycle](/hamiltonian-cycle-np-completeness/), [knapsack](/knapsack-decision/) and [3-partition](/three-partition/), and ends with search problems, [self-reducibility](/self-reducibility/) and the [parsimonious reductions](/parsimonious-reduction/) used for counting.

**Chapter 39.4, Parameterized complexity**, measures time in the input size $n$ and a [parameter](/parameter/) $k$, such as the solution size, and asks which problems are [fixed-parameter tractable](/fixed-parameter-tractable/), with time $f(k) \cdot n^{O(1)}$. Vertex cover, independent set and clique are the running examples. The methods are [bounded search trees](/bounded-search-tree/), [kernelization](/kernelization/), [color coding](/color-coding/), [iterative compression](/iterative-compression/), and dynamic programming over graphs of bounded [treewidth](/treewidth/), where [Courcelle's theorem](/courcelle-theorem/) decides every property of monadic second-order logic in linear time. On the hardness side come the [W-hierarchy](/w-hierarchy/), in which $k$-clique is [W[1]-complete](/clique-w1-completeness/), and [kernel lower bounds](/kernel-lower-bounds/), which hold unless $\mathsf{NP} \subseteq \mathsf{coNP/poly}$.

**Chapter 39.5, Fine-grained complexity**, asks whether the exponents of polynomial algorithms can be lowered, for example whether edit distance has an $O(n^{2-\varepsilon})$ algorithm. It states the hypotheses, SETH, the [orthogonal vectors hypothesis](/orthogonal-vectors-hypothesis/), the [3SUM conjecture](/3sum-conjecture/) and the [APSP conjecture](/apsp-conjecture/), and connects problems by [fine-grained reductions](/fine-grained-reduction/). Every lower bound in the chapter is conditional on one of them, as in the [SETH lower bound for edit distance](/edit-distance-seth-lower-bound/). The hypotheses are not equally safe: the entries report preprints of October 2026, not yet refereed, that claim a truly subquadratic algorithm for 3SUM and a truly subcubic one for APSP. If those are correct, the two conjectures are false; the reductions built on them stay theorems but no longer give lower bounds, and the entries say case by case what changes. The chapter also covers [dynamic lower bounds](/dynamic-lower-bounds/) and the [algorithmic method](/algorithmic-method/), which turns faster algorithms into circuit lower bounds.

**Chapter 39.6, Communication and query complexity**, counts the bits that players exchange when each holds part of the input, or the input bits an algorithm reads. In these models lower bounds are proved without hypotheses. It covers the [communication model](/communication-model/), [equality](/equality-problem/) and [set disjointness](/disjointness-problem/), the fooling set, rank and discrepancy methods, the open [log-rank conjecture](/log-rank-conjecture/), [decision tree complexity](/decision-tree-complexity/), the [sensitivity theorem](/sensitivity-theorem/), the polynomial method and [quantum query complexity](/quantum-query-complexity/).

**Chapter 39.7, Computability**, asks which problems can be solved at all. It covers the [Church-Turing thesis](/church-turing-thesis/), which is a thesis and not a theorem, the [universal Turing machine](/universal-turing-machine/), the [halting problem](/halting-problem/), [Rice's theorem](/rice-theorem/), undecidable problems such as [Post's correspondence problem](/post-correspondence-problem/) and [Hilbert's tenth problem](/hilberts-tenth-problem/), [Gödel's incompleteness theorems](/godel-incompleteness-theorem/), [Turing degrees](/turing-degrees/) and [Kolmogorov complexity](/kolmogorov-complexity/). Its results need no unproved hypothesis.

## How to read this volume

Chapters 39.1 to 39.3 form the core and are best read in order, although 39.2 and 39.3 can be read before most of 39.1. Chapters 39.4 to 39.7 each lean on the core and not on one another, so take them in any order. Chapter 39.7 can be read first by anyone who has seen Turing machines; the oracle machines and diagonalization of its entries are used in the barrier entries of 39.1.

For a first course, take these eighteen entries in order: [decision problem](/decision-problem/), [complexity class](/complexity-class/), [P](/p/), [NP](/np/), [polynomial-time reduction](/polynomial-time-reduction/), [NP-complete](/np-complete/), [the Cook-Levin theorem](/cook-levin-theorem/), [NP-completeness proof](/np-completeness-proof/), [3-coloring](/three-coloring/), [PSPACE](/pspace/), [the time hierarchy theorem](/time-hierarchy-theorem/), [BPP](/bpp/), [fixed-parameter tractable](/fixed-parameter-tractable/), [kernelization](/kernelization/), [the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis](/strong-exponential-time-hypothesis/), [orthogonal vectors](/orthogonal-vectors/), [set disjointness](/disjointness-problem/) and [the halting problem](/halting-problem/). Each chapter's opening page gives a longer first reading.

Each entry stands on its own; its facts box lists what it needs, by entry number.

## What you need first

From Volume 1 you need the [Turing machine](/turing-machine/) and its multitape form, [input size](/input-size/), [polynomial time](/polynomial-time/), [asymptotic notation](/asymptotic-notation/), [Boolean circuits](/boolean-circuit/) and basic discrete probability, such as the [Chernoff bound](/chernoff-bound/). The reductions start from [SAT](/sat/), [3-SAT](/3-sat/) and [conjunctive normal form](/conjunctive-normal-form/) in Volume 16, and many examples are graph problems from Volume 7, such as [graph reachability](/graph-reachability/) and [treewidth](/treewidth/).

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
