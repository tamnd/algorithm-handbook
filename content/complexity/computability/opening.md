Take the program that, on input $n$, follows the map $n \mapsto n/2$ for even $n$ and $n \mapsto 3n + 1$ for odd $n$, and stops when it reaches $1$. Nobody knows whether it stops for every $n$. A general tool that reads any program and its input and says whether the program halts would settle this question and many others. Turing proved in 1936 that no such tool exists. The [halting problem](/halting-problem/) is undecidable, and the proof uses no hypothesis: it is a theorem about every [Turing machine](/turing-machine/).

Computability theory asks which problems an algorithm can solve at all, with no bound on time or memory. Its answers are unconditional theorems, unlike the open separations of the other chapters of this volume. They reach every programming language through the [Church-Turing thesis](/church-turing-thesis/), the claim that every mechanical method can be carried out by a Turing machine. The thesis is supported by every model ever proposed, but it is not a theorem, since one side of it is informal. Undecidability also spreads by reduction. By [Rice's theorem](/rice-theorem/), no algorithm decides any nontrivial property of the language a program accepts, so exact program analysis is impossible, and analysis tools must give up exactness, completeness or termination.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups. The first four build the theory up to Rice's theorem, and the last four apply it and refine it.

1. **Decidable and enumerable languages.** [Decidable](/decidable-language/) and [computably enumerable](/recursively-enumerable-language/) languages, the Church-Turing thesis and [computable functions](/computable-function/), which are partial by default.
2. **Counting and diagonalization.** [Countability](/countability/), [undecidability](/undecidability/) and the [diagonalization argument](/diagonalization-argument/).
3. **Programs as data.** [Gödel numbering](/godel-numbering/), [primitive recursive](/primitive-recursive-function/) and [partial recursive](/partial-recursive-function/) functions, the [universal Turing machine](/universal-turing-machine/) and [Turing completeness](/turing-completeness/).
4. **The halting problem and reductions.** The [acceptance problem](/acceptance-problem/), the halting problem, [uncomputable functions](/uncomputable-function/), [many-one reducibility](/many-one-reducibility/) and Rice's theorem.
5. **Undecidable problems in mathematics.** The [Post correspondence problem](/post-correspondence-problem/), the [Entscheidungsproblem](/entscheidungsproblem/) and [Church's theorem](/church-theorem/), [Hilbert's tenth problem](/hilberts-tenth-problem/), the [Wang tiling problem](/wang-tiling-problem/), the [word problem for groups](/word-problem-for-groups/) and [Gödel's incompleteness theorems](/godel-incompleteness-theorem/).
6. **Indices and self-reference.** The [s-m-n theorem](/s-m-n-theorem/) and [Kleene's recursion theorem](/kleene-recursion-theorem/).
7. **Growth, information and real numbers.** The [busy beaver function](/busy-beaver-function/), [computable real numbers](/computable-real-numbers/), [Kolmogorov complexity](/kolmogorov-complexity/) and [Chaitin's constant](/chaitins-constant/).
8. **Degrees of unsolvability.** [Oracle Turing machines](/oracle-turing-machine/), [Turing degrees](/turing-degrees/), the [Turing jump](/turing-jump/), the [arithmetical hierarchy](/arithmetical-hierarchy/), [Post's theorem](/posts-theorem/) and [Post's problem](/post-problem/).

Two kinds of reduction run through the chapter, and they should be kept apart. A many-one reduction maps each instance of one problem to a single instance of another with the same answer, and it preserves both decidability and computable enumerability. A Turing reduction may ask an oracle many questions and use the answers in any way. It preserves decidability but not enumerability: the complement of the halting problem is Turing reducible to the halting problem, but not many-one reducible to it.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these twelve entries in order: decidable language, computably enumerable language, the Church-Turing thesis, countability, the diagonalization argument, Gödel numbering, the universal Turing machine, the acceptance problem, the halting problem, many-one reducibility, Rice's theorem and the Post correspondence problem. Together they give the standard first course: what a decider is, why most problems have none, how a machine runs other machines, and how one undecidable problem yields many more.

You can skip groups 6 to 8 at first. The s-m-n and recursion theorems, the degrees and the hierarchy are the tools of recursion theory proper, and their entries assume the first four groups. In group 5, Church's theorem and the incompleteness theorems need some first-order logic, and the proofs for Diophantine equations, tilings and groups are long; read their statements first and return to the proofs later. The primitive and partial recursive functions can also wait, unless you want a model of computation without machines.
