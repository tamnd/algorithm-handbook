## History

The theory began in 1971 with Stephen Cook's paper *The complexity of theorem-proving procedures*, given at the third ACM Symposium on Theory of Computing. Cook showed that every set accepted in polynomial time by a nondeterministic machine reduces to the tautologies in disjunctive normal form, and he showed the reduction from SAT to clauses with at most three literals. His reductions were polynomial-time Turing reductions. In the Soviet Union, Leonid Levin found the same idea on his own and published it in 1973 as *Universal sequential search problems*, stated for search problems. The theorem now carries both names.

Richard Karp turned the result into a method. His 1972 paper *Reducibility among combinatorial problems* used the stronger many-one reductions, now called Karp reductions, and proved 21 problems complete by a tree of reductions from SAT. Among them were clique, Hamilton circuit, three-dimensional matching, exact cover, knapsack and partition. Many of these problems were old, and people had tried for years to solve them fast. After Karp's paper, a failure to find a fast algorithm could be explained, and a proof of hardness became a normal part of studying a problem. The names "NP-hard" and "NP-complete" were settled in 1974, after Donald Knuth polled the community and reported the result in *A terminological proposal*.

Michael Garey and David Johnson shaped the field for the next generation. In 1978 they set out strong NP-completeness in the *Journal of the ACM*, which separates number problems with pseudo-polynomial algorithms, such as subset sum, from those like 3-partition that stay hard with small numbers. Their 1979 book *Computers and Intractability: A Guide to the Theory of NP-Completeness* gave the method of proof and an appendix of more than 300 problems. Johnson kept the list current in his NP-completeness column, which began in 1981.

The structure of NP under the assumption P ≠ NP came next. Richard Ladner proved, in work presented in 1973 and published in 1975 as *On the structure of polynomial time reducibility*, that NP would then contain problems that are neither in P nor complete. In 1977 Leonard Berman and Juris Hartmanis noted that all known NP-complete problems were polynomial-time isomorphic, and they conjectured that no sparse set is NP-complete unless P = NP. Piotr Berman proved this for tally sets in 1978, and Steven Fortune proved the version for coNP in 1979. Stephen Mahaney proved the full conjecture, with the result published in 1982. Mitsunori Ogiwara and Osamu Watanabe extended it to bounded truth-table reductions in 1991, with the left-set method that most textbooks now use.

Along the way, some problems left the list of candidates. Vaughan Pratt showed in 1975 that primes have short certificates, and in 2004 Manindra Agrawal, Neeraj Kayal and Nitin Saxena showed that primality is in P. Leonid Khachiyan put linear programming in P in 1979. Johnson's own account of the story is *A brief history of NP-completeness, 1954–2012*.

## Further reading

- Michael R. Garey and David S. Johnson, *Computers and Intractability: A Guide to the Theory of NP-Completeness*, W. H. Freeman (1979). The standard guide to proving problems NP-complete, with the compendium of problems as its appendix.
- Sanjeev Arora and Boaz Barak, *Computational Complexity: A Modern Approach*, Cambridge University Press (2009). Chapter 2 covers NP, the Cook-Levin theorem and Ladner's theorem, and the later chapters place them in the rest of complexity theory.
- Michael Sipser, *Introduction to the Theory of Computation*, third edition. Chapter 7 is a careful first course in P, NP and the Cook-Levin proof by tableaux.
- Jon Kleinberg and Éva Tardos, *Algorithm Design* (2006). Chapter 8 teaches reductions as an algorithm designer uses them, with problems grouped by kind.
- David S. Johnson, *A brief history of NP-completeness, 1954–2012*, Documenta Mathematica, Extra Volume: Optimization Stories (2012). A short history of the theory, from early work on hard problems to the open questions, by the coauthor of the guide above.
