Alice holds a string $x$ of $n$ bits and Bob holds a string $y$, and they want to know whether $x = y$. Every deterministic protocol needs $n + 1$ bits in the worst case. If Alice reads $x$ as a polynomial over a prime field of size about $n^2$ and sends its value at a random point, about $4 \log_2 n$ bits suffice, with error below $1/n$. Communication complexity studies such games. It ignores how long each player computes and counts only the bits that cross between them. Query complexity does the same for one player: it counts how many bits of the input an algorithm must read, as a decision tree does.

These models matter because in them lower bounds can be proved without any hypothesis. A bound on communication is a theorem about every protocol, not a statement that holds unless P = NP. The models are also small enough to carry into larger settings. The one-way bound for the index problem becomes a space bound for streaming algorithms. The Karchmer-Wigderson game turns formula depth into communication. Query bounds show how far quantum algorithms can beat classical ones: search needs $\Theta(n)$ classical queries and $\Theta(\sqrt n)$ quantum ones, and no quantum algorithm does better.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups. The first six are about communication and the last two about queries. The two halves share their methods more than their models, so you can read the query groups first.

1. **The two-party model.** The [communication model](/communication-model/) and the [communication matrix](/communication-matrix/), whose monochromatic rectangles are the leaves of a protocol. On top of them come the [deterministic](/deterministic-communication-complexity/), [nondeterministic](/nondeterministic-communication-complexity/), [randomized](/randomized-communication-complexity/), [one-way](/one-way-communication-complexity/) and [simultaneous](/simultaneous-message-model/) costs.
2. **The standard problems.** [Equality](/equality-problem/) with its [randomized protocol](/equality-testing-protocol/), [greater-than](/greater-than-problem/), [disjointness](/disjointness-problem/), [inner product](/inner-product-problem/), [index](/index-problem/), [gap Hamming distance](/gap-hamming-distance/) and [pointer chasing](/pointer-chasing-problem/). Each one separates two models or serves as the source of reductions.
3. **Deterministic lower bounds.** The [fooling set method](/fooling-set-method/) and the [log-rank lower bound](/log-rank-lower-bound/), and the open [log-rank conjecture](/log-rank-conjecture/).
4. **Randomized lower bounds and information.** The [discrepancy method](/discrepancy-method/), the [corruption bound](/corruption-bound/) and [sign rank](/sign-rank/) bound randomized protocols. [Information complexity](/information-complexity/) adds up over copies, which gives [direct sum](/direct-sum-communication/) results, and [round elimination](/round-elimination/) bounds protocols with few rounds.
5. **More players and quantum messages.** [Multiparty communication](/multiparty-communication-complexity/) in the [number-in-hand](/number-in-hand-model/) and [number-on-forehead](/number-on-forehead-model/) models, and [quantum communication](/quantum-communication-complexity/).
6. **Communication and formula depth.** The [Karchmer-Wigderson relation](/karchmer-wigderson-relation/) and the [Karchmer-Wigderson theorem](/karchmer-wigderson-theorem/), which says that its cost equals formula depth.
7. **The query model and its measures.** The [query model](/query-model/), [decision tree complexity](/decision-tree-complexity/), [certificate complexity](/certificate-complexity/), [sensitivity](/sensitivity/), [block sensitivity](/block-sensitivity/) and [randomized query complexity](/randomized-query-complexity/), tied together by the [sensitivity theorem](/sensitivity-theorem/).
8. **Quantum queries and their lower bounds.** The [quantum query model](/quantum-query-model/), [approximate degree](/approximate-degree/), the [polynomial method](/polynomial-method/), the [hybrid argument](/hybrid-argument/) and the [quantum adversary method](/quantum-adversary-method/).

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these twelve entries in order: [communication complexity](/communication-complexity/), the communication model, the communication matrix, deterministic communication complexity, the equality problem, the equality testing protocol, the fooling set method, the log-rank lower bound, randomized communication complexity, the [query model](/query-model/), [query complexity](/query-complexity/) and decision tree complexity. Work the small examples by hand: write the $4 \times 4$ matrix of a function on two bits, find its rectangles, and compute its rank.

You can skip groups 4 to 6 and group 8 at first. The discrepancy and corruption bounds, information complexity and the quantum adversary method are research tools, and their entries assume the first groups. Gap Hamming distance, pointer chasing and the number-on-forehead model can wait until you need a lower bound for a streaming or circuit problem.
