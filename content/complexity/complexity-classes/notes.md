## History

The classes began with the measurement of resources. Juris Hartmanis and Richard Stearns defined time-bounded computation on multitape machines in 1965, in *On the computational complexity of algorithms*, and proved the time hierarchy theorem. With Philip Lewis they did the same for space in the same year. Alan Cobham and Jack Edmonds argued, also in 1965 and independently, that polynomial time is the right meaning of "efficient". Edmonds added the idea of a good characterization, a problem whose yes and no answers both have short proofs.

Nondeterminism and its cost came next. Walter Savitch showed in 1970 that nondeterministic space $s$ fits in deterministic space $s^2$. Stephen Cook in 1971 and Richard Karp in 1972 made P and NP the center of the subject, Albert Meyer and Larry Stockmeyer defined the polynomial hierarchy in 1972, and in 1973 they proved QSAT PSPACE-complete. Theodore Baker, John Gill and Robert Solovay found in 1975 oracles that make P equal to NP and oracles that separate them, the first sign that diagonalization alone would not settle the question. In 1987 and 1988 Neil Immerman and Róbert Szelepcsényi proved, independently, that nondeterministic space is closed under complement, against the general expectation.

Randomness entered with Gill's 1977 paper, which defined PP, BPP, RP and ZPP. Leonard Adleman showed in 1978 that randomized polynomial time has small circuits, and Michael Sipser and Péter Gács placed BPP in the polynomial hierarchy in 1983. Circuits became the main hope for lower bounds: Merrick Furst, James Saxe and Michael Sipser, and independently Miklós Ajtai, proved in the early 1980s that parity is not in AC0, and Alexander Razborov proved superpolynomial bounds for monotone circuits in 1985. Richard Karp and Richard Lipton had shown in 1980 that small circuits for NP would collapse the hierarchy.

Interactive proofs were defined in 1985, by Shafi Goldwasser, Silvio Micali and Charles Rackoff, and by László Babai as Arthur-Merlin games. In a few weeks around the end of 1989, Carsten Lund, Lance Fortnow, Howard Karloff and Noam Nisan found the sum-check protocol and Adi Shamir proved IP = PSPACE. Babai, Fortnow and Lund proved MIP = NEXP in 1990, and the PCP theorem of Arora and Safra and of Arora, Lund, Motwani, Sudan and Szegedy followed in 1992. Seinosuke Toda proved in 1989 that the hierarchy lies inside polynomial time with a counting oracle. The barriers were named later: Razborov and Steven Rudich described natural proofs in 1994, and Scott Aaronson and Avi Wigderson described algebrization in 2008.

## Further reading

- Sanjeev Arora and Boaz Barak, *Computational Complexity: A Modern Approach*, Cambridge University Press (2009). The standard graduate text; it covers every group of this chapter, from the hierarchy theorems to the PCP theorem and the barriers.
- Michael Sipser, *Introduction to the Theory of Computation*, third edition, Cengage (2012). Chapters 7 to 10 are a careful first course in time and space classes, Savitch's theorem and the hierarchy theorems.
- Christos H. Papadimitriou, *Computational Complexity*, Addison-Wesley (1994). Strong on the polynomial hierarchy, the Boolean hierarchy and the search classes, which Papadimitriou helped to define.
- Oded Goldreich, *Computational Complexity: A Conceptual Perspective*, Cambridge University Press (2008). Explains why the definitions are what they are, with long chapters on randomness and on proof systems.
- Scott Aaronson, *P =? NP*, in *Open Problems in Mathematics*, Springer (2016). A survey of what is known about the main open question and of the barriers that stand in the way.
