## History

Recovery was designed together with the first transaction systems. Dennis Severance and Guy Lohman proposed differential files in 1976, as a way to keep a large file read-only and make backup and recovery cheap; the Bloom filter they used to route lookups came from Burton Bloom's paper of 1970, and in 1982 Gremillion, and Aghili and Severance, studied how to design such files. Raymond Lorie described shadow pages in 1977, as the method of the System R storage layer for keeping a consistent copy of each file. Jim Gray's *Notes on Data Base Operating Systems* of 1978 set out the write-ahead log, the commit rule, two-phase commit and the idea of a transaction as the unit of recovery. In 1981 Gray and his colleagues reported on the recovery manager of System R and found that shadow pages were costly next to the log, which was needed anyway.

Theo Haerder and Andreas Reuter gave the field its vocabulary in 1983, in *Principles of Transaction-Oriented Database Recovery*. They named the ACID properties and classified recovery methods by the steal and force choices of the buffer manager, by how they propagate changes, and by the kind of checkpoint they take, fuzzy checkpoints among them. Their taxonomy explained why the fastest buffer policy, steal with no-force, needs both undo and redo.

Group commit appeared in IMS Fast Path in the late 1970s, and was studied by David DeWitt and his colleagues for main memory databases in 1984. In 1992 C. Mohan, Don Haderle, Bruce Lindsay, Hamid Pirahesh and Peter Schwarz published ARIES. It combined the write-ahead log, a log sequence number on each page, fuzzy checkpoints, repeating history and compensation log records, and it supported record-level locking and logical undo. ARIES became the reference design, and IBM DB2, Microsoft SQL Server and many other systems use variants of it.

Operations above the page level led to multi-level recovery. Gerhard Weikum set out multilevel transaction management in 1991, and David Lomet gave the MLR recovery method in 1992; ARIES reached a similar effect with nested top actions. Undo by logical inverses lets locks on pages be released at the end of each operation, while the logical locks are held to commit.

Later work changed the hardware assumptions. Copy-on-write B-trees, as in LMDB, revived shadow paging for embedded stores. Nirmesh Malviya, Ariel Weisberg, Samuel Madden and Michael Stonebraker compared command logging with physiological logging in VoltDB in 2014, and found that the smaller log gave higher throughput while replay made recovery slower. Joy Arulraj, Matthew Perron and Andrew Pavlo proposed write-behind logging for non-volatile memory in 2016, with measurements on an emulator. Theo Härder, Caetano Sauer, Goetz Graefe and Wey Guy described instant restart in 2015, as part of a family of instant recovery methods that also restore media page by page on demand.

## Further reading

- Theo Haerder and Andreas Reuter, *Principles of Transaction-Oriented Database Recovery*, ACM Computing Surveys 15(4), 1983. The steal and force taxonomy and the ACID properties.
- C. Mohan, Don Haderle, Bruce Lindsay, Hamid Pirahesh and Peter Schwarz, *ARIES: A Transaction Recovery Method Supporting Fine-Granularity Locking and Partial Rollbacks Using Write-Ahead Logging*, ACM Transactions on Database Systems 17(1), 1992. The full algorithm, with many design discussions.
- Jim Gray and Andreas Reuter, *Transaction Processing: Concepts and Techniques*, Morgan Kaufmann, 1993. Logging, buffers and recovery as real systems build them.
- Raghu Ramakrishnan and Johannes Gehrke, *Database Management Systems*, 3rd edition, McGraw-Hill, 2003. A textbook account of ARIES with worked logs.
- Goetz Graefe, Wey Guy and Caetano Sauer, *Instant Recovery with Write-Ahead Logging*, second edition, Morgan and Claypool, 2016. Single-page repair, instant restart and instant restore built on ARIES.
- Philip A. Bernstein, Vassos Hadzilacos and Nathan Goodman, *Concurrency Control and Recovery in Database Systems*, Addison-Wesley, 1987. The recovery chapters give the model of the crash recovery problem.
