A bank keeps its customers, accounts and transfers in tables. A query asks for the customers with an account in two cities, or for every account reachable from a given one by transfers. Behind each such table and query sits the theory of this chapter. It says what a table is, which queries can be asked of it, how to tell whether two queries always give the same answer, and how to design tables so that each fact is stored once.

Relational theory is the mathematics of database systems. Edgar Codd's model of 1970 treats data as finite relations and queries as operations on them, so a query can be rewritten, compared and optimized like an algebraic expression. Much of what a database system does later, from schema design to query rewriting and join planning, rests on the results collected here. Many of them are theorems with exact complexity: the closure of a set of attributes takes linear time, containment of conjunctive queries is NP-complete, and Datalog evaluation is P-complete in data complexity.

## What the chapter covers

1. **The model and its query languages.** The [relational model](/relational-model/), [relational algebra](/relational-algebra/) and [relational calculus](/relational-calculus/), the [three-valued logic of SQL nulls](/sql-null-semantics/), [domain independence](/domain-independent-query/), [Codd's theorem](/codds-theorem/) that the algebra and the safe calculus have the same power, [conjunctive queries](/conjunctive-query/), and [data and combined complexity](/data-and-combined-complexity/).
2. **Functional dependencies and keys.** [Functional dependencies](/functional-dependency/), [Armstrong's axioms](/armstrongs-axioms/), the [attribute closure algorithm](/attribute-closure-algorithm/), [candidate keys](/candidate-key/), the [implication problem](/dependency-implication-problem/), [minimal covers](/minimal-cover-problem/) and the [NP-completeness of the prime attribute problem](/prime-attribute-np-completeness/).
3. **Normal forms.** From [first normal form](/first-normal-form/) and the aims of [normalization](/normalization/) through [2NF](/second-normal-form/) and [3NF](/third-normal-form/) to [BCNF](/boyce-codd-normal-form/), with the [entity-relationship model](/entity-relationship-model/) as the design step before them.
4. **Decompositions.** [Lossless joins](/lossless-join-decomposition/), [Heath's theorem](/heaths-theorem/), the [tableau test](/lossless-join-test/), [dependency preservation](/dependency-preserving-decomposition/), [BCNF decomposition](/bcnf-decomposition/) and [3NF synthesis](/third-normal-form-synthesis/).
5. **More dependencies and the chase.** [Multivalued](/multivalued-dependency/) and [join dependencies](/join-dependency/) with [4NF](/fourth-normal-form/) and [5NF](/fifth-normal-form/), [inclusion dependencies](/inclusion-dependency/), the general [equality-generating](/equality-generating-dependency/) and [tuple-generating dependencies](/tuple-generating-dependency/), and the [chase](/chase-algorithm/).
6. **Containment and minimization.** The [query containment problem](/query-containment-problem/), the [Chandra-Merlin theorem](/chandra-merlin-theorem/), [conjunctive query minimization](/conjunctive-query-minimization/) and the [Sagiv-Yannakakis theorem](/sagiv-yannakakis-theorem/) for unions.
7. **Acyclicity and width.** [Acyclic hypergraphs](/acyclic-hypergraph/), the [GYO reduction](/gyo-reduction/), [hypertree width](/hypertree-width/) and [fractional hypertree width](/fractional-hypertree-width/).
8. **Datalog and recursion.** [Datalog](/datalog/), the [evaluation problem](/datalog-evaluation/), [semi-naive evaluation](/semi-naive-evaluation/), [magic sets](/magic-sets/) and [regular path queries](/regular-path-query/).
9. **Provenance and uncertainty.** [Why- and where-provenance](/why-and-where-provenance/), [provenance semirings](/provenance-semirings/), [probabilistic databases](/probabilistic-database/) and the [Dalvi-Suciu dichotomy](/dalvi-suciu-dichotomy/).

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these thirteen entries in order: the relational model, relational algebra, conjunctive queries, SQL nulls, functional dependencies, Armstrong's axioms, the attribute closure algorithm, candidate keys, 3NF, BCNF, lossless joins, BCNF decomposition and 3NF synthesis. They are the core of schema design, and each has small examples worth redoing by hand. Then read the Chandra-Merlin theorem and Datalog, which open the two other large themes of the chapter, query reasoning and recursion.

You can leave the calculus, domain independence and Codd's theorem for later unless you care about query languages as logic. The fifth group, the width measures of the seventh and the whole last group are more advanced. They matter for data exchange, query evaluation and uncertain data, and they lean on the earlier groups.

## Before you start

You need sets, relations and first-order logic at the level of a first course in discrete mathematics. The width measures use [hypergraphs](/hypergraph/), [trees](/tree/), [tree decompositions](/tree-decomposition/) and [edge covers](/edge-cover/) from Volume 7, and [linear programming](/linear-programming-problem/) from Volume 15. Datalog builds on [Horn clauses](/horn-clause/) from Volume 16, and probabilistic databases on [model counting](/model-counting/) from the same volume. Regular path queries use [nondeterministic finite automata](/nondeterministic-finite-automaton/) and the [product construction](/product-construction/) from Volume 9, and [graph reachability](/graph-reachability/) from Volume 7. The hardness results use [NP-completeness](/np-complete/) from Volume 39.
