## History

Distributed database design began with the placement of files. Wesley Chu gave a zero-one model of file allocation in 1969, and Kapali Eswaran showed in 1974 that the placement problem is NP-complete. For splitting relations, William McCormick, Paul Schweitzer and Thomas White published the bond energy algorithm in 1972 as a way to cluster a data matrix, and Shamkant Navathe, Stefano Ceri, Gio Wiederhold and Jinglie Dou used it for vertical partitioning in 1984. Ceri, Mauro Negri and Giuseppe Pelagatti treated horizontal fragmentation by predicates in 1982.

The first systems came at the end of the 1970s. Michael Stonebraker proposed primary copy locking for distributed INGRES in 1979. SDD-1, built at Computer Corporation of America, was described in ACM Transactions on Database Systems in 1980 and 1981; Philip Bernstein, Nathan Goodman, Eugene Wong, Christopher Reeve and James Rothnie made the semijoin its main query tool, and Bernstein and Dah-Ming Chiu gave the full reducers of tree queries in the same year. Robert Thomas and David Gifford proposed voting for replicated data in 1979, and Bernstein and Goodman gave the available copies algorithm in 1984, with a proof based on one-copy serializability. K. Mani Chandy, Jayadev Misra and Laura Haas published their probe algorithm for distributed deadlock detection in 1983.

Parallel databases on many nodes followed. The GRACE machine of Masaru Kitsuregawa, Hidehiko Tanaka and Tohru Moto-oka split relations into buckets by hashing in 1983, and David DeWitt and Robert Gerber gave multiprocessor hash joins in 1985. Stonebraker argued for the shared-nothing design in 1986, and the Gamma machine of DeWitt and his group showed close to linear join speedup on 32 nodes in 1990. In 1996 Jim Gray, Pat Helland, Patrick O'Neil and Dennis Shasha showed with a simple model that eager replication and lazy group replication do not scale, because deadlocks and reconciliations grow with the cube of the number of nodes.

A newer line of work began around 2010, when large systems ran on many cheap machines across data centres. Carlo Curino, Evan Jones, Yang Zhang and Samuel Madden presented Schism in 2010. Daniel Peng and Frank Dabek described Percolator, snapshot isolation on Bigtable, in the same year. Alexander Thomson, Daniel Abadi and their colleagues presented Calvin at SIGMOD 2012, and Jiaqing Du, Sameh Elnikety and Willy Zwaenepoel gave Clock-SI in 2013. Peter Bailis, Alan Fekete, Ali Ghodsi, Joseph Hellerstein and Ion Stoica, with others, studied which guarantees need coordination: highly available transactions in 2013, RAMP transactions at SIGMOD 2014, and invariant confluence in 2014.

## Further reading

- M. Tamer Özsu and Patrick Valduriez, *Principles of Distributed Database Systems*, fourth edition (2020). The standard textbook: fragmentation, allocation, distributed query processing, replication and distributed transactions.
- Philip A. Bernstein, Vassos Hadzilacos and Nathan Goodman, *Concurrency Control and Recovery in Database Systems* (1987). Chapter 8 gives the theory of one-copy serializability and the available copies algorithm with proofs.
- Jim Gray, Pat Helland, Patrick O'Neil and Dennis Shasha, *The Dangers of Replication and a Solution* (1996). The scaling model of eager and lazy replication in a short paper.
- Donald Kossmann, *The State of the Art in Distributed Query Processing*, ACM Computing Surveys 32(4), 2000. Data shipping, query shipping, semijoins and the cost models of distributed plans.
- David J. DeWitt and Jim Gray, *Parallel Database Systems: The Future of High Performance Database Systems*, Communications of the ACM 35(6), 1992. The case for shared-nothing parallelism and partitioned dataflow.
