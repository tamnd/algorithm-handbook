A bank keeps its accounts on twenty machines in three cities. A transfer from an account in one city to an account in another touches two machines, and both must apply it or neither. A customer in each city wants to read its balance from the nearest copy, so each account has copies in several places, and the copies must agree well enough that no money appears or vanishes. When the link between two cities fails, the bank must choose between refusing some work and accepting work that may later conflict. This chapter is about the algorithms that make these choices: where to put the data, how to run queries and transactions over it, and which guarantees still hold when the nodes cannot talk.

Most of the chapter builds on the single-node material of the volume. A distributed query is a query plan with a cost for each byte shipped. A distributed transaction still has to be serializable, now over copies. The general machinery of agreement, [two-phase commit](/two-phase-commit/), [Paxos](/paxos/) and [state machine replication](/state-machine-replication/), is in Volume 21, and this chapter uses it without proving it again.

## What the chapter covers

1. **Splitting the data across nodes.** The [shared-nothing architecture](/shared-nothing-architecture/) gives each node its own memory and disks. A relation is cut by rows with [horizontal fragmentation](/horizontal-fragmentation/) or by columns with [vertical fragmentation](/vertical-fragmentation/), and the [bond energy algorithm](/bond-energy-algorithm/) groups columns that queries use together. The [data allocation problem](/data-allocation-problem/) places fragments and copies on sites, and is NP-hard. [Schism](/schism-partitioning/) learns a partition of the rows from a trace by graph partitioning.
2. **Queries over distributed data.** [Distributed query processing](/distributed-query-processing/) adds the cost of shipping data to the plan. [SDD-1](/sdd1-semijoin-optimization/) reduces relations with semijoins before it ships them, and the [parallel hash join](/parallel-hash-join/) reshuffles both inputs by a hash of the key in one round.
3. **Replicated data.** [One-copy serializability](/one-copy-serializability/) is the goal for copies. [Eager and lazy replication](/eager-and-lazy-replication/) differ in when the copies change, with deadlock and reconciliation rates that grow fast with the number of nodes. [Primary copy locking](/primary-copy-locking/) and [read-one-write-all](/read-one-write-all/) are the two classic eager protocols.
4. **Transactions across nodes.** The [Chandy-Misra-Haas algorithm](/chandy-misra-haas/) finds deadlock cycles by sending probes. [Calvin](/calvin-deterministic-transactions/) orders transactions before they run and needs no two-phase commit. [Clock-SI](/clock-si/) and [Percolator](/percolator-transactions/) give snapshot isolation without and with a central timestamp source.
5. **Avoiding coordination.** [Highly available transactions](/highly-available-transactions/) sorts the isolation levels into those that a system can give during a partition and those it cannot. [RAMP transactions](/ramp-transactions/) give atomic visibility with no locks, and [invariant confluence](/invariant-confluence/) says which invariants survive the merge of concurrent work.

## A first reading

For a first pass, read the shared-nothing architecture, horizontal and vertical fragmentation, distributed query processing, the parallel hash join, one-copy serializability, eager and lazy replication, read-one-write-all, primary copy locking and highly available transactions. These ten give the vocabulary and the main trade-offs. Then read one of Calvin, Clock-SI or Percolator in full, to see how a modern system puts the pieces together.

The bond energy algorithm, the allocation problem and SDD-1 are design-time methods from the 1970s and 1980s; skip them unless you plan a schema by hand. Chandy-Misra-Haas, RAMP and invariant confluence can wait until you need them.
