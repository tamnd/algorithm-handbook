Many problems ask for a sequence of moves. A chess program chooses a move against an opponent who wants the opposite result. A route planner looks for the cheapest path through a map too large to list. A robot needs a plan of actions that turns the world it sees into the world it wants, or a path for its arm through a room full of obstacles. A puzzle solver looks for the shortest way to restore a Rubik's Cube. All of them search a space of states that is given by rules, not by a list, and far too large to explore in full.

This volume is about those searches. Its four chapters hold 209 entries: game tree search, heuristic search, planning and puzzles.

## Why games and search get a volume of their own

The searches of this volume are built from parts of other volumes: depth-first and breadth-first search, priority queues, hash tables, dynamic programming and linear programming. What they add is a way of stating what a search finds and what it costs. A few ideas come back in every chapter:

- **The state space is implicit.** A game, a puzzle or a planning task is given by a start state and rules for the moves, and the [state space](/state-space/) has more states than any memory can hold: about $4.3 \times 10^{19}$ for the Rubik's Cube, about $10^{123}$ games of chess. The algorithms generate states as they need them and spend their effort on not generating the rest.
- **Each guarantee names its model.** [Minimax](/minimax/) values are for two-player zero-sum games of perfect information; mixed strategies and equilibria are needed when information is hidden. [A*](/a-star-search/) returns a cheapest path when its heuristic is [admissible](/admissible-heuristic/), and expands no state twice when it is [consistent](/consistent-heuristic/). A cost is a worst case, an expected case, a best case with perfect [move ordering](/move-ordering/), or a limit: [Monte Carlo tree search](/monte-carlo-tree-search/) finds the minimax move only in the limit of many playouts, and [RRT*](/rrt-star/) is asymptotically optimal with probability one.
- **Some tools are only measured.** Evaluation functions, [null-move pruning](/null-move-pruning/), late move reductions and futility pruning are kept because they win games in matches. Forward pruning can change the value a search returns. Planners are compared on the benchmarks of the [International Planning Competition](/international-planning-competition/). The entries say which claims are proved and which are measured.
- **A heuristic is a solution of an easier problem.** An admissible heuristic is often the exact cost in a relaxed problem: the Manhattan distance of the sliding puzzle, a [pattern database](/pattern-database/), the [delete relaxation](/delete-relaxation/) of a planning task. The same idea gives an evaluation function its features.
- **The general problems are hard.** Generalized geography and Hex on $n \times n$ boards are PSPACE-complete, [STRIPS plan existence](/strips-plan-existence-pspace-complete/) is PSPACE-complete, plan existence with a bound in unary is [NP-complete](/bounded-plan-existence-np-complete/), and many puzzles generalized to $n \times n$ boards are NP-complete or PSPACE-complete. These results are about the family of instances, not about the fixed 9 by 9 Sudoku or the 15-puzzle, which a program solves in moments.

## The chapters

**Chapter 29.1, Game tree search**, chooses moves in games between two players. It covers the [game tree](/game-tree/), [minimax](/minimax/) and [negamax](/negamax/); [alpha-beta pruning](/alpha-beta-pruning/), which reads all $b^d$ leaves in the worst case and $b^{\lceil d/2 \rceil} + b^{\lfloor d/2 \rfloor} - 1$ with perfect ordering, the least possible by the [Knuth-Moore theorem](/knuth-moore-theorem/); move ordering, the [transposition table](/transposition-table/), [principal variation search](/principal-variation-search/), [MTD(f)](/mtdf/) and [SSS*](/sss-star/); the measured techniques of chess programs, from [quiescence search](/quiescence-search/) to forward pruning; [proof-number search](/proof-number-search/), [retrograde analysis](/retrograde-analysis/), [endgame tablebases](/endgame-tablebase/) and [solved games](/solved-game/) such as checkers, a draw; [expectiminimax](/expectiminimax/) for dice, and Monte Carlo tree search with [UCT](/uct/); the [minimax theorem](/minimax-theorem/), [Nash equilibria](/nash-equilibrium/) and [counterfactual regret minimization](/counterfactual-regret-minimization/) for hidden information; and the combinatorial games, from [Nim](/nim/) and the [Sprague-Grundy theorem](/sprague-grundy-theorem/) to [Hex](/hex-game/) and [generalized geography](/generalized-geography/).

**Chapter 29.2, Heuristic search**, finds cheapest paths in large implicit graphs. It covers the [state space](/state-space/), [best-first search](/best-first-search/) and the [heuristic function](/heuristic-function/); A* and its [optimality](/a-star-optimality/) with an admissible heuristic, its [optimal efficiency](/optimal-efficiency-of-a-star/) with a consistent one, and [heuristic dominance](/heuristic-dominance/); the memory-bounded searches [IDA*](/iterative-deepening-a-star/), [RBFS](/recursive-best-first-search/) and [SMA*](/simplified-memory-bounded-a-star/); [weighted A*](/weighted-a-star/), within a factor $w$ of the optimum, [beam search](/beam-search/) and the anytime searches; pattern databases and [relaxation heuristics](/relaxation-heuristic/); search on grids with [jump point search](/jump-point-search/) and [Theta*](/theta-star/); replanning when the map changes with [LPA*](/lifelong-planning-a-star/) and [D* Lite](/d-star-lite/); real-time search with [LRTA*](/learning-real-time-a-star/); and [multi-agent pathfinding](/multi-agent-pathfinding/) with [conflict-based search](/conflict-based-search/).

**Chapter 29.3, Planning**, finds sequences of actions that reach a goal. It covers [STRIPS](/strips/), [PDDL](/pddl/) and [classical planning](/classical-planning/), with the [blocks world](/blocks-world/) and the [Sussman anomaly](/sussman-anomaly/); forward and [backward search](/backward-state-space-search/), [means-ends analysis](/means-ends-analysis/) and [partial-order planning](/partial-order-plan/); the [planning graph](/planning-graph/) and [GRAPHPLAN](/graphplan/), and planning as satisfiability with [SATPLAN](/satplan/); the delete relaxation and the heuristics built on it, the admissible [h-max](/h-max-heuristic/), the inadmissible [h-add](/h-add-heuristic/), the [FF planner](/fast-forward-planner/) and [landmarks](/landmark-heuristic/); [hierarchical task networks](/htn-planning/); the complexity of planning, PSPACE-complete in general and NP-complete with a length bound written in unary; planning under uncertainty with the [Bellman equation](/bellman-equation/), [stochastic shortest paths](/stochastic-shortest-path/), [POMDPs](/pomdp-planning/) and [conformant](/conformant-planning/) and [contingent](/contingent-planning/) planning; and [motion planning](/motion-planning/) in the [configuration space](/configuration-space/), from the [piano movers' problem](/piano-movers-problem/) to the sampling planners [PRM](/probabilistic-roadmap/), [RRT](/rapidly-exploring-random-tree/) and RRT*, whose [asymptotic optimality](/rrt-star-asymptotic-optimality/) needed a corrected proof.

**Chapter 29.4, Puzzles**, solves puzzles exactly and shows when their generalizations are hard. It covers the [Tower of Hanoi](/tower-of-hanoi/) in $2^n - 1$ moves and the [Frame-Stewart algorithm](/frame-stewart-algorithm/) for more pegs; the [sliding-tile puzzle](/sliding-tile-puzzle/), whose [solvability](/sliding-puzzle-solvability/) is a parity test; the [Rubik's Cube](/rubiks-cube-problem/), [Kociemba's algorithm](/kociemba-algorithm/) and [God's number](/gods-number/), 20 face turns; [Lights Out](/lights-out-puzzle/), solved by linear algebra over GF(2); [Mastermind](/mastermind-problem/), the [counterfeit coin](/counterfeit-coin-problem/) and [twenty questions](/twenty-questions-problem/), where each answer is information; the [knight's tour](/knights-tour-problem/) with [Warnsdorff's rule](/warnsdorff-rule/), the [Josephus problem](/josephus-problem/), [magic squares](/magic-square-problem/) and the [water jugs](/water-jug-problem/); and the hardness of the generalized puzzles: [Sudoku](/sudoku/), [Tetris](/tetris-problem/) and the pencil puzzles are NP-complete, and [Rush Hour](/rush-hour-problem/) and [Sokoban](/sokoban-problem/) are PSPACE-complete.

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course, read these entries in this order: the game tree, minimax, alpha-beta pruning, the Knuth-Moore theorem, the transposition table, Monte Carlo tree search, Nim, the state space, the heuristic function, A* search, admissible heuristic, consistent heuristic, IDA*, pattern database, STRIPS, classical planning, delete relaxation, the planning graph, configuration space, the rapidly-exploring random tree, the Tower of Hanoi and sliding-puzzle solvability. Do the exercises marked ◦ at the end of each chapter you touch.

If you already know the basic algorithms, start with the opening page of each chapter. It names the groups in the chapter and the order to read them. Chapters 29.1 and 29.2 can be read in either order. Chapter 29.3 uses the heuristic search of Chapter 29.2, since most planners are A* or a greedy search guided by a heuristic, and Chapter 29.4 uses both, for the searches that solve puzzles and for the pattern databases that guide them.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained. Entries in other volumes are cited by number too.

## What you need first

You should be able to read a short program and follow a proof by induction. From Volume 7 you need [depth-first search](/depth-first-search/), [breadth-first search](/breadth-first-search/) and [Dijkstra's algorithm](/dijkstra/), of which A* is a refinement. From Volume 3 you need the [priority queue](/priority-queue/) and the [hash table](/hash-table/); from Volume 2, [iterative deepening](/iterative-deepening-search/), [backtracking](/generic-backtracking-algorithm/), [dynamic programming](/tabulation/) and [bitwise operations](/bitwise-operations/); from Volume 5, [Zobrist hashing](/zobrist-hashing/). Planning as satisfiability uses [SAT](/sat/) from Volume 16, and the hardness proofs reduce from its [quantified Boolean formulas](/qbf/). Games of mixed strategies use [LP duality](/lp-duality/) from Volume 15, and planning under uncertainty uses the [Markov decision process](/markov-decision-process/) and [value iteration](/value-iteration/) of Volume 25.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
