## History

Many of these puzzles are older than the algorithms that solve them. Euler studied knight's tours in 1759, and Warnsdorff proposed his rule of the fewest onward moves in 1823. Édouard Lucas published the Tower of Hanoi in 1883. Frame and Stewart gave the four-peg recurrence in 1941, and its optimality for four pegs waited until Bousch's proof of 2014; for five or more pegs it is still a conjecture. Dyson settled the counterfeit coin problem in 1946 with a scheme of ternary labels whose weighings are all fixed in advance.

The 15-puzzle became a craze around 1880. Johnson and Story proved in 1879 that half of the arrangements cannot be solved, and Wilson in 1974 found which permutations are reachable for a sliding puzzle on any 2-connected graph. Ratner and Warmuth proved in 1990 that finding a shortest solution of the $(n^2 - 1)$-puzzle is NP-hard. Korf's IDA* of 1985, and later pattern databases, made optimal solutions of the 15- and 24-puzzles practical.

Rubik's cube led to a long chain of bounds on the number of moves. Thistlethwaite's subgroup method of 1981 needed at most 52 moves. Kociemba's two-phase algorithm of 1992 reduced the work to two searches, Reid showed in 1995 that every position can be solved in 29 moves, and Korf solved random positions optimally in 1997 with pattern databases. In 2010 Rokicki, Kociemba, Davidson and Dethridge proved that God's number is 20 in the half-turn metric, by solving every coset of a large subgroup by computer.

In 1961 Kasteleyn, and independently Temperley and Fisher, counted the domino tilings of a rectangle by a product formula, and Schwenk in 1991 characterized the boards with a closed knight's tour. The complexity of puzzles grew into a field of its own around 2000. Colbourn proved Latin square completion NP-complete in 1984, Culberson proved Sokoban PSPACE-complete in 1999, Kaye proved Minesweeper consistency NP-complete in 2000, Flake and Baum proved Rush Hour PSPACE-complete in 2002, and Yato and Seta showed in 2003 that Sudoku and other pencil puzzles are ASP-complete. Demaine, Hohenberger and Liben-Nowell proved offline Tetris NP-complete in 2003, and Hearn and Demaine built a general tool for PSPACE-hardness of motion puzzles, nondeterministic constraint logic, in 2005.

## Further reading

- Robert A. Hearn and Erik D. Demaine, *Games, Puzzles, and Computation* (A K Peters, 2009). Nondeterministic constraint logic and the hardness proofs for sliding blocks, Rush Hour, Sokoban and many other puzzles.
- Andreas M. Hinz, Sandi Klavžar and Ciril Petr, *The Tower of Hanoi: Myths and Maths*, 2nd ed. (Birkhäuser, 2018). Hanoi graphs, variants with more pegs and the Frame-Stewart conjecture.
- Tomas Rokicki, Herbert Kociemba, Morley Davidson and John Dethridge, "The diameter of the Rubik's Cube group is twenty", SIAM Review 56(4), 2014. The coset method and the computation that proved God's number.
- Solomon W. Golomb, *Polyominoes: Puzzles, Patterns, Problems, and Packings*, 2nd ed. (Princeton University Press, 1994). Colouring arguments and the classical tiling puzzles.
- John J. Watkins, *Across the Board: The Mathematics of Chessboard Problems* (Princeton University Press, 2004). Knight's tours, Schwenk's theorem and domino tilings at an elementary level.
- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming*, Volume 4B, *Combinatorial Algorithms, Part 2* (Addison-Wesley, 2022). Dancing links and exact cover, with Sudoku, polyomino and many other puzzles as examples.
