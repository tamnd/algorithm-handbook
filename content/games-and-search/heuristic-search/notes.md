## History

Best-first search with an estimate of the distance to the goal appeared in Doran and Michie's Graph Traverser of 1966. Peter Hart, Nils Nilsson and Bertram Raphael published A* in 1968. They proved that it is optimal with an admissible heuristic, and that it expands no more states than any admissible algorithm with the same information, under an assumption that is consistency in today's terms; a correction of 1972 claimed that the assumption was not needed. Ira Pohl studied the weighted evaluation in 1970 and bidirectional heuristic search in 1971. Alberto Martelli showed in 1977 that A* with an admissible but inconsistent heuristic can make an exponential number of reexpansions, and László Mérő gave pathmax in 1984. Judea Pearl's book *Heuristics* (1984) collected the theory, and Rina Dechter and Pearl settled the question of optimal efficiency in 1985: they defined the classes of algorithms exactly and showed that the result needs consistency, so the claim of 1972 was false.

Where heuristics come from was the next question. John Gaschnig measured search algorithms and the effect of relaxations in his 1979 thesis, and Pearl described heuristics as the costs of simplified models. Joseph Culberson and Jonathan Schaeffer introduced pattern databases in 1998, Richard Korf used them to solve random instances of Rubik's Cube optimally in 1997, and Korf and Ariel Felner gave disjoint additive databases in 2002.

Memory was the other limit. Korf's IDA* of 1985 solved random 15-puzzles optimally in linear space, and his RBFS of 1993 kept the best-first order in linear space. Stuart Russell gave SMA* in 1992, Takayuki Yoshizumi, Teruhisa Miura and Toru Ishida partial expansion A* in 2000, and Yngvi Björnsson, Markus Enzenberger, Robert Holte and Jonathan Schaeffer fringe search in 2005. For suboptimal search, beam search goes back to Bruce Lowerre's Harpy speech recognizer of 1976, William Harvey and Matthew Ginsberg introduced limited discrepancy search in 1995 and Korf improved it in 1996, and Rong Zhou and Eric Hansen gave beam-stack search in 2005. Maxim Likhachev, Geoff Gordon and Sebastian Thrun published ARA* in 2003.

Robots and games brought search into worlds that change. Korf's RTA* and LRTA* of 1990 limited the work per move. Anthony Stentz's D* of 1994 replanned for a robot on partly known terrain, and Sven Koenig, Likhachev and David Furcy's LPA* led to D* Lite by Koenig and Likhachev in 2002. Adi Botea, Martin Müller and Schaeffer gave HPA* in 2004, Alex Nash, Kenny Daniel, Koenig and Felner gave Theta* in 2007, and Daniel Harabor and Alban Grastien jump point search in 2011. Holte, Felner, Guni Sharon and Nathan Sturtevant gave MM in 2016. For many agents, Sharon, Roni Stern, Felner and Sturtevant introduced conflict-based search in 2012, and Jingjin Yu and Steven LaValle proved optimal multi-agent pathfinding NP-hard in 2013.

## Further reading

- Stuart Russell and Peter Norvig, *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach*, 4th ed. (Pearson, 2020). Chapter 3 is the standard first account of A*, its conditions and its heuristics.
- Judea Pearl, *Heuristics: Intelligent Search Strategies for Computer Problem Solving* (Addison-Wesley, 1984). The classic theory of admissibility, consistency, dominance and the complexity of A*.
- Stefan Edelkamp and Stefan Schrödl, *Heuristic Search: Theory and Applications* (Morgan Kaufmann, 2012). A broad treatment of memory-bounded, external, incremental and real-time search and of pattern databases.
- Rina Dechter and Judea Pearl, "Generalized best-first search strategies and the optimality of A*", Journal of the ACM (1985). The paper that fixes what optimal efficiency means and when it holds.
- Roni Stern and others, "Multi-agent pathfinding: definitions, variants, and benchmarks", Symposium on Combinatorial Search (2019). The common terms and conflict rules of multi-agent pathfinding.
