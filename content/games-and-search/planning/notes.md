## History

Planning began with the General Problem Solver of Allen Newell, J. C. Shaw and Herbert Simon (1959), which reduced the differences between a state and a goal by [means-ends analysis](/means-ends-analysis/). John McCarthy and Patrick Hayes named the [frame problem](/frame-problem/) in 1969. Richard Fikes and Nils Nilsson built STRIPS in 1971 as the planner of the Shakey robot, and its operators are still the base model; in 1972 Fikes, Peter Hart and Nilsson added triangle tables for execution monitoring. Gerald Sussman's thesis of 1975 described the blocks-world task that showed that working on one goal at a time can fail.

Earl Sacerdoti's NOAH (1975) and Austin Tate's NONLIN (1977) kept plans as partial orders. David Chapman's TWEAK (1987) gave the first precise account, with the modal truth criterion, and showed that richer action representations make planning undecidable. David McAllester and David Rosenblitt gave the systematic planner SNLP in 1991, and J. Scott Penberthy and Daniel Weld added conditional effects and quantifiers in UCPOP in 1992. Kutluhan Erol, James Hendler and Dana Nau gave hierarchical planning a formal semantics and its first decidability results in 1994. Tom Bylander proved in 1994 that plan existence for propositional STRIPS is PSPACE-complete.

Avrim Blum and Merrick Furst presented Graphplan in 1995. Henry Kautz and Bart Selman proposed planning as satisfiability in 1992 and showed in 1996 that SAT encodings could beat Graphplan. Drew McDermott organized the first International Planning Competition at AIPS-98 and introduced PDDL with it. Blai Bonet and Hector Geffner computed heuristics from the delete relaxation in ASP and HSP, and Jörg Hoffmann and Bernhard Nebel's FF was the fastest fully automatic planner at the competition of 2000. Malte Helmert's Fast Downward (2006) brought the SAS+ representation and the causal graph heuristic, and the LAMA planner of Silvia Richter and Matthias Westphal (2010) made landmarks central to satisficing planning.

Planning under uncertainty draws on Richard Bellman's dynamic programming. Dimitri Bertsekas and John Tsitsiklis gave the theory of stochastic shortest paths in 1991, and Andrew Barto, Steven Bradtke and Satinder Singh introduced RTDP in 1995. Richard Smallwood and Edward Sondik showed in 1973 that the finite-horizon value function of a POMDP is piecewise linear and convex, and Joelle Pineau, Geoffrey Gordon and Sebastian Thrun restricted the backups to a set of beliefs in PBVI (2003). Alessandro Cimatti, Marco Pistore, Marco Roveri and Paolo Traverso planned for nondeterministic actions by symbolic model checking (2003), and Miquel Ramírez and Geffner recast plan recognition as planning in 2009 and 2010.

Tomás Lozano-Pérez made configuration space the base of motion planning in 1983. John Reif proved the generalized movers' problem PSPACE-hard in 1979; Jacob Schwartz and Micha Sharir solved the general piano movers' problem in 1983 by cylindrical algebraic decomposition, and John Canny's roadmap algorithm of 1988 improved the bound to singly exponential. Oussama Khatib introduced potential fields in 1986, and Vladimir Lumelsky and Alexander Stepanov the bug algorithms in 1987. Lydia Kavraki, Petr Švestka, Jean-Claude Latombe and Mark Overmars named the probabilistic roadmap in 1996, and Steven LaValle introduced the RRT in 1998. Sertac Karaman and Emilio Frazzoli proved in 2011 that the RRT is not asymptotically optimal and gave RRT*; Kiril Solovey and coauthors found a gap in its optimality proof in 2020 and closed it with a larger connection radius.

## Further reading

- Malik Ghallab, Dana Nau and Paolo Traverso, *Automated Planning: Theory and Practice* (2004). The standard text on classical, plan-space, HTN, temporal and nondeterministic planning.
- Hector Geffner and Blai Bonet, *A Concise Introduction to Models and Methods for Automated Planning* (2013). A short account of the models from classical planning to POMDPs, and of the delete-relaxation heuristics.
- Stuart Russell and Peter Norvig, *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach*, fourth edition (2020). Chapter 11 is a first introduction to classical planning.
- Mausam and Andrey Kolobov, *Planning with Markov Decision Processes: An AI Perspective* (2012). Stochastic shortest paths, RTDP and heuristic search for MDPs.
- Steven M. LaValle, *Planning Algorithms* (2006). Configuration space, combinatorial and sampling-based motion planning, and differential constraints.
- Howie Choset and six coauthors, *Principles of Robot Motion* (2005). Bug algorithms, potential functions, roadmaps and cell decompositions, at the level of a first course.
