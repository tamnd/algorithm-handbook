## History

The value of a game came first. Zermelo showed in 1913 that in chess one side can force a win or both can force a draw, though his argument bounded the length of a forced win and was not yet backward induction; König and Kalmár extended it in the 1920s. Von Neumann proved the minimax theorem for mixed strategies in 1928, and his book with Morgenstern in 1944 made backward induction the standard method. Kuhn gave the extensive form with information sets in 1953, the model of the chapter, and in 1950 he had solved the small poker game named after him. On the combinatorial side, Bouton solved Nim with the binary sum in 1901–02, and Sprague (1935–36) and Grundy (1939) showed independently that every impartial game under normal play is a Nim heap.

Shannon's paper of 1950 on a chess program proposed what most programs still do: search to a fixed depth, score the positions there with an evaluation function, and back the scores up by minimax. Alpha-beta grew out of work by McCarthy (1956), Newell, Shaw and Simon (1958), and Hart and Edwards (1961); Brudno published a proof in 1963. Knuth and Moore's analysis of 1975 gave the leaf count with perfect ordering and showed that no algorithm can do better; Pearl found the expected behavior on random trees around 1980 and introduced SCOUT. Transposition tables appeared in Greenblatt's program of 1967 and Zobrist's hashing in 1970, and the narrow-window methods followed: principal variation search from Marsland and Campbell in the early 1980s, SSS* from Stockman in 1979, and MTD(f) from Plaat, Schaeffer, Pijls and de Bruin in 1996, who showed that SSS* is a sequence of null-window searches. Berliner named the horizon effect in 1973. Null-move pruning, late move reductions and futility pruning came from chess programs, and matches, not theorems, showed their worth.

Programs also began to solve games. Retrograde analysis of chess endgames goes back to Bellman in 1965 and Ströhlein in 1970, and Thompson built tablebases in the 1980s. Allis's thesis of 1994 introduced proof-number search and defined the three strengths of a solution, and Connect Four was solved in 1988 by Allen and by Allis. Nine men's morris was shown a draw by Gasser in 1996, awari by Romein and Bal in 2002, and checkers by Schaeffer's team in 2007, after a computation of 18 years. Nagai's df-pn of 2002 made proof-number search fit in bounded memory with a transposition table.

Go resisted alpha-beta, because no good evaluation function was known. Abramson in 1990 and Brügmann in 1993 scored positions by random playouts. In 2006 Coulom's Crazy Stone grew a search tree from playouts, and Kocsis and Szepesvári gave UCT, which chooses moves inside the tree by the UCB1 bandit rule; MoGo and RAVE followed. AlphaGo in 2016 combined tree search with learned policy and value networks and beat a top professional. For hidden information, Zinkevich, Johanson, Bowling and Piccione introduced counterfactual regret minimization in 2007, building on the regret matching of Hart and Mas-Colell, and variants of it solved heads-up limit hold'em in 2015.

## Further reading

- Donald E. Knuth and Ronald W. Moore, "An analysis of alpha-beta pruning", *Artificial Intelligence* 6 (1975), 293–326. The correctness of alpha-beta, the node types, and the best and worst cases, with the proofs.
- Stuart Russell and Peter Norvig, *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach*, 4th edition, Pearson, 2020. The chapter on adversarial search covers minimax, alpha-beta, evaluation functions, Monte Carlo tree search and games of chance.
- L. Victor Allis, *Searching for Solutions in Games and Artificial Intelligence*, PhD thesis, University of Limburg, 1994. Proof-number search, the levels of solving, and the solutions of several games.
- Cameron B. Browne and others, "A survey of Monte Carlo tree search methods", *IEEE Transactions on Computational Intelligence and AI in Games* 4 (2012), 1–43. The variants of MCTS and UCT up to 2012, with what is proved and what is measured.
- Elwyn R. Berlekamp, John H. Conway and Richard K. Guy, *Winning Ways for Your Mathematical Plays*, Academic Press, 1982; 2nd edition, A K Peters, 2001–2004. Nim, the Sprague-Grundy theory, octal games and the partisan theory, with many games worked out.
