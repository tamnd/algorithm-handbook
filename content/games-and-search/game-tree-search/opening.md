A program that plays a game has to choose a move against an opponent who wants the opposite outcome. The choice is a search through a [game tree](/game-tree/): the root is the position, each edge a legal move, each leaf an end of the game with its outcome. Tic-tac-toe has 255,168 complete games, few enough to search in full, and the search shows that the game is a draw. Chess has a tree estimated at $10^{123}$ leaves, so a chess program searches a few moves deep, scores the positions where it stops, and spends most of its effort on not searching the rest. Between the two lie games solved by large computations, such as checkers, a draw with perfect play, and games such as Go and poker, where programs beat people and nobody knows the value.

Most of the chapter works in one model: two players move in turn, see the whole position, meet no chance, and get opposite payoffs. There the [minimax](/minimax/) value is the right answer, and every exact method of the chapter returns it. The methods differ in how many leaves they read. On a uniform tree, [alpha-beta pruning](/alpha-beta-pruning/) reads all $b^d$ leaves in the worst case, and $b^{\lceil d/2 \rceil} + b^{\lfloor d/2 \rfloor} - 1$ when the best move comes first everywhere, which no algorithm can beat ([Knuth-Moore theorem](/knuth-moore-theorem/)); on random trees the expected count lies in between. So each cost here names its case: worst case, best case with perfect ordering, or expected under a stated random model.

A program that cannot reach the end of the game stops at a depth and calls an [evaluation function](/evaluation-function/). From then on much of what it does is measured in matches, not proved. Null-move pruning, late move reductions and futility pruning are forward pruning, and they can change the value the search returns. [Monte Carlo tree search](/monte-carlo-tree-search/) scores positions by random playouts and finds the minimax move only in the limit of many playouts. When the players hide information, as in poker, the answer is a mixed strategy, and [counterfactual regret minimization](/counterfactual-regret-minimization/) approaches an equilibrium of a two-player zero-sum game in its average strategy. The pages say which kind of claim each method has.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in ten groups, read in order.

1. **Games and their trees.** The game tree, the [branching factor](/branching-factor/), [perfect information](/perfect-information-game/) and [zero sum](/zero-sum-game/), [winning and losing states](/winning-losing-states/), [Zermelo's theorem](/zermelo-theorem/), and [game tree evaluation](/game-tree-evaluation/) as a problem of how many leaves to read.
2. **Minimax and alpha-beta.** Minimax and [negamax](/negamax/), [pruning](/pruning/), alpha-beta and the Knuth-Moore theorem, the [principal variation](/principal-variation/) and [best move selection](/best-move-selection/).
3. **Searching to a fixed depth.** The evaluation function, the [horizon effect](/horizon-effect/), [quiescence search](/quiescence-search/) and [search extensions](/search-extensions/).
4. **Move ordering and memory.** [Bitboards](/bitboard/), [move ordering](/move-ordering/), the [killer](/killer-heuristic/) and [history](/history-heuristic/) heuristics, and the [transposition table](/transposition-table/).
5. **Narrow windows and best-first search.** [Aspiration search](/aspiration-search/), [SCOUT](/scout-search/), [principal variation search](/principal-variation-search/), [MTD(f)](/mtdf/) and [SSS*](/sss-star/): the same value as alpha-beta, with the work done in another order.
6. **Forward pruning and parallel search.** [Null-move pruning](/null-move-pruning/), [futility pruning](/futility-pruning/), [late move reductions](/late-move-reductions/), [parallel alpha-beta](/parallel-alpha-beta/) and [Young Brothers Wait](/young-brothers-wait/).
7. **Proving and solving games.** [Proof-number search](/proof-number-search/), [df-pn](/df-pn/), [games on graphs](/games-on-graphs/), [retrograde analysis](/retrograde-analysis/), [endgame tablebases](/endgame-tablebase/), [endgame solving](/endgame-solving/), [opening books](/opening-book/) and [solved games](/solved-game/).
8. **Chance and sampling.** [Stochastic games](/stochastic-game/), [expectiminimax](/expectiminimax/), the [rollout algorithm](/rollout-algorithm/), Monte Carlo tree search, [UCT](/uct/) and [RAVE](/rapid-action-value-estimation/).
9. **Mixed strategies and hidden information.** The [minimax theorem](/minimax-theorem/), the [Nash equilibrium](/nash-equilibrium/), [Nash's existence theorem](/nash-existence-theorem/), [computing equilibria](/nash-equilibrium-computation/), [imperfect-information games](/imperfect-information-game/) and counterfactual regret minimization.
10. **Combinatorial games.** The [mex function](/mex-function/), [Nim](/nim/), [Bouton's theorem](/boutons-theorem/), [Grundy numbers](/grundy-number/), the [Sprague-Grundy theorem](/sprague-grundy-theorem/), [combinatorial game theory](/combinatorial-game-theory/), the [strategy-stealing argument](/strategy-stealing-argument/), [Hex](/hex-game/) and [generalized geography](/generalized-geography/).

## A first reading

Take these fifteen entries in order: game tree, zero-sum game, winning and losing states, minimax, negamax, alpha-beta pruning, the Knuth-Moore theorem, evaluation function, horizon effect, move ordering, transposition table, principal variation search, Monte Carlo tree search, the minimax theorem and Nim. They give the model, the exact search with its best and worst cases, what a depth-limited program adds, the tools that bring alpha-beta near its best case, the sampling method that took over in Go, the value of a game with mixed strategies, and the first game solved by a formula.

Leave for later the selective searches of groups 3 and 6, whose details differ from program to program, and SSS* and MTD(f). Come back to proof-number search and retrograde analysis when you want to solve a game rather than play it, and to the Sprague-Grundy theorem once Nim is familiar.

## Before you start

The searches are [depth-first search](/depth-first-search/) (Volume 7) on an implicit tree, run to growing depths by [iterative deepening](/iterative-deepening-search/) (Volume 2); retrograde analysis is a [breadth-first search](/breadth-first-search/) run backward. The transposition table is a [hash table](/hash-table/) (Volume 3) keyed by [Zobrist hashing](/zobrist-hashing/) (Volume 5), SSS* keeps a [priority queue](/priority-queue/), and bitboards and the XOR of Nim use [bitwise operations](/bitwise-operations/) (Volume 2). Proof-number search is a [best-first search](/best-first-search/). Monte Carlo tree search builds on the [multi-armed bandit](/multi-armed-bandit/) and [UCB1](/ucb1/) (Volume 25), and the rollout algorithm is stated for a [Markov decision process](/markov-decision-process/). The minimax theorem is [LP duality](/lp-duality/) (Volume 15), and counterfactual regret minimization builds on [regret](/regret/) (Volume 19). Generalized geography is shown PSPACE-complete by a reduction from [quantified Boolean formulas](/qbf/) (Volume 16). [Expected values](/expected-value/) come from Volume 1.
