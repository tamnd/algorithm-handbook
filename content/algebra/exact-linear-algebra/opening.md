Take the integer matrix with rows $(2, 1, 0)$, $(1, 3, 1)$ and $(0, 1, 4)$. Its determinant is $18$, and its inverse is $\tfrac{1}{18}$ times the integer matrix with rows $(11, -4, 1)$, $(-4, 8, -2)$ and $(1, -2, 5)$. A floating-point solver returns $0.6111\ldots$ for the first entry of the inverse and is close enough for most purposes. This chapter asks for the answer exactly: the integer $18$, the fraction $11/18$, the vector of bits, the polynomial with integer coefficients. Exact answers are what number theory, cryptography, combinatorics and computer algebra need. They are also what a contest problem asks for when it says "modulo $10^9 + 7$".

Exactness changes the problem. Over a finite field every number has a fixed size, and [Gaussian elimination](/gaussian-elimination/) is the whole story. Over the rationals elimination is still correct, but its numbers can grow, and the art is to keep them small: by dividing at the right moment, by working modulo many primes, or by lifting a solution modulo one prime. Over the integers there is no division at all, and the echelon form is replaced by the Hermite and Smith normal forms. For a sparse matrix with millions of rows, elimination fills the matrix in, and methods that only multiply the matrix by vectors take over.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in nine groups, read in order.

1. **Independence, rank and the nullspace.** The vocabulary: [linear independence](/linear-independence/), [rank](/rank/), the [nullspace](/nullspace/) and the [echelon form](/echelon-form/) that shows all three, tied together by the [rank-nullity theorem](/rank-nullity-theorem/).
2. **Determinants.** The [determinant](/determinant/) as a signed sum over permutations, [Laplace expansion](/laplace-expansion/) and [Cramer's rule](/cramers-rule/). These formulas prove things; they are far too slow to compute with.
3. **Gaussian elimination.** The central algorithm, the choice of [pivot](/pivot/), the [reduced row echelon form](/reduced-row-echelon-form/), and the [LU decomposition](/lu-decomposition/) that records the elimination for reuse.
4. **Determinant, rank, nullspace and inverse.** Each object becomes a computing problem, solved by elimination in $O(n^3)$ field operations and faster with fast matrix multiplication.
5. **Elimination over finite fields.** [Elimination modulo a prime](/gaussian-elimination-modular/), and over the field of two elements, where a row is a [bit set](/bit-set/) and adding rows is XOR.
6. **Integer matrices and the size of numbers.** The [Hadamard bound](/hadamard-bound/) on determinants, the fraction-free [Bareiss algorithm](/bareiss-algorithm/), the [multimodular determinant](/multimodular-determinant/) and [Dixon's p-adic lifting](/dixon-p-adic-lifting/).
7. **Integer normal forms.** [Unimodular matrices](/unimodular-matrix/), the [Hermite normal form](/hermite-normal-form/), the [Smith normal form](/smith-normal-form/), and how they solve [linear Diophantine systems](/linear-diophantine-system/).
8. **Characteristic and minimal polynomials.** The [characteristic polynomial](/characteristic-polynomial/), the [Cayley-Hamilton theorem](/cayley-hamilton-theorem/), the [minimal polynomial](/matrix-minimal-polynomial/), division-free and parallel algorithms for them, and the rational canonical and Jordan forms.
9. **Sparse and structured matrices.** The [black-box model](/black-box-matrix/), the [Wiedemann algorithm](/wiedemann-algorithm/) and its block relatives used in integer factoring, and fast solvers for [Toeplitz systems](/toeplitz-system-solving/).

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these twelve entries in order: [linear independence](/linear-independence/), [echelon form](/echelon-form/), [rank](/rank/), [nullspace](/nullspace/), [determinant](/determinant/), [Gaussian elimination](/gaussian-elimination/), [Gauss-Jordan elimination](/gauss-jordan-elimination/), [determinant by Gaussian elimination](/determinant-gaussian-elimination/), [LU decomposition](/lu-decomposition/), [Gaussian elimination for modular systems](/gaussian-elimination-modular/), [the Hadamard bound](/hadamard-bound/) and [the Bareiss algorithm](/bareiss-algorithm/). They give the algorithm that does most of the work in the chapter, over the three kinds of number it meets: rationals, residues modulo a prime, and integers. Add the [characteristic polynomial](/characteristic-polynomial/) and the [Cayley-Hamilton theorem](/cayley-hamilton-theorem/) when you reach the eighth group. The normal forms and the sparse methods can wait for a second pass.
