## History

Elimination is older than algebra in its modern form. Chapter 8 of the Chinese *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*, compiled about two thousand years ago, solves systems by operating on a table of coefficients. In Europe Newton described elimination for students, and Gauss used it in 1809 and after to solve the normal equations of least squares. Gabriel Cramer published his rule for any number of unknowns in 1750, in an appendix to a book on algebraic curves. The name Gaussian elimination and the matrix form of the method came only in the twentieth century; Joseph Grcar traced this history in 2011.

The theory of matrices grew in the nineteenth century. Charles Hermite reduced integer forms in 1851, and Henry Smith gave the diagonal form for systems of linear congruences in 1861. Arthur Cayley stated the Cayley-Hamilton theorem in his memoir of 1858, checking it only for small orders, and Ferdinand Georg Frobenius proved it in general in 1878, in the paper that introduced the minimal polynomial. Karl Weierstrass introduced elementary divisors in 1868, and Camille Jordan published his canonical form in 1870. Jacques Hadamard proved his bound on determinants in 1893. The astronomer Urbain Le Verrier had already computed characteristic coefficients from power sums in 1840.

Exact computation became a question of cost once computers arrived. Jack Edmonds showed in 1967 that elimination over the rationals runs in polynomial time, because its entries are quotients of minors, and Erwin Bareiss published fraction-free elimination in 1968. Modular methods followed: Borosh and Fraenkel solved integer systems by congruences in 1966, and John Dixon gave $p$-adic lifting with a full analysis in 1982. Ravindran Kannan and Achim Bachem gave the first polynomial-time algorithms for the Hermite and Smith normal forms in 1979. Paul Samuelson had found a bordering recurrence for the characteristic polynomial in 1942, and Stuart Berkowitz showed in 1984 that it needs no division and parallelizes well.

The sparse methods came from signal processing and from cryptography. Norman Levinson solved Toeplitz systems in quadratic time in 1947, and James Durbin adapted the method to autoregressive models in 1960. Douglas Wiedemann published his method for sparse systems over finite fields in 1986, motivated by discrete logarithms and factoring, and Brian LaMacchia and Andrew Odlyzko compared it in 1990 with structured elimination and with Lanczos and conjugate gradient methods. Don Coppersmith gave block versions of Lanczos in 1993 and of Wiedemann in 1994, and Peter Montgomery's block Lanczos of 1995 became the standard step of the number field sieve.

## Further reading

- Joachim von zur Gathen and Jürgen Gerhard, *Modern Computer Algebra*, third edition (2013). Covers modular determinants, rational reconstruction and Wiedemann's method with full cost analyses.
- Henri Cohen, *A Course in Computational Algebraic Number Theory* (1993). Chapter 2 gives tested algorithms for elimination, the Hermite and Smith normal forms and the characteristic polynomial over the integers.
- Alexander Schrijver, *Theory of Linear and Integer Programming* (1986). Explains the Hermite and Smith normal forms and linear Diophantine systems, with careful bounds on the size of numbers.
- Arne Storjohann, *Algorithms for Matrix Canonical Forms*, doctoral thesis, ETH Zurich (2000). Brings the cost of the echelon, Hermite, Smith and Frobenius forms close to that of matrix multiplication.
- Kenneth Hoffman and Ray Kunze, *Linear Algebra*, second edition (1971). A clear account of rank, determinants, the minimal polynomial and the rational and Jordan forms.
