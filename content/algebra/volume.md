Algebra in this volume means exact computation with algebraic objects: integers of any size, polynomials, matrices and symbolic expressions. The answers are exact. A product of two thousand-digit numbers has every digit right, a determinant of an integer matrix is an integer, and a factorization of a polynomial is a list of polynomials whose product is the input. Such computations sit under much of the rest of the handbook. Public-key cryptography multiplies and divides numbers of thousands of bits. Coding theory works with polynomials over finite fields. Signal processing and image compression run on the fast Fourier transform. A computer algebra system answers a question about a system of polynomial equations, or about an integral, with a formula and not a number.

The volume moves from the simplest objects to the most general. It starts with integers and polynomials and the four operations on them, then the transforms that make multiplication fast, then matrices: how to multiply them and how to solve linear systems exactly. It ends with computer algebra, where the objects are ideals, expressions, integrals and sums.

## Why algebra gets a volume of its own

The objects change from chapter to chapter, but a few ideas run through all of them.

- **Multiplication is the central operation.** Division, square roots, radix conversion, polynomial division and the evaluation of a polynomial at many points all reduce to a constant or logarithmic number of multiplications. The cost $M(n)$ of a multiplication sets the cost of nearly everything else, so the fast multiplication algorithms are the core of the volume.
- **Divide and conquer beats the school method.** [Karatsuba multiplication](/karatsuba-multiplication/) multiplies two halves with three products instead of four. [Strassen's algorithm](/strassen-algorithm/) multiplies $2 \times 2$ block matrices with seven products instead of eight. Both lower the exponent of the running time.
- **Change the representation.** A polynomial of degree less than $n$ is fixed by its values at $n$ points, and in that form two polynomials multiply point by point. The [evaluation-interpolation paradigm](/evaluation-interpolation-paradigm/) and the [fast Fourier transform](/fast-fourier-transform/) make the change of representation cheap, and give multiplication in $O(n \log n)$ operations.
- **Newton's method on exact objects.** The iteration that finds roots of real functions also computes reciprocals of integers and inverses of power series ([Newton iteration](/newton-iteration/)), and lifts a factorization modulo $p$ to one modulo $p^{2^k}$ ([Hensel lifting](/hensel-lifting/)). Each step doubles the number of correct digits.
- **Control the size of numbers.** Exact algorithms meet a cost that floating point hides: intermediate numbers can grow much larger than the input and the output ([expression swell](/expression-swell/)). Fraction-free methods such as the [Bareiss algorithm](/bareiss-algorithm/) keep them small, and [modular methods](/modular-methods/) avoid them by computing modulo many small primes and combining the results with the [Chinese remainder theorem](/chinese-remainder-theorem/).
- **Decision procedures.** Some questions that look like they need insight have algorithms. [Buchberger's algorithm](/buchberger-algorithm/) decides whether a polynomial lies in an ideal, and the [Risch algorithm](/risch-algorithm/) decides, for a large class of functions, whether a function has an antiderivative built from the same kind of functions.

## The chapters

**Chapter 11.1, Integer arithmetic**, stores a big integer as an array of machine words and builds the operations on it: addition with carries, multiplication from the schoolbook method through [Karatsuba](/karatsuba-multiplication/) and [Toom-Cook](/toom-cook-multiplication/) to the FFT methods, division by [Newton's method](/newton-raphson-division/), integer roots, radix conversion and [exponentiation](/integer-exponentiation/). A second thread follows the same ideas in hardware, from the [ripple-carry adder](/ripple-carry-adder/) to the [Wallace tree](/wallace-tree-multiplier/) multiplier.

**Chapter 11.2, Polynomial arithmetic**, does the same for polynomials with coefficients in a ring. It starts with [Horner's rule](/horner-rule/) and [polynomial long division](/polynomial-long-division/), goes on to the [polynomial gcd](/polynomial-gcd/), fast division by [power series inversion](/power-series-inversion/) and [multipoint evaluation](/multipoint-evaluation/), and ends with factorization over finite fields and over the integers, and with the isolation of real roots.

**Chapter 11.3, Fast transforms**, is about the [discrete Fourier transform](/discrete-fourier-transform/) and the [fast Fourier transform](/fast-fourier-transform/) that computes it in $O(n \log n)$ operations. It covers [convolution](/convolution/), the [number-theoretic transform](/number-theoretic-transform/) for exact work modulo a prime, the variants for lengths that are not powers of two, and the Walsh-Hadamard, cosine and wavelet transforms.

**Chapter 11.4, Matrix multiplication**, starts from the [naive algorithm](/naive-matrix-multiplication/) with $n^3$ multiplications and [Strassen's algorithm](/strassen-algorithm/) with $O(n^{\log_2 7})$. It covers [matrix powers](/matrix-exponentiation/), Boolean and min-plus products, and the blocked and cache-oblivious orders used in practice, and ends with the theory of the [exponent of matrix multiplication](/matrix-multiplication-exponent/), now known to be below 2.372.

**Chapter 11.5, Exact linear algebra**, solves linear systems and computes [determinants](/determinant/), ranks and inverses over the rationals and over finite fields. It starts with [Gaussian elimination](/gaussian-elimination/), then controls the growth of numbers with the Bareiss algorithm and [multimodular](/multimodular-determinant/) methods, computes normal forms of integer matrices such as the [Hermite](/hermite-normal-form/) and [Smith normal forms](/smith-normal-form/), and ends with the [Wiedemann algorithm](/wiedemann-algorithm/) for large sparse systems.

**Chapter 11.6, Computer algebra**, works with symbolic objects. It covers the representation of [expressions](/expression/), [Gröbner bases](/groebner-basis/) and the algorithms that compute them, the solution of polynomial systems, symbolic integration from [partial fractions](/partial-fraction-decomposition/) to the Risch algorithm, symbolic summation with [Gosper's](/gosper-algorithm/) and [Zeilberger's](/zeilberger-algorithm/) algorithms, and [quantifier elimination](/quantifier-elimination/) over the reals.

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course, read these entries in this order. From Chapter 11.1: [multiprecision integer](/multiprecision-integer/), [schoolbook multiplication](/schoolbook-multiplication/), [Karatsuba multiplication](/karatsuba-multiplication/), [long division](/long-division/) and [integer exponentiation](/integer-exponentiation/). From Chapter 11.2: [Horner's rule](/horner-rule/), [polynomial long division](/polynomial-long-division/) and the [polynomial Euclidean algorithm](/polynomial-euclidean-algorithm/). From Chapter 11.3: the [discrete Fourier transform](/discrete-fourier-transform/), the [fast Fourier transform](/fast-fourier-transform/) and [convolution via the FFT](/convolution-via-fft/), and then [FFT polynomial multiplication](/fft-polynomial-multiplication/) back in Chapter 11.2. From Chapter 11.4: [Strassen's algorithm](/strassen-algorithm/) and [matrix exponentiation](/matrix-exponentiation/). From Chapter 11.5: [Gaussian elimination](/gaussian-elimination/) and [Gaussian elimination modulo a prime](/gaussian-elimination-modular/). From Chapter 11.6: [Gröbner bases](/groebner-basis/) and [Buchberger's algorithm](/buchberger-algorithm/). Do the exercises marked ◦ and • at the end of each chapter you read. That is a complete first unit on exact computation.

If you already know the basics, start with the opening page of each chapter. It names the groups of entries in the chapter and the order to read them. The entries marked *Advanced* and *Research* in each chapter are the place to go deeper. The FFT methods of Chapters 11.1 and 11.2 use the transform of Chapter 11.3; read them after it, or take the transform on trust the first time.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained. Entries in other volumes are cited by number too, such as the [master theorem](/master-theorem/) in Volume 1 and the [extended Euclidean algorithm](/extended-euclidean-algorithm/) in Volume 10.

## What you need first

You should be able to read a short program and follow a proof by induction, and you should know complex numbers and the basic notions of linear algebra: matrices, linear systems, rank. From Volume 1 you need [asymptotic notation](/asymptotic-notation/), the [word RAM](/word-ram/) and the [master theorem](/master-theorem/). From Volume 2 you need the [divide-and-conquer paradigm](/divide-and-conquer-paradigm/). From Volume 10 you need [congruences](/congruence/), the [Euclidean algorithm](/euclidean-algorithm/), [modular inverses](/modular-inverse/) and the [Chinese remainder theorem](/chinese-remainder-theorem/). Rings, ideals and groups are defined in the chapters that use them. Chapter 11.6 and the factorization entries of Chapter 11.2 ask for more comfort with abstract algebra than the rest of the volume.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
