## History

The methods of school arithmetic are very old. The Rhind papyrus, of about 1650 BC, multiplies by doubling one factor and adding the doublings that match the binary digits of the other, the method now called [Russian peasant multiplication](/russian-peasant-multiplication/). Lattice multiplication is described by Ibn al-Banna in the Maghreb in the late thirteenth century and spread to Italy as *gelosia*. The Chinese *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art* extract square roots digit by digit, and Heron of Alexandria, in the first century, gave the iteration $x \leftarrow (x + N/x)/2$ that is Newton's method for the square root.

For a long time the quadratic cost of multiplication seemed to be a law. Andrey Kolmogorov conjectured in 1956, and again at his Moscow seminar in 1960, that $n$-digit multiplication needs on the order of $n^2$ operations. Anatoly Karatsuba, a student at the seminar, disproved it within about a week with his method of three half-size products, published in 1962 under the names of Karatsuba and Ofman. Andrei Toom generalized the idea to $k$ pieces in 1963, and Stephen Cook made it an algorithm in his Harvard thesis of 1966; the same thesis showed that division costs a constant number of multiplications, by Newton's method. Arnold Schönhage and Volker Strassen used the fast Fourier transform in 1971 to reach $O(N \log N \log \log N)$ and conjectured that $N \log N$ is the right order. The bound stood until Martin Fürer improved it in 2007, and David Harvey and Joris van der Hoeven reached $O(N \log N)$ in 2019, in a paper published in the *Annals of Mathematics* in 2021.

Practical division took its own path. Knuth gave the normalized long division, with the proof that the trial digit is at most two too large, as Algorithm D in the first edition of Volume 2 in 1969. Torbjörn Granlund and Peter Montgomery showed in 1994 how a compiler can replace every division by a constant with a multiplication, and Christoph Burnikel and Joachim Ziegler gave their recursive division in 1998; both are now in GMP. Daniel Bernstein's scaled remainder trees (2004) replaced the divisions of a remainder tree by multiplications. On the side of powers, Arnold Scholz posed his conjecture on addition chains in 1937, Alfred Brauer gave the $2^k$-ary method and proved the conjecture for star chains in 1939, and Edsger Dijkstra wrote up the doubling method for Fibonacci numbers in 1978.

The hardware thread began with the first electronic computers. Burks, Goldstine and von Neumann argued in their report of 1946 that, for random $n$-bit operands, the longest carry chain has an average length of at most about $\log_2 n$. Andrew Booth published his signed multiplication in 1951. The search for fast adders produced carry lookahead (Weinberger and Smith, 1958), conditional-sum addition (Sklansky, 1960), carry skip (Lehman and Burla, 1961) and carry select (Bedrij, 1962), and Ofman showed in 1962 that addition needs only depth $O(\log n)$ and size $O(n)$. Algirdas Avizienis studied signed-digit representations in 1961, C. S. Wallace proposed the multiplier tree in 1964, and Luigi Dadda refined it in 1965. Kogge and Stone (1973), Ladner and Fischer (1980) and Brent and Kung (1982) recast carry computation as a parallel prefix problem, the view from which the modern prefix adders come.

## Further reading

- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 2: Seminumerical Algorithms*, third edition (1997). Sections 4.1 to 4.4 and 4.6.3 cover number systems, the classical algorithms, fast multiplication, radix conversion and addition chains, with their history.
- Richard P. Brent and Paul Zimmermann, *Modern Computer Arithmetic* (2010). Chapter 1 is the best single account of how a library such as GMP multiplies, divides and takes roots, with the constants and thresholds that matter.
- Behrooz Parhami, *Computer Arithmetic: Algorithms and Hardware Designs*, second edition (2010). The standard textbook for the hardware groups: adders, multipliers, dividers and redundant number systems.
- Miloš D. Ercegovac and Tomás Lang, *Digital Arithmetic* (2004). A more advanced treatment of the same hardware, with digit-recurrence division and square root.
- Henry S. Warren, Jr., *Hacker's Delight*, second edition. Overflow tests, division by constants and other word-level tricks, worked out in detail.
