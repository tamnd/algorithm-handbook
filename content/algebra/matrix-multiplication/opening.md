The definition of the matrix product is also an algorithm: entry $(i, j)$ of $AB$ is the dot product of row $i$ of $A$ and column $j$ of $B$, and for two $n \times n$ matrices that is $n^3$ multiplications. For over a century nobody had reason to doubt that this was the right count. In 1969 Volker Strassen showed that two $2 \times 2$ matrices can be multiplied with seven multiplications instead of eight, and that applied to blocks this gives $O(n^{\lg 7})$, about $O(n^{2.807})$. The question of the true cost has been open ever since. It is written as the exponent $\omega$, the least number such that $n \times n$ matrices can be multiplied in $O(n^{\omega + \varepsilon})$ operations for every $\varepsilon > 0$. The best bounds known today are $2 \le \omega < 2.371552$.

The answer matters well beyond this chapter. Matrix inversion, determinants, solving linear systems, transitive closure, triangle detection and many graph problems run in $O(n^\omega)$ time, so every improvement to $\omega$ improves them too. But the chapter is not only about the exponent. It also covers the products that programs actually compute: powers, products over other semirings, sparse products, and products tuned for caches and for many processors.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups, read in order.

1. **The product and its cost.** [Naive matrix multiplication](/naive-matrix-multiplication/), the [matrix-vector product](/matrix-vector-multiplication/), and the [lower bound](/matrix-multiplication-quadratic-lower-bound/) of $n^2$ that comes from reading the input.
2. **Fewer multiplications.** [Divide and conquer](/divide-and-conquer-matrix-multiplication/), [Strassen's algorithm](/strassen-algorithm/), Winograd's inner product trick, Laderman's 23 products for $3 \times 3$ matrices, [the exponent $\omega$](/matrix-multiplication-exponent/), and the proof that inversion and multiplication have the same exponent.
3. **Powers.** [Matrix exponentiation](/matrix-exponentiation/) by repeated squaring, and its best-known use, [Fibonacci numbers by matrix powers](/matrix-fibonacci/).
4. **Other semirings.** [Boolean](/boolean-matrix-multiplication/) and [min-plus](/min-plus-matrix-multiplication/) products, for reachability and shortest paths.
5. **Sparse matrices.** Products whose cost depends on the number of nonzero entries.
6. **Memory and processors.** [Blocked](/blocked-matrix-multiplication/) and [cache-oblivious](/cache-oblivious-matrix-multiplication/) multiplication, which move the fewest words between cache and memory, and [Cannon's algorithm](/cannons-algorithm/) for a grid of processors.
7. **Tensors and bilinear algorithms.** The [Kronecker product](/kronecker-product/), the [matrix multiplication tensor](/matrix-multiplication-tensor/) and its [rank](/tensor-rank/), which counts the multiplications of the best bilinear algorithm, and [AlphaTensor](/alphatensor/), a machine search that found new small algorithms.
8. **Bounds on omega.** [Border rank](/border-rank/), [Schönhage's asymptotic sum inequality](/schonhage-asymptotic-sum-inequality/), the [laser method](/laser-method/) and the [Coppersmith-Winograd algorithm](/coppersmith-winograd-algorithm/): the tools behind every bound on $\omega$ since 1979.
