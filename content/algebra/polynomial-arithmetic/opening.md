A polynomial, for an algorithm, is a list of coefficients. Adding two lists is easy. Multiplying them is a convolution, the same sum that a digital filter computes and that sits inside the multiplication of large integers. Dividing, taking a gcd, evaluating at many points, factoring: each is a short program on these lists, and each has a fast version that does far less work than the obvious one. Error-correcting codes such as Reed-Solomon codes evaluate and interpolate polynomials over finite fields. Computer algebra systems factor polynomials to simplify expressions and solve equations. Lattice-based cryptography multiplies polynomials in $\mathbb{F}_q[x]/(x^n + 1)$.

The chapter has one idea running through it. Once multiplication is fast, almost everything else is fast too. Division, the gcd, evaluation at $n$ points and interpolation through $n$ points all cost a constant or a logarithmic factor times $M(n)$, the cost of one product of polynomials with $n$ coefficients. With the FFT, $M(n) = O(n \log n)$ over rings with suitable roots of unity, so all these problems run in nearly linear time.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in nine groups, read in order.

1. **Polynomials and how to store them.** The [polynomial ring](/polynomial-ring/), the [degree](/polynomial-degree/), [monic](/monic-polynomial/) and [irreducible](/irreducible-polynomial/) polynomials, and the [dense](/dense-polynomial-representation/) and [sparse](/sparse-polynomial-representation/) representations.
2. **Evaluation and roots.** [Horner's rule](/horner-rule/) evaluates with $n$ multiplications and $n$ additions. The [factor theorem](/factor-theorem/) links roots to linear factors, and [Estrin's scheme](/estrin-scheme/) and the [Paterson-Stockmeyer algorithm](/paterson-stockmeyer-algorithm/) adapt evaluation to parallel hardware and to expensive points.
3. **Multiplication.** The [problem](/polynomial-multiplication/), the [point-value representation](/point-value-representation/), the [evaluation-interpolation paradigm](/evaluation-interpolation-paradigm/) and [FFT multiplication](/fft-polynomial-multiplication/) in $O(n \log n)$, with [Kronecker substitution](/kronecker-substitution/) for integer coefficients and a heap-based method for sparse inputs.
4. **Division and Newton iteration.** [Long division](/polynomial-long-division/) in quadratic time, then [Newton iteration](/newton-iteration/) for [power series inversion](/power-series-inversion/), which gives [fast division](/fast-polynomial-division/) in $O(M(n))$.
5. **Many points, composition and recurrences.** The [subproduct tree](/subproduct-tree/) gives [fast multipoint evaluation](/fast-multipoint-evaluation/) and [fast interpolation](/fast-interpolation/) in $O(M(n) \log n)$. Then [composition](/polynomial-composition/) and the [$N$-th term of a linear recurrence](/linear-recurrence-nth-term/).
6. **Greatest common divisors and resultants.** The [polynomial Euclidean algorithm](/polynomial-euclidean-algorithm/) and its [extended form](/polynomial-extended-euclidean-algorithm/), the [resultant](/resultant/) and the [discriminant](/discriminant/), and the [subresultant](/subresultant-gcd/) and [modular](/brown-modular-gcd/) algorithms that keep integer coefficients small.
7. **Factoring over finite fields.** [Square-free factorization](/square-free-factorization/), [Rabin's irreducibility test](/rabin-irreducibility-test/), and the [Cantor-Zassenhaus](/cantor-zassenhaus-algorithm/) and [Berlekamp](/berlekamp-factorization/) algorithms.
8. **Factoring over the integers.** [Eisenstein's criterion](/eisenstein-criterion/), [Hensel lifting](/hensel-lifting/) and the [Zassenhaus algorithm](/zassenhaus-algorithm/).
9. **Real roots.** [Descartes' rule of signs](/descartes-rule-of-signs/), [Sturm's theorem](/sturm-theorem/) and [real root isolation](/real-root-isolation/) by the [Vincent-Collins-Akritas](/vincent-collins-akritas/) method.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these fourteen entries in order: [polynomial ring](/polynomial-ring/), [dense representation](/dense-polynomial-representation/), [Horner's rule](/horner-rule/), [factor theorem](/factor-theorem/), [polynomial multiplication](/polynomial-multiplication/), [point-value representation](/point-value-representation/), [FFT multiplication](/fft-polynomial-multiplication/), [polynomial long division](/polynomial-long-division/), [Newton power series inversion](/newton-power-series-inversion/), [fast polynomial division](/fast-polynomial-division/), [polynomial Euclidean algorithm](/polynomial-euclidean-algorithm/), [square-free factorization](/square-free-factorization/), [Cantor-Zassenhaus algorithm](/cantor-zassenhaus-algorithm/) and [Sturm's theorem](/sturm-theorem/). They give the classical methods, the step from quadratic to nearly linear time, and one complete factoring algorithm.

On a first pass you can skip the groups on integer coefficients (the subresultant and modular gcds, Hensel lifting and the Zassenhaus algorithm) and the algorithms whose point is a constant factor or a special setting, such as Estrin's scheme, Brent-Kung composition and Vincent's theorem. Come back to them when you need exact arithmetic over $\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{Q}$, where the size of the coefficients, not the number of operations, decides the running time.

## Before you start

You need [asymptotic notation](/asymptotic-notation/) and [loop invariants](/loop-invariant/) from Volume 1. From Volume 10 you need the integer [gcd](/greatest-common-divisor/), the [Euclidean algorithm](/euclidean-algorithm/) and its [extended form](/extended-euclidean-algorithm/), the [Chinese remainder theorem](/chinese-remainder-theorem/), [binary exponentiation](/binary-exponentiation/) and [Fermat's little theorem](/fermat-little-theorem/); the polynomial algorithms copy the integer ones closely. FFT multiplication uses the [fast Fourier transform](/fast-fourier-transform/) and [roots of unity](/roots-of-unity/) from Chapter 11.3, and Kronecker substitution uses [integer multiplication](/integer-multiplication/) from Chapter 11.1. The resultant is a [determinant](/determinant/), and Berlekamp's algorithm finds a [null space](/nullspace/), both from Chapter 11.5. Some comfort with rings and fields, at the level of knowing what $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is, is assumed throughout.
