## History

The classical methods are old. Qin Jiushao used the scheme now called Horner's rule in his *Mathematical Treatise in Nine Sections* of 1247, to solve equations numerically; Paolo Ruffini described it in 1804 and William Horner in 1819. Simon Stevin applied Euclid's algorithm to polynomials in 1585. René Descartes stated his rule of signs in 1637. Charles Sturm found his sequences in 1829, and Alexander Vincent proved in 1836 the theorem on continued fractions that real root isolation still uses.

The nineteenth century added the tools for integer coefficients. Theodor Schönemann in 1846 and Gotthold Eisenstein in 1850 gave the irreducibility criterion that bears Eisenstein's name. J. J. Sylvester defined the resultant as a determinant in 1840, and Kurt Hensel developed $p$-adic numbers, and with them the lifting lemma, around 1900.

The fast algorithms came with computers. After James Cooley and John Tukey published the FFT in 1965, polynomial products in $O(n \log n)$ followed at once. Arnold Schönhage and Volker Strassen gave multiplication over any ring in 1971, and David Cantor and Erich Kaltofen extended it to all algebras in 1991. Malte Sieveking in 1972 and H. T. Kung in 1974 used Newton iteration for power series inversion, and with it division in $O(M(n))$. Charles Fiduccia, and Robert Moenck and Allan Borodin, gave fast multipoint evaluation in 1972, and Strassen showed in 1973 that $\Omega(n \log n)$ operations are needed for it. Michael Paterson and Larry Stockmeyer gave their evaluation scheme in 1973, and Richard Brent and Kung their composition algorithm in 1978. Fiduccia's 1985 method for the $N$-th term of a recurrence was simplified by Alin Bostan and Ryuhei Mori in 2021.

Gcds and factoring were the core problems of early computer algebra. George Collins introduced subresultants in 1967, and W. S. Brown the modular gcd in 1971. Elwyn Berlekamp gave the first practical factoring algorithm over finite fields in 1967, Hans Zassenhaus used Hensel lifting to factor over the integers in 1969, and Cantor and Zassenhaus gave their randomized method in 1981. Michael Rabin's irreducibility test appeared in 1980, and David Yun's square-free factorization in 1976. The same year Collins and Alkiviadis Akritas turned Vincent's theorem into an algorithm. The LLL algorithm of 1982 made factoring over $\mathbb{Q}$ polynomial-time, and Mark van Hoeij's knapsack method of 2002 made it fast in practice. Kiran Kedlaya and Christopher Umans showed in 2008 that modular composition takes nearly linear time in a bit model.

## Further reading

- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 2: Seminumerical Algorithms*, 3rd edition, Addison-Wesley, 1997. Section 4.6 covers polynomial arithmetic, gcds, factoring and evaluation, with many exercises and much history.
- Joachim von zur Gathen and Jürgen Gerhard, *Modern Computer Algebra*, 3rd edition, Cambridge University Press, 2013. The standard text on fast polynomial algorithms, from multiplication to factoring over finite fields and over the integers.
- Victor Shoup, *A Computational Introduction to Number Theory and Algebra*, 2nd edition, Cambridge University Press, 2009. A careful account of polynomials over finite fields, with complete proofs of the factoring algorithms.
- Keith O. Geddes, Stephen R. Czapor and George Labahn, *Algorithms for Computer Algebra*, Kluwer, 1992. Good on subresultants, modular gcds and Hensel lifting.
- Richard P. Brent and Paul Zimmermann, *Modern Computer Arithmetic*, Cambridge University Press, 2010. Newton iteration and fast multiplication from the point of view of an implementer.
- Saugata Basu, Richard Pollack and Marie-Françoise Roy, *Algorithms in Real Algebraic Geometry*, 2nd edition, Springer, 2006. Sturm sequences, subresultants and real root isolation in depth.
