## History

The first programs that worked with formulas were written in 1953, when H. G. Kahrimanian and J. Nolan each wrote one to differentiate expressions. John McCarthy designed LISP around 1960 partly with this kind of work in mind, and the large systems of the next two decades were built on it or near it: MACSYMA at MIT, REDUCE, and later Maple and Mathematica. Daniel Richardson proved in 1968 that zero-equivalence is undecidable once $\pi$, $\sin$ and $|\cdot|$ are allowed, which marked the limit that every simplifier still works within.

The growth of coefficients was the first practical obstacle. James Sylvester had defined his matrix in 1840, and George Collins turned it into the subresultant remainder sequence in 1967; W. S. Brown and Joseph Traub gave the general theory in 1971. Brown's modular gcd of the same year, built on the Chinese remainder theorem, made the size of the answer rather than the size of the intermediate results set the cost. Bruce Char, Keith Geddes and Gaston Gonnet introduced the heuristic gcd for Maple in 1984. Kronecker's factorization method of 1882 gave way to Elwyn Berlekamp's factoring modulo a prime in 1967, Hans Zassenhaus's lifting in 1969, and the lattice method of Lenstra, Lenstra and Lovász in 1982. Richard Zippel's sparse interpolation of 1979 and the method of Michael Ben-Or and Prasoon Tiwari of 1988 made it practical to recover polynomials with few terms.

Bruno Buchberger defined Gröbner bases and gave his algorithm in his 1965 thesis at Innsbruck, written under Wolfgang Gröbner, and added his pair criteria in 1979. The foundations were older: Hilbert's basis theorem of 1890 and his Nullstellensatz of 1893. Ernst Mayr and Albert Meyer showed in 1982 that ideal membership can need doubly exponential degree. Daniel Lazard related Gröbner bases to the matrices of Francis Macaulay in 1983, and that link led to Jean-Charles Faugère's F4 in 1999 and F5 in 2002. FGLM, by Faugère, Gianni, Lazard and Mora, appeared in 1993, and the Gröbner walk followed in 1997. Wu Wenjun's characteristic sets, from 1977, offered another route through Ritt's differential algebra, and Shang-Ching Chou used them to prove 512 geometry theorems by machine in 1988.

Alfred Tarski proved around 1930 that the theory of real closed fields admits quantifier elimination, but his procedure, published in 1948 and 1951, was far too slow to run. Abraham Seidenberg gave another proof in 1954. George Collins introduced cylindrical algebraic decomposition in 1973 and published it in 1975, and James Davenport and Joos Heintz showed in 1988 that its doubly exponential cost cannot be avoided in general.

Integration in finite terms begins with Joseph Liouville, in papers from 1833 to 1841. Robert Risch turned the theory into an algorithm in 1969 and 1970, and Joel Moses implemented large parts of it in MACSYMA. Michael Rothstein and Barry Trager each found the resultant for the logarithmic part in 1976, and Daniel Lazard and Renaud Rioboo removed its algebraic extensions in 1990. Manuel Bronstein's book of 1997 gave the complete transcendental case. For sums, R. William Gosper published his algorithm in 1978, Doron Zeilberger turned Sister Mary Celine Fasenmyer's method of the 1940s into creative telescoping in 1990, and Marko Petkovšek gave Hyper in 1992.

## Further reading

- David Cox, John Little and Donal O'Shea, *Ideals, Varieties, and Algorithms*, fourth edition, Springer, 2015: the standard first text on Gröbner bases, elimination and the Nullstellensatz.
- Joachim von zur Gathen and Jürgen Gerhard, *Modern Computer Algebra*, third edition, Cambridge University Press, 2013: complete proofs and costs for gcds, modular methods, factoring and symbolic summation.
- Keith Geddes, Stephen Czapor and George Labahn, *Algorithms for Computer Algebra*, Kluwer, 1992: the algorithms of a working system, from the heuristic gcd to the Risch algorithm.
- Manuel Bronstein, *Symbolic Integration I: Transcendental Functions*, second edition, Springer, 2005: the full theory and algorithms behind Hermite reduction, Rothstein-Trager and Risch.
- Saugata Basu, Richard Pollack and Marie-Françoise Roy, *Algorithms in Real Algebraic Geometry*, second edition, Springer, 2006: cylindrical algebraic decomposition, quantifier elimination and their costs.
- Marko Petkovšek, Herbert Wilf and Doron Zeilberger, *A = B*, A K Peters, 1996: Gosper, Zeilberger, WZ pairs and Hyper, with many worked identities.
