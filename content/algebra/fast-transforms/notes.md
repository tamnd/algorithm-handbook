## History

Carl Friedrich Gauss used a fast method for trigonometric interpolation around 1805, to fit the orbits of the asteroids Pallas and Juno. He split a sum of length $n = n_1 n_2$ into shorter sums, which is the idea of the Cooley-Tukey algorithm, but the work stayed in his notebooks and appeared only in his collected works in 1866, in Latin. The idea was found again several times: by Carl Runge in 1903 and 1905, by Gordon Danielson and Cornelius Lanczos in 1942, and by I. J. Good in 1958, whose method for coprime factors became the prime-factor algorithm. Gerald Goertzel gave his recurrence for single coefficients in the same year.

The method took hold in 1965, when James Cooley and John Tukey published *An algorithm for the machine calculation of complex Fourier series*. Computers had become fast enough for long transforms to matter, and within a few years the field filled in. W. Morven Gentleman and Gordon Sande gave the decimation-in-frequency form in 1966, and Thomas Stockham showed in the same year how to convolve fast with the FFT. Charles Rader gave his algorithm for prime lengths in 1968, and R. Yavne the operation count that split radix later reached. Leo Bluestein presented the chirp method in 1968, and Lawrence Rabiner, Ronald Schafer and Rader turned it into the chirp z-transform in 1969. Richard Singleton published a widely used mixed-radix program in 1969, and Cooley, Peter Lewis and Peter Welch showed how to transform real data at half the cost in 1970.

Exact transforms came next. John Pollard described the FFT over finite fields in 1971, and Arnold Schönhage and Volker Strassen used it in the same year to multiply integers in $O(n \log n \log\log n)$ bit operations. Ramesh Agarwal and C. Sidney Burrus studied number-theoretic transforms for digital filtering in 1974 and 1975. On the side of lower bounds, Jacques Morgenstern proved in 1973 that a linear algorithm with bounded constants needs $\Omega(n \log n)$ operations. No bound above $\Omega(n)$ is known for general circuits.

The real transforms have their own line. Nasir Ahmed, T. Natarajan and K. R. Rao introduced the discrete cosine transform in 1974, and John Makhoul gave a fast algorithm through the FFT in 1980. Ronald Bracewell published the fast Hartley transform in 1984. Shmuel Winograd's work of 1976 and 1978 nested minimal-multiplication DFTs of small lengths into larger ones. Pierre Duhamel and H. Hollmann made split radix known in 1984, and Steven Johnson and Matteo Frigo lowered the count to $\frac{34}{9} n \lg n + O(n)$ in 2007. Michael Heideman, Don Johnson and Burrus traced the history back to Gauss in a paper of 1984.

The other transforms in the chapter are older or newer. Alfréd Haar defined his wavelet basis in 1910, James Sylvester his matrices in 1867, Jacques Hadamard his in 1893 and Joseph Walsh his functions in 1923. Stéphane Mallat gave the fast wavelet transform in 1989. The nonuniform FFT comes from Alok Dutt and Vladimir Rokhlin in 1993 and Gregory Beylkin in 1995 and was made faster by Leslie Greengard and June-Yub Lee in 2004. Sparse Fourier methods go back to Eyal Kushilevitz and Yishay Mansour in 1993; Haitham Hassanieh, Piotr Indyk, Dina Katabi and Eric Price gave the sparse FFT of 2012.

## Further reading

- Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest and Clifford Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, fourth edition (2022). Chapter 30 covers the DFT, the FFT, polynomial multiplication and the parallel circuit, with the opposite sign convention.
- Joachim von zur Gathen and Jürgen Gerhard, *Modern Computer Algebra*, third edition (2013). Chapter 8 treats the FFT over rings and its use for fast multiplication.
- Charles Van Loan, *Computational Frameworks for the Fast Fourier Transform* (SIAM, 1992). Writes every FFT variant as a factorization of the DFT matrix.
- Nicholas J. Higham, *Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms*, second edition (2002). Chapter 24 proves the $O(u \log n)$ error bound of the FFT.
- Michael T. Heideman, Don H. Johnson and C. Sidney Burrus, *Gauss and the History of the Fast Fourier Transform*, IEEE ASSP Magazine 1(4), 1984.
