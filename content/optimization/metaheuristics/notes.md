## History

The oldest idea in the chapter comes from physics. In 1953 Nicholas Metropolis and his coauthors gave the acceptance rule that samples the equilibrium states of a system of particles. Thirty years later Scott Kirkpatrick, C. Daniel Gelatt and Mario Vecchi turned it into an optimization method, simulated annealing, in a paper in *Science* (1983); Vladimír Černý published the same idea for the traveling salesman problem in 1985. Bruce Hajek's paper *Cooling Schedules for Optimal Annealing* (1988) gave the exact condition for convergence. Gunter Dueck and Tobias Scheuer replaced the random test by a threshold in 1990, and Dueck gave the great deluge in 1993. Edmund Burke and Yuri Bykov proposed late acceptance in 2008.

Evolutionary methods grew in two places at once in the 1960s. At the Technical University of Berlin, Ingo Rechenberg, Hans-Paul Schwefel and Peter Bienert started evolution strategies, first on physical experiments such as the shape of a plate in a wind tunnel, and Rechenberg gave the 1/5 success rule in 1973. At the University of Michigan, John Holland developed the genetic algorithm and set it out, with the schema analysis, in *Adaptation in Natural and Artificial Systems* (1975). David Goldberg's book of 1989 made genetic algorithms widely known and stated the building block hypothesis. John Koza's *Genetic Programming* (1992) evolved programs, after work by Nichael Cramer in 1985. Rainer Storn and Kenneth Price introduced differential evolution in 1995, and Nikolaus Hansen and Andreas Ostermeier published CMA-ES in its standard form in 2001.

The word came from Fred Glover. His 1986 paper on integer programming and artificial intelligence introduced tabu search and called it a "meta-heuristic", a strategy placed on top of another heuristic. The years after brought many such strategies built around local search: GRASP from Thomas Feo and Mauricio Resende (1989, named in 1995), the large-step Markov chains of Olivier Martin, Steve Otto and Edward Felten (1991) that became iterated local search, variable neighborhood search from Nenad Mladenović and Pierre Hansen (1997), and guided local search from Christos Voudouris and Edward Tsang. Pablo Moscato named memetic algorithms in 1989.

Swarm methods came next. Marco Dorigo, Vittorio Maniezzo and Alberto Colorni proposed the Ant System in 1991, and Thomas Stützle and Holger Hoos gave MAX-MIN Ant System in 1997. James Kennedy and Russell Eberhart introduced particle swarm optimization in 1995. After 2000 a stream of methods with new metaphors followed: harmony search, the artificial bee colony, the firefly, cuckoo and bat algorithms. Kenneth Sörensen's paper *Metaheuristics—the Metaphor Exposed* (2015) argued that such metaphors often hide familiar operators, and Christian Camacho-Villalón, Marco Dorigo and Thomas Stützle showed in detail that several of these methods are variants of particle swarm optimization, evolution strategies and differential evolution.

The theory is younger than the methods. David Wolpert and William Macready proved the no free lunch theorem in 1997, and Christopher Schumacher, Michael Vose and Darrell Whitley sharpened it in 2001. Rigorous run-time analysis began in the 1990s with simple algorithms such as the $(1+1)$ EA; Stefan Droste, Thomas Jansen and Ingo Wegener proved the $\Theta(n \log n)$ bound for linear functions in 2002.

## Further reading

- Holger H. Hoos and Thomas Stützle, *Stochastic Local Search: Foundations and Applications* (2004). The best single account of local search methods, with careful definitions and a method for measuring them.
- Michel Gendreau and Jean-Yves Potvin (editors), *Handbook of Metaheuristics*, third edition (2019). One chapter per method, each by an author of the method or a leading user.
- A. E. Eiben and J. E. Smith, *Introduction to Evolutionary Computing*, second edition (2015). A textbook on genetic algorithms, evolution strategies and their parts, with advice on parameters and experiments.
- Fred Glover and Manuel Laguna, *Tabu Search* (1997). The standard book on tabu search, its memories and path relinking.
- Thomas Jansen, *Analyzing Evolutionary Algorithms: The Computer Science Perspective* (2013). How to prove run-time bounds for evolutionary algorithms.
- Kenneth Sörensen, *Metaheuristics—the Metaphor Exposed*, International Transactions in Operational Research 22(1), 2015. A short critique of methods described by metaphors instead of by their operators.
