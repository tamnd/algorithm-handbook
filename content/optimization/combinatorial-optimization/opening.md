A combinatorial optimization problem has a finite set of feasible solutions and a cost for each one, and it asks for the cheapest. A parcel company chooses the order in which a van visits its stops. A chip designer splits a circuit into two halves with few wires between them. A planner picks projects whose total cost fits a budget and whose total value is as large as possible. Each problem is finite, so in principle you can try every solution. In practice the number of solutions grows too fast. A tour through 20 cities can be chosen in $19!/2 \approx 6.1 \times 10^{16}$ ways, which would take about 1.9 years at $10^9$ tours per second, while a dynamic program over subsets needs only about $4.2 \times 10^8$ steps.

That contrast is the subject of the chapter. Most of its problems are NP-hard, so no method is known that is both exact and fast on every input. What remains is a set of tools: exact methods that are fast when a parameter is small, bounds that prove how good a solution is, heuristics that work well in practice without a guarantee, and a few structures, matroids and submodular functions, under which greedy and related methods are provably optimal.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in six groups, read in order.

1. **The problems and the knapsack.** What a [combinatorial optimization problem](/combinatorial-optimization-problem/) is, and the [knapsack problem](/knapsack-problem/) as a first example. The [greedy fractional knapsack](/greedy-fractional-knapsack/) solves the relaxation in $\Theta(n \log n)$ time, and [knapsack dynamic programming](/knapsack-dynamic-programming/) solves the 0-1 problem in $\Theta(nW)$ time.
2. **Tours and routes.** The [traveling salesman problem](/traveling-salesman-problem/) and the [vehicle routing problem](/vehicle-routing-problem/), with the [nearest neighbor heuristic](/nearest-neighbor-heuristic/) for tours and [Clarke-Wright savings](/clarke-wright-savings/) for routes.
3. **Splitting graphs.** The [graph partitioning problem](/graph-partitioning-problem/) and its two classic local search methods, [Kernighan-Lin](/kernighan-lin/) and [Fiduccia-Mattheyses](/fiduccia-mattheyses/).
4. **Placing facilities.** [Facility location](/facility-location-problem/), the [quadratic assignment problem](/quadratic-assignment-problem/), and [competitive facility location](/competitive-facility-location/), a game on a graph.
5. **Matroids and submodular functions.** The [Rado-Edmonds theorem](/rado-edmonds-theorem/) on when greedy is optimal, [submodular functions](/submodular-function/) and their [minimization](/submodular-minimization/), and [matroid intersection](/matroid-intersection/) with its [min-max theorem](/matroid-intersection-theorem/) and [algorithm](/matroid-intersection-algorithm/).
6. **General methods for hard problems.** [Lagrangian relaxation](/lagrangian-relaxation/) for bounds, and [large neighborhood search](/large-neighborhood-search/) for good solutions.

The groups on problems come first because the later methods are easier to follow once you have the problems in mind. Each problem entry has a small worked instance, and several instances come back in later entries: the knapsack with capacity 10 and four items appears in the knapsack, greedy and dynamic programming entries, and the graph of two triangles appears in the partitioning entries.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these thirteen entries in order: [combinatorial optimization problem](/combinatorial-optimization-problem/), [knapsack problem](/knapsack-problem/), [greedy fractional knapsack](/greedy-fractional-knapsack/), [knapsack dynamic programming](/knapsack-dynamic-programming/), [traveling salesman problem](/traveling-salesman-problem/), [nearest neighbor heuristic](/nearest-neighbor-heuristic/), [vehicle routing problem](/vehicle-routing-problem/), [Clarke-Wright savings](/clarke-wright-savings/), [graph partitioning problem](/graph-partitioning-problem/), [Kernighan-Lin](/kernighan-lin/), [facility location problem](/facility-location-problem/), [Rado-Edmonds theorem](/rado-edmonds-theorem/) and [submodular function](/submodular-function/). They are marked *Intro* or *Core*. The rest of the chapter is *Advanced*. Skip the matroid intersection entries, submodular minimization and the quadratic assignment problem at first. Read Lagrangian relaxation after the duality entries of Chapters 15.1 and 15.3, and large neighborhood search after the local search entries of Chapter 15.6.

## Before you start

From Volume 2 you need the [greedy algorithm](/greedy-algorithm/), the [fractional knapsack](/fractional-knapsack/), [matroids](/matroid/) and the [matroid greedy algorithm](/matroid-greedy-algorithm/), and the dynamic programming ideas of [optimal substructure](/optimal-substructure/) and [tabulation](/tabulation/). From Volume 7 you need [graphs](/graph/), [cuts](/cut/), [minimum spanning trees](/minimum-spanning-tree/), [Hamiltonian cycles](/hamiltonian-cycle/) and the [assignment problem](/assignment-problem/). The routing and location entries use [bin packing](/bin-packing/) and [set cover](/set-cover/) from Volume 18, and Fiduccia-Mattheyses uses the [bucket queue](/bucket-queue/) from Volume 3. From this volume, the bounds use [LP duality](/lp-duality/) and the [LP relaxation](/lp-relaxation/), and Lagrangian relaxation uses [Lagrangian duality](/lagrangian-duality/) and [subgradients](/subgradient/). Throughout, you should know what it means for a problem to be NP-hard, and how a [reduction](/reduction/) transfers hardness from one problem to another.
