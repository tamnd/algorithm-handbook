## History

The oldest method in the chapter is older than the problem: Joseph Fourier described the elimination of variables from a system of linear inequalities in 1826, and Theodore Motzkin found it again in his thesis of 1936. Gyula Farkas published versions of his lemma from 1894 on, in work on the equilibrium of mechanical systems, and gave the full proof in 1902. Leonid Kantorovich wrote down linear models of production planning in 1939, and Frank Hitchcock stated the transportation problem in 1941.

The subject as it is now began in 1947. George Dantzig, working on planning problems for the US Air Force, designed the simplex method that year and published it in 1951, in the Cowles Commission volume *Activity Analysis of Production and Allocation* edited by Tjalling Koopmans. In October 1947 Dantzig described the problem to John von Neumann, who saw at once its link to his theory of zero-sum games and stated the duality theorem in an unpublished note. David Gale, Harold Kuhn and Albert Tucker published the first proof of duality in the same 1951 volume. Within a few years Dantzig and William Orchard-Hays built the revised simplex method at RAND around 1953 for machines with little memory; Carlton Lemke published the dual simplex method in 1954; Alan Hoffman (1953) and E. M. L. Beale (1955) found programs on which the method cycles, and Abraham Charnes, and Dantzig, Alex Orden and Philip Wolfe, gave perturbation and lexicographic rules that prevent it. Dantzig and Wolfe published their decomposition in 1960, and Paul Gilmore and Ralph Gomory used column generation for cutting stock in 1961.

The question of speed came next. Warren Hirsch asked Dantzig in 1957 how few pivots could always suffice, which became the Hirsch conjecture on the diameter of polytopes; Francisco Santos disproved it in 2010 with a polytope of dimension 43. Victor Klee and George Minty showed in 1972 that Dantzig's rule can visit all $2^n$ vertices of a squashed cube; bad examples for the other common rules followed. Robert Bland found his least-index rule, with its short proof that it never cycles, in 1977. Karl Heinz Borgwardt (1977, published in 1982) and Steve Smale (1983) gave polynomial average-case bounds for random models, and Daniel Spielman and Shang-Hua Teng presented the smoothed analysis of the simplex method in 2001, with the journal version in 2004.

Polynomial algorithms came from outside the simplex tradition. Naum Shor, and David Yudin and Arkadi Nemirovski, developed the ellipsoid method for convex optimization in the 1970s. Leonid Khachiyan used it in 1979 to show that linear programming is in P, and Martin Grötschel, László Lovász and Alexander Schrijver showed in 1981 that it needs only a separation oracle. Narendra Karmarkar's projective method of 1984, the first polynomial method claimed to be fast in practice, started the interior-point methods; I. I. Dikin's affine scaling method of 1967 was found again soon after as a simpler form of it. In fixed dimension, Nimrod Megiddo (1983, 1984) and Martin Dyer (1984) gave linear-time algorithms, and Raimund Seidel gave his short randomized one in 1990. Whether a strongly polynomial algorithm for linear programming exists is still open.

## Further reading

- Vašek Chvátal, *Linear Programming* (1983). A clear account of the simplex method with dictionaries, its pitfalls and its many applications; the best book to learn the method by hand.
- Dimitris Bertsimas and John N. Tsitsiklis, *Introduction to Linear Optimization* (1997). Geometry, duality, the simplex and dual simplex methods, sensitivity, the ellipsoid method and interior-point methods in one careful volume; most entries of this chapter cite it.
- Jiří Matoušek and Bernd Gärtner, *Understanding and Using Linear Programming* (2007). A short book for computer scientists, with good chapters on duality, Farkas' lemma and the algorithms of fixed dimension.
- Alexander Schrijver, *Theory of Linear and Integer Programming* (1986). The reference for the theory: polyhedra, Fourier-Motzkin elimination, the complexity of linear programming and its history.
- Martin Grötschel, László Lovász and Alexander Schrijver, *Geometric Algorithms and Combinatorial Optimization*, second edition (1993). The ellipsoid method, separation and optimization, and their uses in combinatorics.
- George B. Dantzig, *Linear Programming and Extensions* (1963). The founder's own book, with the origins of the subject.
