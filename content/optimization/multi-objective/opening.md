A route planner asked for the fastest and the cheapest way from $s$ to $t$ can find a path of cost $(2, 9)$ in time and money, one of cost $(5, 5)$ and one of cost $(8, 2)$, and none of the three beats another in both. A fourth path of cost $(9, 9)$ is beaten by $(5, 5)$ and can be dropped. What remains is not one answer but a set of trade-offs, and the choice among them belongs to the person who travels. The same thing happens when a portfolio trades return against risk, a design trades weight against strength, and a schedule trades cost against lateness. This chapter is about such problems: how to describe the set of best trade-offs, how to compute it or a good part of it, and how to judge an approximation of it.

The two sides of the chapter meet in the [Pareto front](/pareto-front/). Exact methods turn the problem into a sequence of single-objective problems, one point of the front per solve. Evolutionary methods and local search keep a whole set of points and improve it as a set. Both need fast ways to compare vectors, and both run into fronts that are far too large to list.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups, read in order.

1. **The problem and its order.** [Multi-objective optimization](/multi-objective-optimization/), [Pareto dominance](/pareto-dominance/), [Pareto optimality](/pareto-optimality/) and the front, with the [ideal point](/ideal-point/) and the [nadir point](/nadir-point/) that bound it.
2. **Preferences and scalarization.** How preferences enter, and the classic ways to turn several objectives into one: the [weighted sum](/weighted-sum-method/), the [epsilon-constraint method](/epsilon-constraint-method/), the [weighted Tchebycheff method](/weighted-tchebycheff-method/), [goal programming](/goal-programming/) and [lexicographic optimization](/lexicographic-optimization/).
3. **Reference points and directions.** The [achievement scalarizing function](/achievement-scalarizing-function/), the interactive [reference point method](/reference-point-method/) built on it, and [normal-boundary intersection](/normal-boundary-intersection/), which spreads points evenly along the front.
4. **Finite sets of vectors.** The [Pareto front of a finite set](/pareto-front-computation/), [Kung's algorithm](/kung-algorithm/), [non-dominated sorting](/non-dominated-sorting/) into layers, ranks and the [crowding distance](/crowding-distance/).
5. **Evolutionary algorithms.** The [general scheme](/multi-objective-evolutionary-algorithm/) and its main instances, from [VEGA](/vega/) to [NSGA-II](/nsga-ii/), [SPEA2](/spea2/), [NSGA-III](/nsga-iii/) and [MOEA/D](/moea-d/).
6. **Local search with an archive.** [PAES](/paes/), [multi-objective simulated annealing](/multi-objective-simulated-annealing/) and [Pareto local search](/pareto-local-search/).
7. **Measuring and approximating fronts.** [Epsilon dominance](/epsilon-dominance/), the quality indicators led by the [hypervolume](/hypervolume-indicator/), and [approximate Pareto sets](/approximate-pareto-set/) of polynomial size.
8. **Problems with structure.** [Multi-objective linear programs](/multi-objective-linear-programming/) and [Benson's algorithm](/benson-algorithm/), then the [shortest path](/multi-objective-shortest-path/), [spanning tree](/multi-objective-minimum-spanning-tree/) and [knapsack](/multi-objective-knapsack/) problems with several costs.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these fifteen entries in order: [multi-objective optimization](/multi-objective-optimization/), [Pareto dominance](/pareto-dominance/), [Pareto optimality](/pareto-optimality/), [the Pareto front](/pareto-front/), [the ideal point](/ideal-point/), [the nadir point](/nadir-point/), [scalarization](/scalarization/), [the weighted sum method](/weighted-sum-method/), [the epsilon-constraint method](/epsilon-constraint-method/), [the weighted Tchebycheff method](/weighted-tchebycheff-method/), [Pareto front computation](/pareto-front-computation/), [non-dominated sorting](/non-dominated-sorting/), [crowding distance](/crowding-distance/), [NSGA-II](/nsga-ii/) and [the hypervolume indicator](/hypervolume-indicator/). They give the language of the chapter, the three scalarizations that every later method uses, and the most used evolutionary method with the indicator that judges it. Many entries share one example, the seven points $(2, 9), (3, 6), (4, 7), (5, 4), (7, 3), (8, 5), (9, 1)$, so a result met in one entry can be checked against another. Skip at first the early evolutionary methods of group 5, which matter mostly as history, and the hypervolume algorithms for more than three objectives.

## Before you start

The chapter assumes single-objective optimization. You need [constrained optimization](/constrained-optimization/), the [linear programming problem](/linear-programming-problem/) and [LP duality](/lp-duality/) from Chapter 15.1 and Chapter 15.3, and for Benson's algorithm also [polyhedra](/polyhedron/), [vertex enumeration](/vertex-enumeration/) and [supporting hyperplanes](/supporting-hyperplane/). The evolutionary groups build on Chapter 15.6: [genetic algorithms](/genetic-algorithm/), [tournament selection](/tournament-selection/), [evolution strategies](/evolution-strategies/), [local search](/local-search/) and [simulated annealing](/simulated-annealing/). The last group extends [Dijkstra's algorithm](/dijkstra/), [Kruskal's algorithm](/kruskal/) and the [dynamic program for knapsack](/knapsack-dynamic-programming/). Kung's algorithm is [divide and conquer](/divide-and-conquer-paradigm/) on the [lexicographic order](/lexicographic-order/), and hypervolume computation is a case of [Klee's measure problem](/klee-measure-problem/).
