To optimize is to choose the best of many possible answers. An airline assigns crews to flights at the least cost. A refinery picks how much of each product to make from limited crude oil. A delivery firm orders the stops of its vans. A machine learning model sets millions of weights to fit its training data. Each of these is the same question in a different form: here is a set of feasible choices and a number for each one, so find the choice whose number is smallest or largest.

This volume collects the main ways to answer that question. It starts with linear programs, where the objective and the constraints are linear and the variables are real, and moves to integer programs, where some variables must be whole numbers. Then come convex problems and the gradient methods that solve smooth problems at scale. The last three chapters leave the comfortable cases behind: combinatorial problems on finite sets, the general search strategies called metaheuristics, and problems with several objectives at once.

## Why optimization gets a volume of its own

Optimization runs under much of the rest of the handbook. Shortest paths, maximum flows and matchings are linear programs, and the integer programs of this volume model most of the scheduling, routing and planning done in industry. The ideas that recur are few, and this volume is where they are stated in general:

- **Duality.** Every [linear program](/linear-programming-problem/) has a [dual](/dual-linear-program/), and [weak duality](/weak-duality/) makes any feasible dual value a bound on the primal. The same idea gives [Lagrangian duality](/lagrangian-duality/) for convex problems and [Lagrangian relaxation](/lagrangian-relaxation/) for hard ones.
- **Relaxation.** Drop the hard constraints, solve what remains, and use the answer as a bound. The [LP relaxation](/lp-relaxation/) of an integer program is the standard case.
- **Walking along the boundary or through the interior.** The [simplex method](/simplex-method/) moves from vertex to vertex of a polyhedron. The [interior-point method](/interior-point-method/) follows a path through the inside, and runs in polynomial time.
- **Descent.** [Gradient descent](/gradient-descent/) steps against the gradient. Its rate depends on smoothness and convexity, and lower bounds say how much any first-order method can gain.
- **Local search and its escapes.** [Local search](/local-search/) stops at a local optimum. [Simulated annealing](/simulated-annealing/), tabu search and the population methods are ways to keep going.
- **Structure that makes problems easy.** [Total unimodularity](/total-unimodularity/) makes an LP give integer answers, and [matroids](/rado-edmonds-theorem/) make greedy optimal. Most hard problems lack such structure, and much of the volume is about what to do then.

## The chapters

**Chapter 15.1, Linear programming**, is the base of the volume. It defines the [linear programming problem](/linear-programming-problem/) and its standard and slack forms, and proves [strong duality](/strong-duality/) and [complementary slackness](/complementary-slackness/). The [simplex method](/simplex-method/) is built from pivots, ratio tests and reduced costs, with [Bland's rule](/bland-rule/) to stop cycling. The [Klee-Minty cube](/klee-minty-cube/) forces Dantzig's rule through exponentially many vertices, while the [ellipsoid method](/ellipsoid-method/) of 1979 and [Karmarkar's algorithm](/karmarkar-algorithm/) of 1984 run in polynomial time. The chapter ends with column generation, Dantzig-Wolfe decomposition, linear programming in fixed dimension and the smoothed analysis of the simplex method.

**Chapter 15.2, Integer programming**, adds the condition that variables be integers, which makes the problem NP-hard. Its tools are the [LP relaxation](/lp-relaxation/) and its [integrality gap](/integrality-gap/), formulations with big-M and indicator constraints, and the totally unimodular matrices, whose relaxations are already integral. [Cutting planes](/cutting-plane-method-integer-programming/), from Gomory's cuts to cover and flow cover inequalities, tighten the relaxation, and [branch-and-cut](/branch-and-cut/) and [branch-and-price](/branch-and-price/) combine it with branching. The chapter also covers Benders decomposition, the heuristics inside modern solvers, and [Lenstra's theorem](/lenstra-theorem/) that integer programs in fixed dimension are solvable in polynomial time.

**Chapter 15.3, Convex optimization**, studies problems where every local minimum is global. It starts from [convex functions](/convex-function/), subgradients and the [Lagrangian](/lagrangian-function/), proves duality under [Slater's condition](/slater-condition/), and states the [KKT conditions](/kkt-conditions/). Quadratic, second-order cone, geometric and [semidefinite](/semidefinite-programming/) programs are the main problem classes. The methods include the barrier and [interior-point methods](/interior-point-method/), dual decomposition, the augmented Lagrangian method and [ADMM](/admm/), and bundle methods for nonsmooth problems.

**Chapter 15.4, Gradient methods**, is about first-order methods for smooth problems, the kind used to train models. [Gradient descent](/gradient-descent/) with step $1/L$ has error $O(1/k)$ on convex functions and a linear rate on strongly convex ones. Line searches choose the step, and [Nesterov's method](/nesterov-accelerated-gradient/) reaches $O(1/k^2)$, which a [lower bound](/first-order-lower-bound/) shows is optimal. The stochastic half of the chapter covers [stochastic gradient descent](/stochastic-gradient-descent/), AdaGrad, RMSprop, [Adam](/adam/) and the variance-reduced methods SVRG and SAGA. Quasi-Newton methods such as [BFGS](/bfgs/), mirror descent and proximal gradient methods complete it.

**Chapter 15.5, Combinatorial optimization**, treats problems with a finite but huge set of solutions: the [knapsack problem](/knapsack-problem/), the [traveling salesman problem](/traveling-salesman-problem/), vehicle routing, graph partitioning and facility location. For each it gives a simple exact method or heuristic, such as dynamic programming for knapsack, savings for routes and Kernighan-Lin for partitioning. The [Rado-Edmonds theorem](/rado-edmonds-theorem/) says when greedy is optimal, [submodular functions](/submodular-function/) can be minimized in polynomial time, and two matroids can still be intersected in polynomial time by [matroid intersection](/matroid-intersection/). Lagrangian relaxation and [large neighborhood search](/large-neighborhood-search/) apply across the chapter.

**Chapter 15.6, Metaheuristics**, collects general strategies for searching when no exact method is fast enough. It starts with [neighborhood structures](/neighborhood-structure/), [local optima](/local-optimum/) and [hill climbing](/hill-climbing/), and then the methods that escape local optima: [simulated annealing](/simulated-annealing/), whose [convergence theorem](/simulated-annealing-convergence/) ties the cooling schedule to the depth of the deepest trap; [tabu search](/tabu-search/); and iterated local search, variable neighborhood search and GRASP. The population methods follow: [genetic algorithms](/genetic-algorithm/) with selection, crossover and mutation, evolution strategies and [CMA-ES](/cma-es/), differential evolution, [particle swarm optimization](/particle-swarm-optimization/) and [ant colony optimization](/ant-colony-optimization/). The [no free lunch theorem](/no-free-lunch-theorem/) sets a limit on all of them.

**Chapter 15.7, Multi-objective optimization**, handles several objectives that conflict. It defines [Pareto dominance](/pareto-dominance/) and the [Pareto front](/pareto-front/), and turns many objectives into one by [scalarization](/scalarization/): weighted sums, the epsilon-constraint method and the weighted Tchebycheff method. [Non-dominated sorting](/non-dominated-sorting/) and [crowding distance](/crowding-distance/) are the parts of [NSGA-II](/nsga-ii/), and the chapter also covers SPEA2, NSGA-III and the decomposition method MOEA/D. The [hypervolume indicator](/hypervolume-indicator/) measures the quality of an approximate front. The chapter ends with multi-objective linear programming and Benson's algorithm, and with shortest paths and knapsacks whose fronts can be exponentially large.

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course, read these entries in this order: [linear programming](/linear-programming-problem/), [the dual linear program](/dual-linear-program/), [weak duality](/weak-duality/), [the simplex method](/simplex-method/) and [strong duality](/strong-duality/) in Chapter 15.1; [the LP relaxation](/lp-relaxation/) and [total unimodularity](/total-unimodularity/) in Chapter 15.2; [convex functions](/convex-function/), [Lagrangian duality](/lagrangian-duality/) and [the KKT conditions](/kkt-conditions/) in Chapter 15.3; [gradient descent](/gradient-descent/) and [stochastic gradient descent](/stochastic-gradient-descent/) in Chapter 15.4; [the knapsack problem](/knapsack-problem/) and [the traveling salesman problem](/traveling-salesman-problem/) in Chapter 15.5; [local search](/local-search/) and [simulated annealing](/simulated-annealing/) in Chapter 15.6; and [the Pareto front](/pareto-front/) in Chapter 15.7. Do the exercises marked ◦ and • at the end of each of these chapters. That is a first unit on optimization.

The first four chapters lean on one another, so read them in order. Chapters 15.5 to 15.7 can be read in any order once you know Chapter 15.1, though large neighborhood search in Chapter 15.5 and the evolutionary methods of Chapter 15.7 use the local search and genetic algorithm entries of Chapter 15.6. If you already know the basics, start with the opening page of each chapter. It names the groups in the chapter and the order to read them. The entries marked *Advanced* and *Research* are the place to go deeper.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained. Entries in other volumes are cited by number too, as the [greedy algorithm](/greedy-algorithm/) in Volume 2 and the [minimum spanning tree](/minimum-spanning-tree/) in Volume 7 are; the number is printed with the link.

## What you need first

You should be able to read a short program and follow a proof by induction. You need linear algebra: [linear independence](/linear-independence/), matrices and [positive definite matrices](/positive-definite-matrix/), and some calculus of several variables for the gradient chapters. [Convex sets](/convex-set/) come from Volume 13. The stochastic methods use [expected values](/expected-value/), and the convergence theory of simulated annealing uses [Markov chains](/markov-chain/). From Volume 2 you need the [greedy algorithm](/greedy-algorithm/) and branch and bound, and from Volume 7 [graphs](/graph/) and [maximum flows](/max-flow-problem/). For the hardness results you should know what NP-hard means and how a [reduction](/reduction/) works.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
