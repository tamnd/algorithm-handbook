A C function stores through two pointers and reads one of them back: `*p = 1; *q = 2; return *p;`. A compiler would like to return the constant 1 and drop the load. It may do so only if `p` and `q` never hold the same address, and the function alone does not say whether they do. The answer depends on every caller, on every structure the pointers came from, and on every assignment the program makes to them. An analysis that finds the targets of `p` and `q` in the whole program, and finds them disjoint, lets the compiler keep 1 in a register. One that cannot tell must keep the load.

That question is [alias analysis](/alias-analysis/): whether two references may, or must, name the same location. Most analyses answer it through [points-to analysis](/points-to-analysis/), which gives each pointer a set of abstract locations, one for each variable and one for each allocation site, and calls two references aliases when their sets meet. The same sets give the targets of calls through function pointers and virtual methods, the side effects of a call, and the objects that can live on the stack.

The chapter holds three kinds of results, and each entry says which kind it gives. Some are limits. Precise may-alias analysis is undecidable once the program can build recursive data structures, and even when the order of statements is ignored the precise answer is NP-hard. Some are exact for a stated model: [Andersen's analysis](/andersen-analysis/) finds the least solution of its subset constraints in $O(n^3)$ time, [Steensgaard's analysis](/steensgaard-analysis/) finds the least solution of its unification constraints in almost linear time, and [0-CFA](/zero-cfa/) is cubic. The rest are measured: how close [one-level flow](/das-one-level-flow/) comes to Andersen's precision, how much cycle detection speeds the solver, and which kind of context sensitivity pays off on Java benchmarks.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in seven groups, read in order.

1. **The problem.** [Alias analysis](/alias-analysis/) and [points-to analysis](/points-to-analysis/).
2. **Inclusion and unification.** [Andersen's analysis](/andersen-analysis/), [Steensgaard's analysis](/steensgaard-analysis/), [one-level flow](/das-one-level-flow/) and [field sensitivity](/field-sensitivity/).
3. **Solving at scale.** [Offline variable substitution](/offline-variable-substitution/), [online cycle elimination](/online-cycle-elimination/), [lazy and hybrid cycle detection](/hybrid-cycle-detection/), [demand-driven points-to analysis](/demand-driven-points-to/) and [sparse flow-sensitive pointer analysis](/sparse-flow-sensitive-pointer-analysis/).
4. **Call graphs for object-oriented programs.** [Call graph construction](/call-graph-construction/), [class hierarchy analysis](/class-hierarchy-analysis/), [rapid type analysis](/rapid-type-analysis/) and [variable type analysis](/variable-type-analysis/).
5. **Higher-order programs and contexts.** [0-CFA](/zero-cfa/), [k-CFA](/k-cfa/), [object sensitivity](/object-sensitivity/) and [BDD-based points-to analysis](/bdd-points-to-analysis/).
6. **Using alias facts.** [Type-based alias analysis](/type-based-alias-analysis/), [mod-ref analysis](/mod-ref-analysis/) and [escape analysis](/escape-analysis/).
7. **Limits.** The [undecidability of may-alias analysis](/may-alias-undecidability/), the [NP-hardness of precise flow-insensitive alias analysis](/flow-insensitive-alias-np-hardness/) and the [EXPTIME-completeness of k-CFA](/k-cfa-exptime-completeness/).

## A first reading

Take these twelve entries in order: alias analysis and points-to analysis; Andersen's analysis and Steensgaard's analysis; call graph construction, class hierarchy analysis and rapid type analysis; 0-CFA and k-CFA; mod-ref analysis and escape analysis; and the undecidability of may-alias analysis. They give the two questions, the two classic solvers and the gap in precision between them, the cheapest sound call graphs, the cost of contexts, two clients of the facts, and the reason no analysis is exact.

One-level flow, field sensitivity, the solver speedups in group 3, variable type analysis, object sensitivity, BDD-based analysis, type-based alias analysis and the two complexity theorems can wait for a second pass.

The chapter works on the [three-address code](/three-address-code/), [control flow graphs](/control-flow-graph/), [call graphs](/call-graph/) and [def-use chains](/def-use-chains/) of Chapter 34.2, and on the ideas of Chapter 34.3: [dataflow analysis](/dataflow-analysis/), the [worklist algorithm](/worklist-dataflow-algorithm/), [flow sensitivity](/flow-sensitivity/) and [context sensitivity](/context-sensitivity/). Class hierarchies use [subtyping](/subtyping/) from Chapter 34.1. Steensgaard's analysis and type-based alias analysis use the [disjoint-set forest](/disjoint-set-forest/) of Volume 3, with the [inverse Ackermann function](/inverse-ackermann-function/) of Volume 1. Andersen's analysis is close to [transitive closure](/transitive-closure/), and the solvers use [graph reachability](/graph-reachability/), [strongly connected components](/strongly-connected-components/) and [Tarjan's algorithm](/tarjan-scc/), all in Volume 7. Many tools write the analyses as [Datalog](/datalog/) rules, evaluated by [semi-naive evaluation](/semi-naive-evaluation/) and restricted to queries by [magic sets](/magic-sets/), from Volume 40, and store them in [reduced ordered BDDs](/reduced-ordered-bdd/) from Volume 16. The control flow analyses use the [lambda calculus](/lambda-calculus/) of Volume 1. The chapter assumes that you know what NP-hardness, EXPTIME and undecidability mean and what a reduction is.
