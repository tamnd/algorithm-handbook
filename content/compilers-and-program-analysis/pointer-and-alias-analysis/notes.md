## History

Alias analysis began with reference parameters in Fortran-like languages, where a procedure may change a variable through a formal that names it. Banning (1979) wrote the side effects of calls as a system of equations over the call graph, and Cooper and Kennedy (1988) solved the alias-free part in linear time with the binding graph. Pointers to heap data made the problem harder. Landi (1992) showed that precise may-alias analysis with recursive data structures is undecidable, and Ramalingam (1994) gave a short reduction from the Post correspondence problem. Horwitz (1997) proved that even the flow-insensitive question is NP-hard, and Chakaravarthy (2003) showed that the undecidability needs no records.

The flow-insensitive solvers came in the 1990s. Lars Ole Andersen defined the inclusion-based analysis in his 1994 PhD thesis at DIKU, as part of a partial evaluator for C. Steensgaard answered its cubic cost at POPL 1996 with a unification analysis in the style of type inference, solved by union-find; he reported a C program of about 75,000 lines analyzed in about 27 seconds. Heintze and McAllester (1997) tied the cubic cost of Andersen's analysis and of 0-CFA to 2NPDA reachability. Das (2000) kept the direction at the top level and measured that this recovers most of Andersen's precision. Hind (2001) surveyed the field and asked whether the problem was solved.

The next decade made Andersen's analysis fast. Fähndrich, Foster, Su and Aiken (1998) merged cycles while solving, Rountev and Chandra (2000) found equal variables before solving, and Hardekopf and Lin (2007) combined a lazy check with an offline pass and analyzed C programs of over a million lines. Heintze and Tardieu (2001) and Sridharan and Bodík (2006) answered queries on demand, and Hardekopf and Lin (2011) ran a flow-sensitive analysis along def-use chains found by Andersen's. Sridharan and Fink (2009) explained the speed on real code by the k-sparse bound.

Object-oriented and functional languages brought calls whose targets are values. Shivers defined 0-CFA and 1-CFA for Scheme in 1988 and in his 1991 thesis. For object-oriented code, Dean, Grove and Chambers (1995) gave class hierarchy analysis, Bacon and Sweeney (1996) rapid type analysis, and Sundaresan and colleagues (2000) variable type analysis. Three papers at OOPSLA 1999, by Choi and colleagues, by Blanchet, and by Whaley and Rinard, used points-to facts for escape analysis. Milanova, Rountev and Ryder (2002) used receiver objects as contexts, Berndl and colleagues (2003) and Whaley and Lam (2004) stored the facts in BDDs, and Van Horn and Mairson (2008) proved k-CFA EXPTIME-complete for every $k \ge 1$. Tools such as Doop now write the analyses as Datalog rules.

## Further reading

- Michael Hind, "Pointer analysis: haven't we solved this problem yet?", *Proceedings of PASTE '01*, 2001. A short survey of the design space, the measurements and the open questions.
- Yannis Smaragdakis and George Balatsouras, "Pointer Analysis", *Foundations and Trends in Programming Languages* 2(1), 2015. Points-to analysis as Datalog rules, with call-site, object and type sensitivity in one framework.
- Alfred V. Aho, Monica S. Lam, Ravi Sethi and Jeffrey D. Ullman, *Compilers: Principles, Techniques, and Tools*, second edition, Addison-Wesley, 2007. Chapter 12 covers interprocedural analysis, Datalog-based points-to analysis and BDDs.
- Flemming Nielson, Hanne Riis Nielson and Chris Hankin, *Principles of Program Analysis*, Springer, 1999. Control flow analysis, from 0-CFA to uniform k-CFA, with proofs.
- Lars Ole Andersen, *Program Analysis and Specialization for the C Programming Language*, PhD thesis, DIKU, University of Copenhagen, 1994. The source of the inclusion-based analysis.
