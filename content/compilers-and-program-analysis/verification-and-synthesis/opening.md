This chapter is about algorithms that reason about what a program does: they find inputs that break it, prove that no input breaks it, or build the program from a description of what it should do. Take a function with two inputs that computes `z = x - y` when `x > y` and `z = y - x` otherwise, and fails when `z == 3`. Its four paths have four [path conditions](/path-condition/), and a solver turns the condition $\alpha > \beta \wedge \alpha - \beta = 3$ of a failing path into the input $(3, 0)$. [Concolic testing](/concolic-testing/) does this path after path on real code, and SAGE, the [generational search](/generational-search/) tool, was reported to find about a third of the bugs that file fuzzing found in Windows 7. In the other direction, from a few examples such as "Alan Turing" becoming "A. Turing", [string transformation synthesis](/string-transformation-synthesis/) writes the program, as Flash Fill does in Excel.

The chapter keeps three kinds of result apart, and each entry says which kind it gives. Testing methods find bugs and prove nothing beyond the paths they cover: concolic testing, [coverage-guided fuzzing](/coverage-guided-fuzzing/) and [equivalence modulo inputs](/equivalence-modulo-inputs/) report only real failures, and a run with no failure shows nothing. Proof methods are sound: a valid set of [verification conditions](/verification-condition-generation/) proves the annotated program correct, and a [linear ranking function](/ranking-function-synthesis/) proves that a loop stops. Synthesis methods search, and they are complete only inside a bounded space. Behind all three stands Rice's theorem: every nontrivial property of what a program computes is undecidable, and so are the existence of an adequate loop invariant, program equivalence and the realizability of a specification by a program from a grammar.

Chapter 1.4 of Volume 1 gives the foundations that this chapter builds on, as short overview entries: [symbolic execution](/symbolic-execution/), [Hoare logic](/hoare-logic/), [weakest preconditions](/weakest-precondition/), [loop invariants](/loop-invariant/), [termination proofs](/termination-proof/) and [differential testing](/differential-testing/). Those entries explain the ideas and assume that the invariant or the ranking function is given. This chapter gives the algorithms behind them: the paths, trees and search orders of symbolic execution, the generation of verification conditions, the automatic search for invariants and ranking functions, and testing of compilers. The solvers come from Volume 16: the [SMT problem](/smt-problem/), [CEGIS](/cegis/) and [syntax-guided synthesis](/syntax-guided-synthesis/), and model checking with [bounded model checking](/bounded-model-checking/) and [CEGAR](/cegar/). This chapter links to them and does not repeat them.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in seven groups, read in order.

1. **Paths and their conditions.** The path condition, the [strongest postcondition](/strongest-postcondition/), the [symbolic execution tree](/symbolic-execution-tree/) and the [path explosion problem](/path-explosion-problem/).
2. **Testing by search.** Concolic testing and generational search, coverage-guided and [grammar-based fuzzing](/grammar-based-fuzzing/), [delta debugging](/delta-debugging/) to shrink a failing input, and equivalence modulo inputs for compilers.
3. **Verification conditions and invariants.** Verification condition generation, [loop invariant synthesis](/loop-invariant-synthesis/), the [Houdini algorithm](/houdini-algorithm/), [invariants from Farkas' lemma](/farkas-invariant-generation/) and [proof-carrying code](/proof-carrying-code/).
4. **Termination.** Linear ranking function synthesis by one linear program, and [transition invariants](/transition-invariants/) for loops with many paths.
5. **Two runs and two programs.** [Noninterference](/noninterference/), [self-composition](/self-composition/), [verified compilation](/verified-compilation/) and [translation validation](/translation-validation/).
6. **Program synthesis.** The [synthesis problem](/program-synthesis/), [programming by example](/programming-by-example/), [bottom-up enumeration](/bottom-up-enumerative-synthesis/), [version space algebras](/version-space-algebra/), string transformations, [program sketching](/program-sketching/), [deductive synthesis](/deductive-program-synthesis/), [type-directed synthesis](/type-directed-synthesis/) and [automated program repair](/automated-program-repair/).
7. **Reactive synthesis.** Controllers from temporal specifications by [reactive synthesis](/reactive-synthesis/), and the [Pnueli-Rosner theorem](/pnueli-rosner-theorem/) on its cost.

## A first reading

Take these twelve entries in order: path condition, symbolic execution tree, path explosion problem, concolic testing, coverage-guided fuzzing, delta debugging, verification condition generation, loop invariant synthesis, Houdini, linear ranking function synthesis, program synthesis and bottom-up enumerative synthesis. They give the two ways to look for bugs, the way to turn code into formulas, the search for invariants and ranking functions, and the plainest synthesis algorithm.

The strongest postcondition, generational search, grammar-based fuzzing and equivalence modulo inputs refine the testing group and can wait. The research entries, Farkas invariants, transition invariants, self-composition, type-directed synthesis, program repair and the Pnueli-Rosner theorem, are for a second pass, and so is reactive synthesis unless you know LTL.

## Before you start

From Volume 1 you need the entries named above, with [Hoare triples](/hoare-triple/), [inductive invariants](/inductive-invariant/), [well-founded relations](/well-founded-relation/) and [structural induction](/structural-induction/). From Volume 16 you need the SMT problem and CEGIS, and for the later groups [safety properties](/safety-property/), [simulation relations](/simulation-relation/), [linear temporal logic](/linear-temporal-logic/), [parity games](/parity-game/) and [resolution](/robinson-resolution/). The invariant and termination entries use [Farkas' lemma](/farkas-lemma/) and [linear programming](/linear-programming-problem/) from Volume 15, and transition invariants use [Ramsey's theorem](/ramsey-theorem/) from Volume 7. Grammar-based fuzzing and synthesis use [context-free grammars](/context-free-grammar/) from Volume 9. Inside this volume, the [control flow graph](/control-flow-graph/) of Chapter 34.2 is the program model of most entries, and type-directed synthesis and proof-carrying code use the [simply typed lambda calculus](/simply-typed-lambda-calculus/) and [type soundness](/type-soundness/) of Chapter 34.1. The chapter assumes that you know what undecidability, NP-completeness and a reduction are.
