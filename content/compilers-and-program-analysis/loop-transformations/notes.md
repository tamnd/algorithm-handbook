## History

The question of when two pieces of a program may run in either order goes back to Bernstein, who in 1966 stated the conditions on the sets of locations they read and write. In 1967 Karp, Miller and Winograd studied systems of uniform recurrence equations and asked when they can be scheduled in parallel, which laid the ground for the later work on schedules. Lamport's 1974 paper on the parallel execution of DO loops gave the hyperplane method and the coordinate method for loops with constant distances.

Dependence analysis took shape in Kuck's group at the University of Illinois, where the Parafrase restructurer turned Fortran loops into vector and parallel code. Banerjee's test came from that group. The paper by Kuck, Kuhn, Padua, Leasure and Wolfe at POPL 1981 set out dependence graphs as the basis of loop optimization. At Rice, Allen and Kennedy built the PFC vectorizer, and their 1987 paper in *ACM Transactions on Programming Languages and Systems* gave the level-by-level algorithm that now carries their names. Wolfe described loop skewing in 1986, Cytron studied DOACROSS loops in 1986, and Irigoin and Triolet introduced supernode partitioning, the origin of tiling, in 1988. Goff, Kennedy and Tseng presented the practical subscript-wise tests at PLDI 1991. Pugh's Omega test, published in 1992, decides integer linear constraints exactly, and its measurements showed it fast on the subscripts of real programs, though its worst case is exponential.

Around 1990 transformations became matrices. Banerjee and, at Stanford, Wolf and Lam treated interchange, reversal and skewing as unimodular matrices, and in 1991 Wolf and Lam published both the theory of such transformations for parallelism and the locality algorithm based on reuse vector spaces. In 1993 Tu and Padua worked out array privatization, and Maydan, Amarasinghe and Lam gave an array dataflow analysis tuned for speed. Gerlek, Stoltz and Wolfe classified induction variables on SSA form in 1995, and Bachmann, Wang and Zima introduced chains of recurrences in 1994.

The polyhedral model grew out of Feautrier's parametric integer programming of 1988. With it he gave exact array dataflow analysis in 1991 and his scheduling algorithm in two papers of 1992. Ancourt and Irigoin showed in 1991 how to scan a polyhedron with loops. Quilleré, Rajopadhye and Wilde gave the separation method for unions of polyhedra in 2000, and Bastoul's CLooG made it practical in 2004. Larsen and Amarasinghe introduced superword-level parallelism in 2000. Bondhugula, Hartono, Ramanujam and Sadayappan presented Pluto at PLDI 2008; it tiles and parallelizes imperfect nests automatically.

## Further reading

- Randy Allen and Ken Kennedy, *Optimizing Compilers for Modern Architectures: A Dependence-Based Approach*, Morgan Kaufmann, 2002. Dependence testing, vectorization, fusion and the Allen-Kennedy algorithm, from the authors of PFC.
- Michael Wolfe, *High Performance Compilers for Parallel Computing*, Addison-Wesley, 1996. A broad treatment of dependences, induction variables and the restructuring transformations.
- Alfred V. Aho, Monica S. Lam, Ravi Sethi and Jeffrey D. Ullman, *Compilers: Principles, Techniques, and Tools*, second edition, Addison-Wesley, 2007. Its chapter on parallelism and locality introduces affine transforms, synchronization-free parallelism and tiling.
- Utpal Banerjee, *Dependence Analysis for Supercomputing*, Kluwer, 1988. The GCD and Banerjee tests and direction vectors, with proofs.
- Paul Feautrier, "Some efficient solutions to the affine scheduling problem", parts I and II, *International Journal of Parallel Programming* 21, 1992. The scheduling algorithm and the affine form of Farkas' lemma.
- Alexander Schrijver, *Theory of Linear and Integer Programming*, Wiley, 1986. The background for Fourier-Motzkin elimination, Farkas' lemma and unimodular matrices.
