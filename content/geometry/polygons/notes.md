## History

The oldest formula in the chapter is the [shoelace formula](/shoelace-formula/). Braden's survey credits it to A. L. F. Meister, who gave it in 1769, and to Gauss, who used it in 1795. Surveyors computed field areas from corner coordinates this way long before computers. [Pick's theorem](/picks-theorem/), which links the area of a lattice polygon to its lattice points, was published by Georg Pick in Prague in 1899. It became widely known through Hugo Steinhaus's book *Mathematical Snapshots*. In 1957 Reeve showed that it has no direct analogue in three dimensions.

The containment tests come from early computing. Shimrat published the [ray crossing test](/crossing-number-algorithm/) as Algorithm 112 in the Communications of the ACM in 1962, and Hacker's certification later that year corrected a flaw in it. Shamos's 1978 thesis gave the wedge method for a [convex polygon](/point-in-convex-polygon/), and the 1985 book of Preparata and Shamos made it standard. The [winding number](/winding-number-algorithm/) is an old idea from topology and complex analysis. In 2001 Hormann and Agathos showed that counting crossings with signs computes it exactly for any polygon, and Sunday published the same test as code that year.

Clipping grew out of computer graphics. Sutherland and Hodgman described [reentrant clipping](/sutherland-hodgman/) in 1974 as a pipeline of identical stages, one per clip plane, meant for hardware. [Weiler and Atherton](/weiler-atherton-algorithm/) introduced their method in 1977 as the clipping step of a hidden surface algorithm. Vatti's [generic solution](/vatti-clipping/) of 1992 handled arbitrary subject and clip polygons. [Greiner and Hormann](/greiner-hormann-algorithm/) published their linked-list method in 1998, and Foster, Hormann and Popa extended it to degenerate intersections in 2019. In 1983 Guibas, Ramshaw and Stolfi described the [Minkowski sum of convex polygons](/minkowski-sum-convex-polygons/) as a merge of edge sequences. In the same year Lozano-Pérez used such sums to build configuration space obstacles for robot motion.

The visibility questions started with a problem Victor Klee posed in 1973: how many guards are needed to watch a gallery with $n$ walls. Chvátal proved the [art gallery theorem](/art-gallery-theorem/) in 1975, and Fisk found the three-coloring proof in 1978. Avis and Toussaint turned it into an $O(n \log n)$ algorithm in 1981, and in 1983 Kahn, Klawe and Kleitman proved the $\lfloor n/4 \rfloor$ bound for rectilinear polygons. Lee and Lin showed in 1986 that the fewest guards is NP-hard to find. In 2018 Abrahamsen, Adamaszek and Miltzow showed that the point-guard version is complete for the existential theory of the reals. Lee and Preparata gave the linear-time [kernel](/polygon-kernel/) algorithm in 1979 and the [funnel algorithm](/funnel-algorithm/) in 1984. Hertel and Mehlhorn published their [convex partition](/hertel-mehlhorn/) in 1983, with the journal version in 1985. Aichholzer, Aurenhammer, Alberts and Gärtner introduced the [straight skeleton](/straight-skeleton/) in 1995.

## Further reading

- Joseph O'Rourke, *Computational Geometry in C*, second edition (1998). Chapter 1 builds the signed area and the turn test as exact integer code, and Section 7.4 treats point in polygon with the half-open rule.
- Joseph O'Rourke, *Art Gallery Theorems and Algorithms* (1987). Collects the guard theorems, with Fisk's proof in Chapter 1 and the rectilinear $\lfloor n/4 \rfloor$ bound in Chapter 2.
- Franco P. Preparata and Michael Ian Shamos, *Computational Geometry: An Introduction* (1985). Covers point location in convex and star-shaped polygons, the simplicity test by sweep, and the Lee-Preparata kernel algorithm.
- Mark de Berg, Otfried Cheong, Marc van Kreveld and Mark Overmars, *Computational Geometry: Algorithms and Applications*, third edition (2008). A textbook account of monotone partition, art galleries and Minkowski sums for motion planning.
- Günther Greiner and Kai Hormann, *Efficient Clipping of Arbitrary Polygons*, ACM Transactions on Graphics 17 (1998). A short paper that compares the main clipping methods and gives a complete one for general polygons.
- Bart Braden, *The Surveyor's Area Formula*, The College Mathematics Journal 17 (1986). Traces the shoelace formula to Meister and Gauss and proves it in several ways.
