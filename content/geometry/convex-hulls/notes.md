## History

Convex sets were studied long before anyone computed a hull. Hermann Minkowski, in his work on the geometry of numbers in the 1890s, showed that a bounded intersection of finitely many half-spaces is the hull of finitely many points. Constantin Carathéodory proved his theorem in 1907, in a paper on power series. Eduard Helly found his theorem in 1913, and Johann Radon published the first proof in 1921, with the partition lemma now named after him; Helly's own paper appeared in 1923. Hermann Weyl gave the converse of Minkowski's result in 1935, and Peter McMullen proved the Upper Bound Theorem on the number of faces of a polytope in 1970, after Theodore Motzkin had stated it in 1957.

The algorithms came in the 1970s. Donald Chand and Sham Kapur described gift wrapping for polytopes in any dimension in 1970. Ronald Graham published his scan in 1972, in a two-page note written for a problem at Bell Laboratories; it was the first planar hull algorithm with an $O(n \log n)$ bound. R. A. Jarvis published the planar gift wrapping in 1973. Quickhull was published by William Eddy in 1977 and found again by A. Bykat in 1978. Franco Preparata and Se June Hong gave divide and conquer in the plane and in space in 1977, and A. M. Andrew replaced Graham's angular sort with a sort on $x$ in 1979.

Michael Shamos's thesis of 1978 set much of the agenda of the young field. It observed that the ordered hull can sort numbers lifted to a parabola, and it used the antipodal pairs of a convex polygon to find its diameter; Godfried Toussaint later named and generalized the rotating calipers. Andrew Yao showed in 1981 that even finding the extreme points needs $\Omega(n \log n)$ quadratic tests, and Michael Ben-Or's 1983 bound for algebraic computation trees settled the question in a strong model. David Kirkpatrick and Raimund Seidel then asked how the cost depends on the output size $h$. Their paper of 1986, "The ultimate planar convex hull algorithm?", gave both an $O(n \log h)$ algorithm and a matching lower bound. Timothy Chan reached the same bound in 1996 with a much simpler method.

Other questions grew from the hull. Mark Overmars and Jan van Leeuwen kept a hull under insertions and deletions in 1981, and Bernard Chazelle computed all convex layers in optimal time in 1985. Linear-time hulls of simple polygons had a troubled start, with several published methods that failed on some inputs; D. T. Lee, and Graham and Frances Yao, gave correct ones in 1983, and Avraham Melkman's deque method of 1987 became the standard. In higher dimensions, Seidel and Michael Kallay turned the beneath-beyond description of Branko Grünbaum's book into an algorithm, Kenneth Clarkson and Peter Shor showed in 1989 that a random insertion order gives the optimal expected time, and Chazelle found a deterministic optimal algorithm in 1993. The Qhull program of Barber, Dobkin and Huhdanpaa, from 1996, extended quickhull to any dimension, and many numerical libraries now call it for hulls, Delaunay triangulations and Voronoi diagrams.

## Further reading

- Mark de Berg, Otfried Cheong, Marc van Kreveld and Mark Overmars, *Computational Geometry: Algorithms and Applications*, third edition (2008). Chapter 1 builds the planar hull with care for degenerate cases, and Chapter 11 the randomized hull in three dimensions.
- Franco P. Preparata and Michael Ian Shamos, *Computational Geometry: An Introduction* (1985). The first textbook of the field; its chapters on hulls cover the classical algorithms, the lower bounds and the diameter.
- Joseph O'Rourke, *Computational Geometry in C*, second edition (1998). Working code for the Graham scan and the 3D hull, with a frank account of what goes wrong in floating point.
- Jiří Matoušek, *Lectures on Discrete Geometry* (2002). The theorems of Carathéodory, Radon, Helly and Tverberg, with their many relatives, and the combinatorics of polytopes.
- Günter M. Ziegler, *Lectures on Polytopes* (1995). The standard text on polytopes, their two descriptions and the counting of their faces.
