## History

Range searching grew out of database queries on several keys. Jon Louis Bentley introduced the kd-tree in 1975 for associative searching, and D. T. Lee and C. K. Wong proved its $O(n^{1-1/d} + k)$ worst case for box queries in 1977. Range trees were found independently between 1978 and 1980 by Bentley, as part of his multidimensional divide and conquer, by George Lueker, by Lee and Wong, and by Dan Willard. Bentley and Jerome Friedman surveyed the field in 1979, at a time when most of these structures were new. Edward McCreight's priority search tree, written up as a report in 1980 and published in 1985, answered three-sided queries in linear space.

The 1980s removed logarithms and settled the orthogonal case. Willard (1985) and the fractional cascading of Bernard Chazelle and Leonidas Guibas (1986) gave the layered range tree with $O(\log n + k)$ query time in the plane. Chazelle's filtering search (1986) reported the points in a box in $O(\log n + k)$ time with $O(n \log n / \log \log n)$ space, and solved point enclosure in linear space; his work of 1988 counted in linear space on the word RAM. On the lower bound side, Michael Fredman (1981) introduced the semigroup model and showed that dynamic structures need $\Omega(n \log^d n)$ time for $n$ operations, and Chazelle's two papers of 1990 gave the static bounds, in the semigroup model and on the pointer machine. Bentley (1979) and Bentley and James Saxe (1980) defined decomposable searching problems and the logarithmic method, and Mark Overmars collected the dynamization techniques in his book of 1983.

Ranges that are not boxes needed new tools. Willard's polygon retrieval (1982) built the first partition tree, with query time about $n^{0.774}$ in the plane. Chazelle, Guibas and Lee (1985) answered halfplane reporting optimally with convex layers and duality. The decisive ideas came from random sampling. Vladimir Vapnik and Alexey Chervonenkis had introduced the VC dimension in 1971 for uniform convergence of frequencies; David Haussler and Emo Welzl (1987) defined $\varepsilon$-nets and proved that small ones exist, to build structures for simplex queries. Kenneth Clarkson and Peter Shor (1989) developed random sampling for arrangements, Chazelle and Welzl (1989) found spanning trees with low crossing number, and Chazelle and Joel Friedman (1990) proved that $(1/r)$-cuttings of size $O(r^d)$ exist. Chazelle (1989) showed that triangle queries need $\Omega(n/\sqrt{m})$ time with $m$ words in the semigroup model.

Jiří Matoušek put the pieces together in 1992: his partition theorem gave linear-space partition trees with query time $n^{1-1/d}$ up to polylogarithmic factors, and his shallow cutting lemma gave halfspace reporting in higher dimensions. Timothy Chan (2012) removed the extra factors with optimal partition trees, and Peyman Afshani and Chan (2009) reached $O(\log n + k)$ halfspace reporting in three dimensions with linear space. On the word RAM, Mihai Pătraşcu (2007) proved the cell probe bound for counting in the plane, and Chan, Kasper Green Larsen and Pătraşcu (2011) gave the fastest known structures for orthogonal reporting.

## Further reading

- Mark de Berg, Otfried Cheong, Marc van Kreveld and Mark Overmars, *Computational Geometry: Algorithms and Applications*, third edition (2008). Chapter 5 covers kd-trees, range trees and fractional cascading, Chapter 10 the segment and priority search trees, and Chapter 16 partition trees and cutting trees.
- Pankaj K. Agarwal and Jeff Erickson, *Geometric Range Searching and Its Relatives*, in Advances in Discrete and Computational Geometry (1999). The standard survey, with tables of upper and lower bounds for every range shape and model.
- Jiří Matoušek, *Geometric Range Searching*, ACM Computing Surveys 26(4), 1994. A shorter survey centered on partition trees, cuttings and the trade-off between space and query time.
- Jiří Matoušek, *Lectures on Discrete Geometry* (2002). Chapter 10 proves the epsilon-net theorem and the bounds on VC dimension that the non-orthogonal structures rest on.
- Jacob E. Goodman, Joseph O'Rourke and Csaba D. Tóth (editors), *Handbook of Discrete and Computational Geometry*, third edition (2017). Agarwal's chapter on range searching gives the current bounds in one place.
