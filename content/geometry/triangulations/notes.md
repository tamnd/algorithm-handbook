## History

The oldest result of the chapter is a count. Leonhard Euler asked in a 1751 letter to Christian Goldbach how many ways a convex polygon can be cut into triangles, and gave the answer from small cases. Johann Andreas von Segner found the recurrence in 1758, and Gabriel Lamé proved the closed form in 1838, the year Eugène Catalan studied the same numbers. That every simple polygon can be triangulated by diagonals was known long before Gary Meisters published "Polygons have ears" in 1975; his proof that two ears always exist made ear clipping a correct algorithm. ElGindy, Everett and Toussaint found one ear in linear time in 1993, and Held's FIST (2001) made ear clipping dependable on large industrial data.

The race for fast polygon triangulation started in 1978, when Garey, Johnson, Preparata and Tarjan combined Lee and Preparata's monotone partition sweep of 1977 with a linear stack method for monotone pieces and got $O(n \log n)$. They asked whether sorting is needed. In 1984 Chazelle and Incerpi gave a bound that depends on how much the boundary winds, and Fournier and Montuno showed that triangulation and the trapezoidal decomposition convert into each other in linear time. Tarjan and Van Wyk reached $O(n \log \log n)$ in 1988, Clarkson, Tarjan and Van Wyk a randomized $O(n \log^* n)$ in 1989, and Chazelle the linear bound, presented at FOCS in 1990 and published in 1991. Seidel's simple randomized algorithm appeared the same year, and Amato, Goodrich and Ramos gave a randomized linear-time algorithm in 2001.

The search for good triangulations began with surface interpolation for contour maps in the 1970s. Gilbert in 1979 and Klincsek in 1980 gave the cubic dynamic program for the minimum weight triangulation of a polygon; in 1979 Manacher and Zobrist showed that neither the greedy nor the Delaunay triangulation always has minimum weight, and Garey and Johnson listed the point-set problem as open. Mulzer and Rote proved it NP-hard in 2006. Lawson proved in 1972 that any two triangulations of a point set are joined by flips. For convex polygons, Sleator, Tarjan and Thurston proved in 1986 that the flip distance is at most $2n - 10$ for $n \ge 13$, and that this is reached for all large $n$, and Pournin showed in 2014 that $n \ge 13$ suffices.

Mesh generation with guarantees dates from the late 1980s: Chew's first algorithm (1989) gave uniform meshes with angles of at least $30°$, and Bern, Eppstein and Gilbert (1990) gave graded meshes with quadtrees. Ruppert presented his Delaunay refinement algorithm at SODA in 1993, with the journal version in 1995, and Chew's second algorithm appeared at the Symposium on Computational Geometry the same year. Shewchuk implemented Ruppert's algorithm in his program Triangle in 1996 and analyzed both algorithms in a 2002 survey. On the data structure side, Guibas and Stolfi introduced the quad-edge structure in 1983, published in full in 1985. Erich Schönhardt described his untetrahedralizable polyhedron in 1928.

## Further reading

- Mark de Berg, Otfried Cheong, Marc van Kreveld and Mark Overmars, *Computational Geometry: Algorithms and Applications*, third edition (2008). Chapter 3 gives the monotone partition and the stack method, and Chapter 9 the counting theorem and flips toward the Delaunay triangulation.
- Joseph O'Rourke, *Computational Geometry in C*, second edition (1998). Chapter 1 builds ear clipping from the two ears theorem with complete, exact code.
- Jesús A. De Loera, Jörg Rambau and Francisco Santos, *Triangulations: Structures for Algorithms and Applications* (2010). The reference for flip graphs, counting and triangulations in higher dimensions.
- Jonathan Richard Shewchuk, *Delaunay Refinement Algorithms for Triangular Mesh Generation*, Computational Geometry 22 (2002). Analyzes Ruppert's and Chew's algorithms in one framework, with the termination proofs.
- Siu-Wing Cheng, Tamal K. Dey and Jonathan Richard Shewchuk, *Delaunay Mesh Generation* (2012). A book-length treatment of quality meshing in two and three dimensions.
