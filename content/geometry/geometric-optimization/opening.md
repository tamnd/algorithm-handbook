A radio mast must reach six villages, and its range should be as small as possible: where does it go? The answer is the center of the smallest circle that holds the six points, and that circle touches two or three of them and ignores the rest. A machined disk is measured at a hundred points, and the inspector wants to know how far it is from round: the answer is the width of the thinnest ring that holds the measurements. A warehouse serves five shops and should keep the total driving distance low, three towns want the shortest road network that joins them, and a robot must cross a room full of tables by the shortest route. Each of these asks for the best shape or the best point of some kind, measured by distances. These are the problems of geometric optimization.

Two things make them interesting to an algorithm designer. The optimum is usually fixed by a few of the input points, so the work is to find those few, and random order, pruning and small samples find them in linear time. And the problems split sharply by difficulty. The smallest circle takes $O(n)$ time, while the shortest network with free junctions is NP-hard, so this chapter also shows how approximation, coresets and spanning trees stand in for an exact answer.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups, read in order.

1. **The smallest enclosing circle.** The [minimum enclosing circle](/minimum-enclosing-circle/), fixed by two or three points, and [Welzl's algorithm](/welzls-algorithm/), which finds it in expected $O(n)$ time. The [minimum enclosing ball](/minimum-enclosing-ball/) carries both to $\mathbb{R}^d$, and the [minimum-width annulus](/minimum-width-annulus/) measures roundness.
2. **Calipers on a convex polygon.** [Antipodal pairs](/antipodal-pairs/), the [diameter](/polygon-diameter/) and the [width](/polygon-width/), the [smallest enclosing rectangle](/minimum-area-enclosing-rectangle/), the [largest inscribed triangle](/maximum-area-inscribed-triangle/) and the [smallest enclosing triangle](/minimum-area-enclosing-triangle/), most of them in $O(n)$ time.
3. **Linear time in fixed dimension.** [Prune and search](/prune-and-search/), the abstract [LP-type problem](/lp-type-problem/), [Clarkson's algorithm](/clarksons-algorithm/) and [Chan's randomized optimization technique](/chans-randomized-optimization/).
4. **Approximation in high dimension.** [Coresets](/coreset/), the [Bădoiu–Clarkson algorithm](/badoiu-clarkson-algorithm/) for the smallest ball, the [minimum volume enclosing ellipsoid](/minimum-volume-enclosing-ellipsoid/) and [Khachiyan's algorithm](/khachiyans-algorithm/) for it.
5. **Sums of distances.** The [Fermat point](/fermat-point/) of a triangle, the [geometric median](/geometric-median/) of many points and [Weiszfeld's algorithm](/weiszfelds-algorithm/).
6. **Shortest networks.** The [Euclidean Steiner tree problem](/euclidean-steiner-tree-problem/), the [rectilinear Steiner tree problem](/rectilinear-steiner-tree-problem/) and the [Steiner ratio](/steiner-ratio/), which compares them with the spanning tree.
7. **Paths among obstacles.** The [visibility graph](/visibility-graph/), the [Euclidean shortest path](/euclidean-shortest-path/) among polygonal obstacles, the [Lee–Preparata algorithm](/lee-preparata-algorithm/) inside a simple polygon, and the [minimum link path](/minimum-link-path/).
8. **Empty regions and spread points.** The [largest empty rectangle](/largest-empty-rectangle/) and the [Heilbronn triangle problem](/heilbronn-triangle-problem/), one an algorithm question, the other an open question about $n$ points in a square.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these ten entries in order: the [minimum enclosing circle](/minimum-enclosing-circle/), [Welzl's algorithm](/welzls-algorithm/), [antipodal pairs](/antipodal-pairs/), the [diameter](/polygon-diameter/), the [width](/polygon-width/), the [smallest enclosing rectangle](/minimum-area-enclosing-rectangle/), the [Fermat point](/fermat-point/), the [geometric median](/geometric-median/), the [Euclidean Steiner tree problem](/euclidean-steiner-tree-problem/) and the [visibility graph](/visibility-graph/). They give one exact linear-time method, the caliper walk on a convex polygon, the first sum-of-distances problems and the first hard problem, and each comes with small worked examples that you can check by hand.

On a first pass you can skip the third and fourth groups, which are about methods more than problems, and the Heilbronn problem. Come back to the [LP-type problem](/lp-type-problem/) when you want to know why the same randomized algorithm solves circles, balls and linear programs, and to coresets when the dimension is large. Read the [Lee–Preparata algorithm](/lee-preparata-algorithm/) after triangulations if you have not met the funnel walk before.

## Before you start

The chapter builds on the [convex hull problem](/convex-hull-problem/) and [rotating calipers](/rotating-calipers/) from the chapter on convex hulls, and on the [orientation test](/orientation-test/). The enclosing circle uses the [circumcircle](/circumcircle/) of three points, and the annulus uses the [farthest-point Voronoi diagram](/farthest-point-voronoi-diagram/). The randomized methods rest on [backwards analysis](/backwards-analysis/), as in [randomized incremental construction](/randomized-incremental-construction/), and they extend [Seidel's algorithm](/seidel-linear-programming/) for [linear programming](/linear-programming-problem/). Prune and search uses the [median of medians](/median-of-medians/). The network and path entries need the [minimum spanning tree](/minimum-spanning-tree/), the [Steiner tree problem](/steiner-tree-problem/) on graphs, [Dijkstra's algorithm](/dijkstra/), [breadth-first search](/breadth-first-search/) and [polygon triangulation](/polygon-triangulation/). The one-dimensional geometric median is the [post office problem](/weighted-median-post-office-location/), a weighted median.
