## History

The smallest circle around a set of points is an old question: Welzl's paper of 1991 traces it to a note by J. J. Sylvester from 1857. The Fermat point is older still. Fermat posed the problem of the point with the least total distance to three given points, Torricelli solved it in the seventeenth century, and Thomas Simpson gave the construction with equilateral triangles in 1750. Alfred Weber made the weighted version a model for placing a factory in 1909, and Endre Weiszfeld published his iteration for the geometric median in 1937. Harold Kuhn repaired its behavior at the data points in 1973.

Shortest networks became a subject with the paper of Edgar Gilbert and Henry Pollak in 1968, which set out the 120 degree rule, the bound on Steiner points and the conjecture that the Steiner ratio in the plane is $\sqrt{3}/2$. Maurice Hanan had already reduced the rectilinear problem to a grid in 1966, and Frank Hwang settled the rectilinear ratio $2/3$ in 1976. Garey, Graham and Johnson showed in 1977 that the Euclidean problem is NP-hard. Du and Hwang announced a proof of the conjecture in 1990, and it was cited as a theorem until Ivanov and Tuzhilin found a gap in 2012. Sanjeev Arora's approximation scheme of 1998 showed that the plane problem, unlike the graph problem, can be approximated as closely as one likes.

The algorithms of the chapter date from the beginnings of computational geometry. Michael Shamos used antipodal pairs for the diameter in his thesis of 1978, Freeman and Shapira proved the flush-edge property of the smallest rectangle in 1975, and Godfried Toussaint turned both into the rotating calipers in 1983. Nimrod Megiddo's prune and search of 1983 gave the first linear-time algorithms for the smallest circle and for linear programming in two and three variables, and in 1984 for any fixed dimension. Randomization then made the bounds simple. Kenneth Clarkson's sampling algorithm appeared in 1988, Raimund Seidel's incremental linear programming in 1991, and Emo Welzl's smallest-disk algorithm in the same year. Micha Sharir and Welzl named the common structure LP-type problems in 1992, and Timothy Chan in 1999 gave a general way to turn decision procedures into optimizers.

For paths, the visibility graph goes back to the robot Shakey at SRI around 1969, and Lozano-Pérez and Wesley made it the standard tool for planning in 1979. D. T. Lee built it in $O(n^2 \log n)$ time in 1978. Lee and Franco Preparata gave the funnel walk inside a triangulated polygon in 1984, and John Hershberger and Subhash Suri reached $O(n \log n)$ time among obstacles in 1999 with the continuous Dijkstra method.

High dimension brought another turn around 2000. Bădoiu, Har-Peled and Indyk showed in 2002 that the smallest ball has a coreset whose size depends only on $\varepsilon$, and Bădoiu and Clarkson found the simple iteration in 2003. Leonid Khachiyan analyzed a simple weight-update method for the smallest ellipsoid in 1996; it is the exchange algorithm of Fedorov and Wynn from optimal experimental design, and Fritz John's theorem of 1948 describes its answer. Heilbronn's problem is still open: Komlós, Pintz and Szemerédi disproved his conjecture in 1982, and the best upper bound, by Cohen, Pohoata and Zakharov, is from 2023.

## Further reading

- Mark de Berg, Otfried Cheong, Marc van Kreveld and Mark Overmars, *Computational Geometry: Algorithms and Applications*, third edition (2008). Covers the smallest enclosing disk with linear programming, and the visibility graph.
- Frank K. Hwang, Dana S. Richards and Pawel Winter, *The Steiner Tree Problem* (1992). The Euclidean, rectilinear and graph problems in one volume, with the structure theorems and the exact methods.
- Bernd Gärtner and Emo Welzl, "A simple sampling lemma: analysis and applications in geometric optimization" (2001). One short lemma that gives the running times of Clarkson's method and of the LP-type algorithms.
- Pankaj K. Agarwal, Sariel Har-Peled and Kasturi R. Varadarajan, "Geometric approximation via coresets" (2005). A survey of coresets for enclosing balls, extent measures, shape fitting and clustering.
- Joseph S. B. Mitchell, "Shortest paths and networks", in *Handbook of Discrete and Computational Geometry*, third edition (2017). Shortest paths among obstacles, link distance and the related network problems.
