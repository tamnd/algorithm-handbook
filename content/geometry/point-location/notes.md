## History

Point location took shape as a problem in the mid-1970s, when geometric searching was first studied with the tools of algorithm analysis. The wedge method for one convex polygon is old; Franco Preparata and Michael Shamos open their chapter on point location with it and credit it to Shamos's work of 1975. David Dobkin and Richard Lipton gave the slab method in 1976, in their paper on multidimensional searching problems. It was the first method with $O(\log n)$ query time for a general subdivision, and it paid for it with quadratic space.

The next decade was spent removing that cost. D. T. Lee and Preparata introduced the chain method for monotone subdivisions in 1977, with linear space and $O(\log^2 n)$ query time. Lipton and Robert Tarjan showed with the planar separator theorem that $O(\log n)$ query time and $O(n)$ space can be had together, but their structure was too complicated to use. David Kirkpatrick's hierarchy of triangulations (1983) was the first simple optimal method. In 1986 three more appeared: Herbert Edelsbrunner, Leonidas Guibas and Jorge Stolfi sped up the chain method with the fractional cascading of Bernard Chazelle and Guibas; Neil Sarnak and Tarjan made the slabs persistent; and Richard Cole solved the same problem of storing many similar lists in another way. James Driscoll, Sarnak, Daniel Sleator and Tarjan turned node copying into a general method for persistence in 1989.

Randomization gave the method most used today. Kenneth Clarkson and Peter Shor introduced randomized incremental construction in 1989, and Ketan Mulmuley applied it to trapezoidal decompositions in 1990. Raimund Seidel's paper of 1991 used the history of the construction as the search structure, which needs no conflict lists, and also triangulated simple polygons in $O(n \log^* n)$ expected time. The textbook of Mark de Berg, Marc van Kreveld, Mark Overmars and Otfried Schwarzkopf (1997) added the tail estimate and made the trapezoidal map the standard first method.

Walks came from practice rather than from theory. Charles Lawson walked through triangulations in 1977 for surface interpolation, and Peter Green and Robin Sibson in 1978 to build Delaunay triangulations. Edelsbrunner proved in 1990 that the in-front relation of a Delaunay triangulation has no cycles, which explains why the simplest walk ends there and can loop elsewhere. Olivier Devillers, Sylvain Pion and Monique Teillaud compared the walks in 2002. On the word RAM, Timothy Chan and Mihai Pătraşcu went below the logarithmic bound in 2009.

## Further reading

- Mark de Berg, Otfried Cheong, Marc van Kreveld and Mark Overmars, *Computational Geometry: Algorithms and Applications*, third edition (2008). Chapter 6 builds the trapezoidal map and its search structure, with the full randomized analysis.
- Franco P. Preparata and Michael Ian Shamos, *Computational Geometry: An Introduction* (1985). The classical deterministic methods: slabs, chains and Kirkpatrick's hierarchy.
- Jacob E. Goodman, Joseph O'Rourke and Csaba D. Tóth (editors), *Handbook of Discrete and Computational Geometry*, third edition (2017). Its chapter on point location surveys the bounds, including dynamic and higher-dimensional versions.
- Neil Sarnak and Robert E. Tarjan, *Planar Point Location Using Persistent Search Trees*, Communications of the ACM 29(7), 1986. A short paper that introduced a data structuring idea through one geometric problem.
- Olivier Devillers, Sylvain Pion and Monique Teillaud, *Walking in a Triangulation*, International Journal of Foundations of Computer Science 13(2), 2002.
