## History

The formulas for two objects are old. Intersecting a line with a circle is a quadratic equation, and Descartes, in *La Géométrie* of 1637, ran the computation the other way: he solved quadratic equations by cutting a circle with a line. The algorithmic questions came much later, when computational geometry took shape in the 1970s. Michael Shamos and Dan Hoey's paper *Geometric intersection problems*, at FOCS 1976, set the agenda: it gave the sweep that decides in $O(n \log n)$ time whether any two of $n$ segments cross, the divide-and-conquer intersection of half-planes, and the intersection of convex polygons by slabs.

Reporting all crossings followed quickly. Jon Bentley and Thomas Ottmann published their sweep in the IEEE Transactions on Computers in 1979, with crossing points added to the event queue as they are found, and K. Q. Brown noted in 1981 that keeping only the events of current neighbors brings the space down to $O(n)$. The question of an algorithm with time $O(n \log n + k)$ took a decade. Bernard Chazelle reached $O(n \log^2 n / \log \log n + k)$ in 1986, and Chazelle and Herbert Edelsbrunner gave the optimal bound at FOCS 1988, in a journal version of 54 pages (1992). Kenneth Clarkson and Peter Shor (1989) and Ketan Mulmuley (1990) found randomized algorithms with the same expected time, and Ivan Balaban (1995) a deterministic one with linear space. The matching lower bound rests on algebraic decision trees, from Michael Steele and Andrew Yao (1982) and Michael Ben-Or (1983).

Axis-parallel problems have their own line. Jon Bentley and Derick Wood reported intersecting rectangles in optimal worst-case time in 1980. Victor Klee asked in the American Mathematical Monthly in 1977 whether the length of a union of intervals could be found in less than $O(n \log n)$ steps. Michael Fredman and Bruce Weide showed in 1978 that it cannot, and Bentley's sweep solved the plane case in that time. Mark Overmars and Chee Yap reached $O(n^{d/2} \log n)$ in $d$ dimensions in 1988, and Timothy Chan removed the logarithm in 2013.

Convex objects were studied in parallel. Joseph O'Rourke, Chi-Bin Chien, Thomas Olson and David Naddor gave the edge-chasing intersection of convex polygons in 1982. Chazelle and David Dobkin showed at STOC 1980 that detection is easier than computation, with a logarithmic overlap test, and David Muller and Franco Preparata intersected convex polyhedra in $O(n \log n)$ time in 1978, a bound Chazelle brought to $O(n)$ in 1992. In robotics, Elmer Gilbert, Daniel Johnson and Sathiya Keerthi published the distance algorithm now called GJK in 1988, and the OBBTree of Stefan Gottschalk, Ming Lin and Dinesh Manocha (1996) made the fifteen-axis box test standard.

Arrangements of lines were counted by Jakob Steiner in 1826. Edelsbrunner, O'Rourke and Raimund Seidel built them in optimal time at FOCS 1983 through the zone theorem, whose first proof had a gap that Edelsbrunner, Seidel and Micha Sharir closed in 1993. Edelsbrunner and Leonidas Guibas's topological sweep (1989) needs only linear working space. Anka Gajentaan and Overmars showed in 1995 that many problems of this kind, such as finding three collinear points, are as hard as 3SUM.

## Further reading

- Mark de Berg, Otfried Cheong, Marc van Kreveld and Mark Overmars, *Computational Geometry: Algorithms and Applications*, third edition (2008). Chapter 2 develops the Bentley-Ottmann sweep with full care for degenerate cases.
- Franco P. Preparata and Michael Ian Shamos, *Computational Geometry: An Introduction* (1985). The classic account of the intersection problems of the 1970s, with their lower bounds.
- Joseph O'Rourke, *Computational Geometry in C*, second edition (1998). Exact code for segment intersection and convex polygon intersection.
- Herbert Edelsbrunner, *Algorithms in Combinatorial Geometry* (1987). The standard reference for arrangements, the zone theorem and duality.
- Christer Ericson, *Real-Time Collision Detection* (2005). Separating axes, GJK and bounding volumes as used in games and simulation.
