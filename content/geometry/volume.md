Computational geometry is the study of algorithms for points, lines, polygons and the shapes built from them. A map program decides which country a click falls in. A game engine checks, many times a second, whether two objects touch. A chip designer looks for wires that cross. A machine that cuts sheet metal follows the offset of a polygon, and a finite element program first cuts its domain into well-shaped triangles. Each of these is a geometric question with an exact answer, and each has to be answered fast, for inputs with millions of points.

This volume collects the algorithms that answer such questions in the plane, with some excursions into three and more dimensions. It starts with the convex hull, the first shape most geometric programs compute, then turns to intersections, triangulations, Voronoi diagrams and Delaunay triangulations, point location, range searching, proximity and polygons, and ends with geometric optimization.

## Why geometry gets a volume of its own

Geometric algorithms use the general methods of the earlier volumes, but they add problems of their own and a few methods that grew up here. In this volume you will meet:

- **Exact predicates.** Almost every algorithm asks one question again and again: do three points turn left or right? The [orientation test](/orientation-test/) answers it with one determinant. Computed exactly, it makes the algorithm correct; computed with careless rounding, it can make the algorithm loop or crash.
- **Degenerate cases.** Three points on a line, two segments that share an end point, a query point on an edge. Proofs often assume [general position](/general-position/); programs cannot, and the entries say how to handle each case.
- **The plane sweep.** Move a vertical line across the plane and keep only what it currently crosses. The [Shamos–Hoey algorithm](/shamos-hoey-algorithm/) and the [Bentley–Ottmann algorithm](/bentley-ottmann-algorithm/) turn questions about all pairs of segments into a sequence of local tests.
- **Divide and conquer, and prune and search.** The [divide-and-conquer hull](/divide-and-conquer-convex-hull/) merges two halves in linear time; the [Kirkpatrick–Seidel algorithm](/kirkpatrick-seidel/) finds the merge first and recurses after.
- **Randomized incremental construction.** Insert the objects in random order and fix the structure after each one. [Seidel's triangulation](/seidel-polygon-triangulation/), the [trapezoidal map](/trapezoidal-map/) and the [beneath-beyond method](/beneath-beyond/) all work this way, and [backwards analysis](/backwards-analysis/) bounds their expected time.
- **Output-sensitive bounds.** The cost can depend on the size of the answer: $O(n \log h)$ for a hull with $h$ corners, $O((n + k) \log n)$ for $k$ crossing points.
- **Lifting and duality.** Map each point $(x, y)$ to $(x, y, x^2 + y^2)$ on a paraboloid and the [Delaunay triangulation](/delaunay-triangulation/) becomes the lower hull of the lifted points; map points to lines and a question about half-planes becomes one about points. A problem in one chapter often turns into a solved problem of another this way.
- **Lower bounds by reduction.** Sorting reduces to the [convex hull](/convex-hull-lower-bound/), and element distinctness to [segment intersection](/segment-intersection-lower-bound/), so neither can be solved in $o(n \log n)$ time in the algebraic decision tree model.

## The chapters

**Chapter 13.1, Convex hulls**, defines [convex sets](/convex-set/) and the [convex hull problem](/convex-hull-problem/) and gives the classical planar algorithms: [Andrew's monotone chain](/andrew-monotone-chain/), the [Graham scan](/graham-scan/), the [Jarvis march](/jarvis-march/), [quickhull](/quickhull/) and the output-sensitive [Chan's algorithm](/chan-algorithm/). It proves the lower bounds, shows how [rotating calipers](/rotating-calipers/) measures a convex polygon, and ends with the theorems of Carathéodory, Radon and Helly and with hulls in higher dimensions.

**Chapter 13.2, Intersections**, starts with [lines](/line-representation/) and the intersection of [two lines](/line-line-intersection/), [circles](/circle-circle-intersection/) and [segments](/segment-intersection-detection/). It then sweeps: whether any two of $n$ segments cross, and [all crossings](/all-segment-intersections/) in output-sensitive time. It also covers the [intersection of half-planes](/half-plane-intersection/), the [arrangement of lines](/line-arrangement/), and collision tests for convex shapes by the [separating axis theorem](/separating-axis-theorem/) and the [GJK algorithm](/gjk-algorithm/).

**Chapter 13.3, Triangulations**, cuts polygons and point sets into triangles. [Ear clipping](/ear-clipping/) runs in $O(n^2)$ time, [triangulation of monotone polygons](/monotone-polygon-triangulation/) gives $O(n \log n)$, and [Chazelle's algorithm](/chazelle-polygon-triangulation/) reaches $O(n)$. The chapter counts triangulations, studies [edge flips](/edge-flip/) and the [flip graph](/flip-graph/), and treats [mesh generation](/mesh-generation/) with [Ruppert's algorithm](/ruppert-algorithm/).

**Chapter 13.4, Voronoi and Delaunay**, studies the [Voronoi diagram](/voronoi-diagram/), which gives each site the region of points nearest to it, and its dual, the [Delaunay triangulation](/delaunay-triangulation/), whose triangles have empty circumcircles. It proves the structure theorems (the size of the diagram, the [local Delaunay criterion](/local-delaunay-criterion/), the max-min angle property and the [lifting map](/paraboloid-lifting-map/)), builds the triangulation by [Lawson's flips](/lawson-flip-algorithm/), [Bowyer and Watson's cavities](/bowyer-watson-algorithm/) and [divide and conquer](/guibas-stolfi-algorithm/), and builds the diagram by [Fortune's sweep](/fortunes-algorithm/). It then treats constrained and higher-dimensional triangulations, the farthest-point, order-$k$ and [power diagrams](/power-diagram/), and applications from the largest empty circle to [Lloyd's algorithm](/lloyds-algorithm/).

**Chapter 13.5, Point location**, answers the question "which face contains this point?" for a [planar subdivision](/planar-subdivision/). After preprocessing, a query takes $O(\log n)$ time, by the [slab method](/slab-method-point-location/), by a persistent search tree as in [Sarnak and Tarjan](/sarnak-tarjan-point-location/), by [Kirkpatrick's hierarchy](/kirkpatrick-point-location/) or by the randomized [trapezoidal map](/trapezoidal-map/).

**Chapter 13.6, Range searching**, builds structures that report, count or test for emptiness the points inside a query range. For axis-parallel boxes it gives the [range tree](/range-tree/) and its [layered form](/layered-range-tree/), the linear-space [kd-tree](/kd-tree-range-search/) and the [priority search tree](/priority-search-tree/), with offline counting, point enclosure and the logarithmic method for updates. For half-planes, disks and simplices it introduces [range spaces](/range-space/), the [epsilon-net theorem](/epsilon-net-theorem/), [cuttings](/geometric-cutting/) and [partition trees](/partition-tree/), and it ends with the lower bounds that show where the trade-off between space and query time lies.

**Chapter 13.7, Proximity**, starts from distances between points, lines, segments and sets, and takes the [closest pair of points](/closest-pair-of-points/) as its model problem: [divide and conquer](/closest-pair-divide-and-conquer/), a sweep line, a [randomized grid](/randomized-closest-pair/) in expected linear time, and the $\Omega(n \log n)$ lower bound. It goes on to the farthest pair, nearest neighbor queries, and the proximity graphs nested inside the Delaunay triangulation, from the [Euclidean minimum spanning tree](/euclidean-minimum-spanning-tree/) to the [Gabriel graph](/gabriel-graph/). It ends with [geometric spanners](/geometric-spanner/), the [well-separated pair decomposition](/well-separated-pair-decomposition/) and the [Fréchet distance](/frechet-distance/) between curves.

**Chapter 13.8, Polygons**, holds the primitives of the whole volume: [points and vectors](/point-and-vector/), the [cross product](/cross-product/) and the [dot product](/dot-product/). On them it builds the basic polygon algorithms: [area](/shoelace-formula/), [point in polygon](/point-in-polygon/), orientation, [Pick's theorem](/picks-theorem/), clipping and [Minkowski sums](/minkowski-sum/). It ends with visibility: the [art gallery theorem](/art-gallery-theorem/) and [shortest paths inside a polygon](/euclidean-shortest-path-in-polygon/).

**Chapter 13.9, Geometric optimization**, asks for the best shape, point or path. It starts with the [smallest enclosing circle](/minimum-enclosing-circle/) and [Welzl's algorithm](/welzls-algorithm/), and measures a convex polygon by its [antipodal pairs](/antipodal-pairs/): [diameter](/polygon-diameter/), [width](/polygon-width/) and the smallest enclosing rectangle. Two groups of methods follow: linear time in fixed dimension by [prune and search](/prune-and-search/) and [LP-type problems](/lp-type-problem/), and approximation in high dimension by [coresets](/coreset/). It then minimizes sums of distances with the [Fermat point](/fermat-point/), the [geometric median](/geometric-median/) and [Weiszfeld's algorithm](/weiszfelds-algorithm/), builds shortest networks with [Steiner trees](/euclidean-steiner-tree-problem/), finds [shortest paths among obstacles](/euclidean-shortest-path/) through the [visibility graph](/visibility-graph/), and ends with the largest empty rectangle and Heilbronn's triangle problem.

## How to read this volume

Start with the primitives in Chapter 13.8, even though the chapter comes late: every other chapter uses them. If this is your first course, read these entries in this order: [point and vector](/point-and-vector/), [cross product](/cross-product/), [dot product](/dot-product/), [orientation test](/orientation-test/), [the shoelace formula](/shoelace-formula/), [the crossing number algorithm](/crossing-number-algorithm/), [the convex hull problem](/convex-hull-problem/), [Andrew's monotone chain](/andrew-monotone-chain/), [Graham scan](/graham-scan/), [rotating calipers](/rotating-calipers/), [line-line intersection](/line-line-intersection/), [segment intersection detection](/segment-intersection-detection/), [the Shamos–Hoey algorithm](/shamos-hoey-algorithm/), [the Bentley–Ottmann algorithm](/bentley-ottmann-algorithm/), [polygon triangulation](/polygon-triangulation/), [ear clipping](/ear-clipping/), [monotone polygon triangulation](/monotone-polygon-triangulation/) [planar point location](/planar-point-location/), [the closest pair of points](/closest-pair-of-points/), [closest pair by divide and conquer](/closest-pair-divide-and-conquer/), [the Voronoi diagram](/voronoi-diagram/), [the Delaunay triangulation](/delaunay-triangulation/), [the range tree](/range-tree/) and [the kd-tree range search](/kd-tree-range-search/). Do the exercises marked ◦ and • at the end of Chapters 13.1, 13.2, 13.4 and 13.8. That is a complete first unit on computational geometry; Chapters 13.6, 13.7 and 13.9 are the second.

If you already know the basics, start with the opening page of each chapter. It names the groups of the chapter and the order to read them. The entries marked *Advanced* and *Research* are the place to go deeper.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained.

## What you need first

You should be able to read a short program, follow a proof by induction and work with vectors and determinants of size 2 and 3. From Volume 1 you need [asymptotic notation](/asymptotic-notation/) and, for the lower bounds, the [algebraic computation tree](/algebraic-computation-tree/). From Volume 2 you need the [sweep line technique](/sweep-line-technique/) and [randomized incremental construction](/randomized-incremental-construction/). From Volume 3 you need [stacks](/stack/), [deques](/deque/), [priority queues](/priority-queue/) and [balanced binary search trees](/balanced-binary-search-tree/). From Volume 5 you need [binary search](/binary-search/). From Volume 7 you need [planar graphs](/planar-graph/), [Euler's formula](/euler-formula/) and the [doubly connected edge list](/doubly-connected-edge-list/).

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
