## History

The idea of splitting space by nearest centers is old. Descartes drew regions of influence around the stars in 1644, and Dirichlet in 1850 and Voronoi in 1908 defined the cells for the points of a lattice, in the study of quadratic forms. Each field found them again under its own name: Thiessen polygons for rainfall averages in meteorology (1911), and Wigner-Seitz cells for crystals in physics (1933). In 1934 Boris Delaunay published "Sur la sphère vide", which defined the empty sphere criterion and showed that it gives the tiling dual to that of Voronoi.

Shamos and Hoey brought the diagram into algorithms in their 1975 paper "Closest-point problems". They built it in $O(n \log n)$ time by divide and conquer and used it for closest-point questions such as all nearest neighbours. Lawson showed in 1972 that edge flips connect any two triangulations of a point set, and in 1977 he flipped diagonals to raise the smallest angle for surface interpolation; Sibson showed in 1978 that the locally optimal triangulation is the Delaunay one. Lee and Schachter gave a divide and conquer Delaunay algorithm in 1980, and Guibas and Stolfi gave it its standard form in 1985, together with the quad-edge structure. Bowyer and Watson published the cavity method independently in the same issue of The Computer Journal in 1981.

The link with convex hulls came next. Brown showed in 1979 that the diagram can be computed from a convex hull in one dimension higher, through an inversion. The paraboloid became the standard picture, and Edelsbrunner and Seidel extended it in 1986 to diagrams of all orders, which appear as levels of an arrangement. Fortune presented his sweep in 1986 and published it in Algorithmica in 1987; Dwyer's strips, with $O(n \log \log n)$ expected time on uniform points, appeared in the same journal that year. Guibas, Knuth and Sharir analyzed randomized incremental insertion in 1992, and Shewchuk's adaptive predicates of 1997 made exact in-circle tests cheap in practice.

The variants have their own lines. Aurenhammer named and developed the power diagram in 1987, after Imai, Iri and Murota had used it in 1985. Edelsbrunner, Kirkpatrick and Seidel defined alpha shapes in 1983, and Edelsbrunner and Mücke took them to three dimensions in 1994. Chew gave an $O(n \log n)$ algorithm for the constrained Delaunay triangulation in 1987. Lloyd's quantization method dates from a Bell Labs report of 1957, published in 1982, and the survey of Du, Faber and Gunzburger in 1999 made centroidal Voronoi tessellations a standard tool.

## Further reading

- M. de Berg, O. Cheong, M. van Kreveld and M. Overmars, *Computational Geometry: Algorithms and Applications*, third edition, Springer, 2008. Its chapters on Voronoi diagrams and on Delaunay triangulations give Fortune's sweep and the randomized incremental method with full proofs.
- A. Okabe, B. Boots, K. Sugihara and S. N. Chiu, *Spatial Tessellations: Concepts and Applications of Voronoi Diagrams*, second edition, Wiley, 2000. The reference for the many generalized diagrams and for their uses outside computer science.
- S.-W. Cheng, T. K. Dey and J. R. Shewchuk, *Delaunay Mesh Generation*, CRC Press. It covers Delaunay, constrained and weighted triangulations in two and three dimensions, with the refinement algorithms built on them.
- L. Guibas and J. Stolfi, "Primitives for the manipulation of general subdivisions and the computation of Voronoi diagrams", *ACM Transactions on Graphics* 4(2), 1985. The source of the quad-edge structure and of the divide and conquer algorithm in the form most programs follow.
- J. E. Goodman, J. O'Rourke and C. D. Tóth (editors), *Handbook of Discrete and Computational Geometry*, third edition, CRC Press, 2017. Its chapter on Voronoi diagrams and Delaunay triangulations collects the known size bounds and running times.
