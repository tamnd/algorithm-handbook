## History

Unicode 1.0 of 1991 was a 16-bit code. Unicode 2.0 of 1996 added the surrogate pairs of UTF-16, which reach beyond the 65,536 values of 16 bits, and the same version moved the Hangul syllables to their present block, the only time Unicode has moved characters. Ken Thompson and Rob Pike designed UTF-8 in September 1992. Its RFCs first allowed sequences of up to six bytes; RFC 3629 of 2003 limited it to four bytes and the range up to 10FFFF. Normalization, collation, segmentation, line breaking and the bidirectional algorithm grew as annexes of the standard, each revised with every version: the line breaking annex started in Unicode 3.0 with 29 classes, and isolates came to the bidirectional algorithm in Unicode 6.3. Adam Costello's Punycode became RFC 3492 in 2003, and John Keiser and Daniel Lemire published their vector validator of UTF-8 in 2021.

Line breaking as an optimization problem comes from TeX. Donald Knuth and Michael Plass published the box, glue and penalty model, badness, demerits and the total-fit algorithm in "Breaking paragraphs into lines" in 1981, and named the method of filling one line at a time first fit. Plass's thesis of the same year studied pagination with figures and showed that it is NP-complete for two simple badness functions. TeX itself kept a best-fit page builder, described in The TeXbook in 1984 and printed in full in TeX: The Program in 1986, along with the math layout rules. Daniel Hirschberg and Lawrence Larmore named the least weight subsequence problem in 1987 and solved its concave case in $O(n \log n)$ time; Robert Wilber, a coauthor of SMAWK, reduced it to $O(n)$ in 1988.

Franklin Liang, a student of Knuth, replaced the hyphenation rules of the first TeX with patterns in his thesis of 1983. He wrote the program patgen to learn them from a word list, and the packed trie to store them; his TeX82 patterns fill a table of 5943 locations.

The diff algorithms of version control came first from Paul Heckel, who in 1978 matched the lines that occur once in each file, and from Walter Tichy, who in 1984 showed that greedy copying of the longest block gives the fewest block moves. Eugene Myers's $O(ND)$ algorithm of 1986 finds a shortest edit script, and it is the default diff of Git. Bram Cohen described patience diff in 2010, and histogram diff was written for JGit in the same year and later ported to Git. Randy Smith wrote GNU diff3 in 1988, and its properties were studied formally by Sanjeev Khanna, Keshav Kunal and Benjamin Pierce in 2007. Sudarshan Chawathe and his coauthors gave the tree diff of this chapter in 1996, and Sven Apel and his coauthors proposed semistructured merge in 2011.

## Further reading

- The Unicode Standard, Version 16.0.0, with its annexes UAX 9, UAX 14 and UAX 29 and the technical standards UTS 10 and UTS 39: the definitions that the Unicode entries follow, with the conformance tests.
- Donald E. Knuth and Michael F. Plass, "Breaking paragraphs into lines", Software: Practice and Experience 11(11), 1981: the model, the costs and the algorithm of TeX, with many examples; reprinted in Knuth's Digital Typography.
- Donald E. Knuth, The TeXbook: the user's view of glue, penalties, hyphenation, page breaking and math layout, with the parameters of plain TeX.
- Franklin Mark Liang, Word Hy-phen-a-tion by Com-put-er, PhD thesis, Stanford University, 1983: the patterns, the packed trie and patgen, with the measurements quoted in the entries.
- Michael F. Plass, Optimal Pagination Techniques for Automatic Typesetting Systems, PhD thesis, Stanford University, 1981: pagination by dynamic programming and its NP-complete variants.
- Sanjeev Khanna, Keshav Kunal and Benjamin C. Pierce, "A formal investigation of Diff3", FSTTCS 2007: a short and precise account of what three-way merge does and does not guarantee.
