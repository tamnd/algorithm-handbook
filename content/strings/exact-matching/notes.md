## History

James H. Morris found the first linear-time matcher while writing a text editor, and with Vaughan Pratt described it in a Berkeley technical report in 1970; that version is now called [Morris-Pratt](/morris-pratt/). Donald Knuth reached the same algorithm independently, starting from Stephen Cook's theorem that two-way deterministic pushdown automata can be simulated in linear time, and added the stronger failure table and the analysis of the delay. The three published *Fast pattern matching in strings* in the SIAM Journal on Computing in 1977, with an account of this history at its end. In the same year Robert S. Boyer and J Strother Moore published their right-to-left algorithm in the Communications of the ACM, and Ronald Rivest proved that every deterministic matcher must read at least $n - m + 1$ characters of some text.

The analysis of Boyer-Moore took longer than the algorithm. Knuth proved a linear bound when the pattern does not occur, Zvi Galil (1979) added the rule that keeps the search linear when it does, and Leonidas Guibas and Andrew Odlyzko improved the constant. Richard Cole proved the tight bound of about $3n$ comparisons at SODA 1991, with the journal version in 1994. Andrew Yao showed in 1979 that on random text the best average is $\Theta((n/m)\log_\sigma m)$ characters read. Practice moved towards simpler programs: R. Nigel Horspool (1980) kept only the bad-character table, and Daniel Sunday's Quick Search (1990) used the character just after the window.

Through the 1980s and early 1990s, work aimed at the best of both families. Alberto Apostolico and Raffaele Giancarlo (1986) recorded earlier matches to bound Boyer-Moore by $2n$ comparisons; Maxime Crochemore and Thierry Lecroq later proved the tight $1.5n$. Turbo-BM and the reverse factor algorithm, the ancestor of [backward DAWG matching](/backward-dawg-matching/), came from Crochemore, Czumaj, Gasieniec, Jarominek, Lecroq, Plandowski and Rytter, in a journal paper of 1994. Galil and Joel Seiferas showed in 1981 that linear time and constant extra space are possible together, and Crochemore and Dominique Perrin reached the same bounds in 1991 with the [two-way algorithm](/two-way-string-matching/), which entered glibc in 2008.

Other lines started from other models. Richard Karp and Michael Rabin's fingerprint method, published in 1987, is treated in Chapter 8.6. Ricardo Baeza-Yates and Gaston Gonnet introduced Shift-Or in 1992, and Sun Wu and Udi Manber extended it to errors the same year in the agrep tool. Dan Gusfield's book of 1997 made the Z-values the basic preprocessing step of exact matching. New notions of a match keep appearing: Brenda Baker defined parameterized matching in 1993 to find duplicated code, and order-preserving matching was introduced in 2013, independently by Kubica, Kulczyński, Radoszewski, Rytter and Waleń and by Kim and coauthors. Matching in compressed text without decompressing began with Amihood Amir, Gary Benson and Martin Farach in the 1990s.

## Further reading

- Dan Gusfield, *Algorithms on Strings, Trees, and Sequences: Computer Science and Computational Biology* (1997). Part I covers exact matching from the Z-values, with Boyer-Moore, Knuth-Morris-Pratt and Apostolico-Giancarlo, and many exercises.
- Maxime Crochemore and Wojciech Rytter, *Text Algorithms* (1994). The combinatorial side: periods, the constant-space algorithms and the analysis of Boyer-Moore.
- Gonzalo Navarro and Mathieu Raffinot, *Flexible Pattern Matching in Strings* (2002). The practical view, with the bit-parallel and factor-based algorithms and measured running times.
- Christian Charras and Thierry Lecroq, *Exact String Matching Algorithms* (1997). A catalog of the classic exact matchers, each with C code and a worked example.
- Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest and Clifford Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, fourth edition (2022). Chapter 32 gives the naive method, Rabin-Karp, the automaton and Knuth-Morris-Pratt with full proofs.
