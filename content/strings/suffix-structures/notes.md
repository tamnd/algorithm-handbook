## History

Peter Weiner introduced the suffix tree in 1973, under the name position tree, at the Symposium on Switching and Automata Theory, with the first linear-time construction. It read the text from right to left and solved at once a list of problems, among them the longest repeated substring and the longest common substring. Edward McCreight gave a simpler and more economical construction in the Journal of the ACM in 1976; it inserts the suffixes from the longest and introduced the suffix links that later algorithms use. Esko Ukkonen presented an online construction that reads the text from left to right in 1992, published in Algorithmica in 1995. Robert Giegerich and Stefan Kurtz showed in 1997 how closely the three are related. In the same year Martin Farach removed the condition of a constant alphabet with a recursion on the odd positions, and Dan Gusfield's book collected the applications of the tree.

The suffix array came from the need to save space. Udi Manber and Gene Myers presented it at the first ACM-SIAM Symposium on Discrete Algorithms in 1990, with the journal version in the SIAM Journal on Computing in 1993. Their construction used the doubling idea of Karp, Miller and Rosenberg from 1972, and their search used stored LCP values to reach $O(m + \log n)$ time. Gaston Gonnet, Ricardo Baeza-Yates and Tim Snider described the same sorted array, the PAT array, at about the same time. In 2001 Toru Kasai and four coauthors gave the linear-time LCP construction and showed that the two arrays can simulate a walk of the suffix tree; Abouelhoda, Kurtz and Ohlebusch built the enhanced suffix array on that idea in 2004.

Three groups found linear-time suffix array constructions in 2003: Kärkkäinen and Sanders with the skew algorithm, Kim, Sim, Park and Park, and Ko and Aluru. Ge Nong, Sen Zhang and Wai Hong Chan published SA-IS in 2009, and induced sorting is now the basis of the fastest practical suffix sorters. The suffix automaton has a separate line: Anselm Blumer and five coauthors introduced the directed acyclic word graph in 1985, with the size bounds and the online construction, and Maxime Crochemore studied the minimal suffix and factor automata in 1986.

Compression joined the story through the Burrows-Wheeler transform of 1994. In 2000 Paolo Ferragina and Giovanni Manzini showed that the transform with small extra structures is a full-text index, the FM-index, and in the same year Roberto Grossi and Jeffrey Vitter introduced the compressed suffix array, which Kunihiko Sadakane turned into a self-index. The read aligners Bowtie and BWA made the FM-index a standard tool in genomics. Travis Gagie, Gonzalo Navarro and Nicola Prezza gave the r-index, whose size depends on the number of runs of the transform, at SODA 2018.

## Further reading

- Dan Gusfield, *Algorithms on Strings, Trees, and Sequences* (1997). Part II is the classic account of suffix trees, with Ukkonen's algorithm and dozens of applications.
- Simon J. Puglisi, W. F. Smyth and Andrew Turpin, "A taxonomy of suffix array construction algorithms", ACM Computing Surveys (2007). It compares the suffix sorting methods known up to then, in theory and by experiment.
- Enno Ohlebusch, *Bioinformatics Algorithms* (2013). It develops the suffix array, the LCP array and the enhanced suffix array in detail, with the algorithms that replace suffix trees.
- Veli Mäkinen, Djamal Belazzougui, Fabio Cunial and Alexandru I. Tomescu, *Genome-Scale Algorithm Design* (2015). It covers the Burrows-Wheeler index and its uses on genomes.
- Gonzalo Navarro, *Compact Data Structures* (2016). Its chapters on text indexes explain compressed suffix arrays, the FM-index and compressed suffix trees from the ground up.
