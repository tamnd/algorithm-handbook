## History

Compare-and-swap came to mainframes with the IBM System/370 in the 1970s, and the free lists of those machines already kept a counter next to the pointer, so that a reused address would make the CAS fail. Treiber's IBM research report of 1986 gave the lock-free stack that still carries his name. Leslie Lamport had shown in 1983, in *Specifying Concurrent Program Modules*, that a producer and a consumer can share a bounded queue with no mutual exclusion at all.

The theory arrived around 1990. Maurice Herlihy and Jeannette Wing defined [linearizability](/linearizability/) in 1990, in a paper whose array queue is still the standard example of an operation with no fixed linearization point. Afek, Attiya, Dolev, Gafni, Merritt and Shavit gave the wait-free [atomic snapshot](/atomic-snapshot/) at PODC 1990, and Aspnes, Herlihy and Shavit introduced [counting networks](/counting-network/) at STOC 1991. In *Wait-free Synchronization* (1991), Herlihy ranked objects by their [consensus number](/consensus-number/), showed that registers cannot build a wait-free queue, and gave a [universal construction](/universal-construction/) from consensus objects. Together with the impossibility result of Fischer, Lynch and Paterson (1985), this explained why every practical design rests on CAS or LL/SC.

Practical structures followed. Maged Michael and Michael Scott presented their queue at PODC 1996. Arora, Blumofe and Plaxton gave a non-blocking work-stealing deque in 1998, and David Chase and Yossi Lev a simpler deque on a growing circular array in 2005. Timothy Harris gave the marked-pointer linked list at DISC 2001. Michael built a hash table on it in 2002, and Ori Shalev and Nir Shavit made the table extensible with split ordering at PODC 2003. Harris, Fraser and Pratt built a multi-word CAS from single-word CAS in 2002, and Keir Fraser's Cambridge thesis of 2003 gave a lock-free skip list. Herlihy, Luchangco and Moir named [obstruction-freedom](/obstruction-freedom/) in 2003.

Later work went in two directions. One made wait-freedom practical: Alex Kogan and Erez Petrank gave a wait-free queue in 2011, and Ellen, Fatourou, Ruppert and van Breugel a non-blocking binary search tree in 2010. The other questioned whether fine-grained CAS is always best: the elimination stack of Hendler, Shavit and Yerushalmi (2004) lets opposite operations cancel, and flat combining (Hendler, Incze, Shavit and Tzafrir, 2010) gives a hot structure to one thread at a time. Lê, Pop, Cohen and Zappa Nardelli (2013) showed exactly which fences the Chase-Lev deque needs on weak memory.

## Further reading

- Maurice Herlihy, *Wait-free Synchronization*, ACM Transactions on Programming Languages and Systems 13(1) (1991). The consensus hierarchy and the universal construction, the theory behind the whole chapter.
- Maurice Herlihy and Jeannette Wing, *Linearizability: A Correctness Condition for Concurrent Objects*, ACM Transactions on Programming Languages and Systems 12(3) (1990). The definition of correctness used by every entry, with the proof that it is local.
- Maged Michael and Michael Scott, *Simple, Fast, and Practical Non-Blocking and Blocking Concurrent Queue Algorithms*, PODC (1996). A short paper whose queue is still the model for lock-free linked structures.
- Timothy Harris, *A Pragmatic Implementation of Non-Blocking Linked-Lists*, DISC (2001). The marked pointer, the one idea behind every lock-free list, set and skip list here.
- Keir Fraser, *Practical Lock-Freedom*, University of Cambridge technical report UCAM-CL-TR-579 (2004). A lock-free skip list from single-word CAS, with the epoch-based reclamation that frees its nodes.
- Maurice Herlihy, Nir Shavit, Victor Luchangco and Michael Spear, *The Art of Multiprocessor Programming*, second edition, Morgan Kaufmann (2020). The textbook that most entries follow, from registers to skip lists and counting networks.
