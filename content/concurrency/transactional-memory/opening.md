A bank keeps its accounts in a shared hash table, and many threads move money between them. With locks, a transfer from $a$ to $b$ must lock both accounts, always in the same global order, or two transfers in opposite directions can [deadlock](/deadlock/). A transfer that also updates a total, or that calls a library that takes its own locks, makes the order harder to keep. Transactional memory lets the programmer write `atomic { a = a - 10; b = b + 10 }` instead. The system runs the block optimistically, records what it reads and writes, and checks that no other thread touched the same words. If one did, it throws the work away and runs the block again. If none did, both writes appear at once. The programmer states what must be atomic, and the system decides how.

The chapter has three kinds of entries. The correctness conditions say what "appears at once" must mean when transactions can abort halfway and when plain code shares data with them. The software designs show how to build transactions from loads, stores and [compare-and-swap](/compare-and-swap/), and what each design pays for each read. The hardware entries show how a processor runs a transaction in its cache, and why it still needs a software fallback.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups, read in order.

1. **Transactions and conflicts.** The [transaction](/transaction/), its [read set and write set](/read-set-and-write-set/), the [conflict](/transaction-conflict/) between two transactions and the [abort](/transaction-abort/) that resolves it.
2. **What a correct transactional memory promises.** [Opacity](/opacity/), the condition that even doomed transactions see a consistent state, and [weak](/weak-atomicity/) and [strong atomicity](/strong-atomicity/) for code outside transactions.
3. **Building blocks of software TM.** [Software transactional memory](/software-transactional-memory/), the [redo log](/redo-log/) and [lazy versioning](/lazy-versioning/), the [undo log](/undo-log/) and [eager versioning](/eager-versioning/), [eager](/eager-conflict-detection/) and [lazy conflict detection](/lazy-conflict-detection/), and [encounter-time](/encounter-time-locking/) and [commit-time locking](/commit-time-locking/).
4. **Validation and its hazards.** [Validation](/transaction-validation/), the [global version clock](/global-version-clock/), [zombie transactions](/zombie-transactions/) and [privatization safety](/privatization-safety/).
5. **Software TM algorithms.** The [Shavit-Touitou STM](/shavit-touitou-stm/), [DSTM](/dstm/), [TL2](/tl2/), [TinySTM](/tinystm/), [SwissTM](/swisstm/), [NOrec](/norec/), the [multi-version STM](/multi-version-stm/) and the [disjoint-access-parallel lower bound](/disjoint-access-parallel-stm-lower-bound/).
6. **Contention management.** The [contention manager](/contention-management/) and four policies: [Polite](/polite-contention-manager/), [Karma](/karma-contention-manager/), [Polka](/polka-contention-manager/) and [Greedy](/greedy-contention-manager/).
7. **Transactions in hardware.** [Hardware transactional memory](/hardware-transactional-memory/), the [Herlihy-Moss design](/herlihy-moss-htm/), [best-effort HTM](/best-effort-htm/), [Intel TSX](/intel-tsx/), [transactional lock elision](/transactional-lock-elision/) and [hybrid TM](/hybrid-transactional-memory/).
8. **Programming with transactions.** [Nested](/nested-transactions/) and [open nested transactions](/open-nested-transactions/), [retry and orElse](/retry-and-orelse/), [irrevocability](/transaction-irrevocability/), [transactional boosting](/transactional-boosting/) and the [STAMP benchmark suite](/stamp-benchmark-suite/).

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these thirteen entries in order: transaction, read set and write set, transaction conflict, opacity, software transactional memory, redo log, lazy versioning, transaction validation, global version clock, TL2, NOrec, privatization safety and best-effort HTM. They give the model, the condition that real STMs meet, the one write strategy that most of them use, and the two designs that a reader should know by heart. Run TL2 and NOrec by hand on a reader of two words and a writer that changes the second word and commits between the two reads. TL2 aborts the reader on a version number; NOrec checks the values and lets it commit. That one schedule explains most of the trade-off between per-word metadata and a single global lock.

Skip DSTM and the Shavit-Touitou STM on a first pass: they matter for the history and for the progress conditions, but no current system works like them. The contention managers other than Greedy can wait until a workload makes them matter. The lower bound and open nesting are the hardest entries in the chapter and assume the rest. Intel TSX is worth reading for the interface, but check the status of the processor first, since microcode has turned it off on many models.

## Before you start

The safety conditions extend [serializability](/serializability/) from databases, and opacity adds the real-time order of [linearizability](/linearizability/). The STM algorithms use compare-and-swap, [fetch-and-add](/fetch-and-add/) and [load-linked and store-conditional](/load-linked-store-conditional/), and their progress claims use [obstruction-freedom](/obstruction-freedom/), [lock-freedom](/lock-freedom/) and [wait-freedom](/wait-freedom/) from the chapter on lock-free structures. From the chapter on mutual exclusion you need the [critical section](/critical-section/), the [spin lock](/spin-lock/), [livelock](/livelock/) and the [condition variable](/condition-variable/), which retry replaces.

Several entries borrow from databases: [two-phase locking](/two-phase-locking/) for transactional boosting, [multiversion concurrency control](/multiversion-concurrency-control/) for the multi-version STM, and [snapshot isolation](/snapshot-isolation/) for its weaker cousin. The Polite manager uses [exponential backoff](/exponential-backoff/). Look these up when you reach them.
