## History

Mutual exclusion begins in Amsterdam. Th. J. Dekker found the first correct solution for two processes in 1959, using only reads and writes, and Dijkstra reported it and proved it in *Cooperating Sequential Processes* (EWD 123, 1965). In the same year Dijkstra posed the problem for $n$ processes in a one-page paper in Communications of the ACM, "Solution of a problem in concurrent programming control". His solution let a process wait for ever. Knuth (1966) gave the first $n$-process solution without that flaw, de Bruijn (1967) lowered its worst wait, and Eisenberg and McGuire (1972) brought the bound down to $n - 1$ turns. Lamport's bakery algorithm (1974) was the first first-come, first-served solution, and it works even when a read that overlaps a write returns any value.

The second wave asked what the problem costs. Peterson (1981) gave the short two-process algorithm that every textbook now uses, together with its generalization to $n$ processes, the filter lock. Peterson and Fischer (1977) had already built locks from a tree of two-process locks. Burns and Lynch proved that $n$ registers are needed, first at a conference in 1980 and in Information and Computation in 1993; Burns (1981) and Lamport (1986) found the algorithm with $n$ bits that meets the bound. Lamport made the doorway and the FCFS condition precise in 1974 and 1986, and his fast algorithm (1987) takes a constant number of steps when a process runs alone.

Hardware changed the question again. The IBM System/360 had a test-and-set instruction in 1964, but a spinning test-and-set floods the memory system. Rudolph and Segall proposed test-and-test-and-set in 1984. Anderson (1990) measured the spin lock family and gave backoff and an array-based queue lock; Graunke and Thakkar found a similar lock the same year. Mellor-Crummey and Scott (1991) gave the ticket lock in its modern form and the MCS queue lock, which won the Dijkstra Prize in 2006. Craig (1993) and, independently, Magnusson, Landin and Hagersten (1994) gave the CLH lock. With reads and writes alone, Yang and Anderson (1995) reached $O(\log n)$ RMRs per passage, and Attiya, Hendler and Woelfel (2008) proved this optimal. Dice, Marathe and Shavit (2012) built NUMA locks by lock cohorting.

The blocking primitives grew from the same root. Dijkstra introduced semaphores in EWD 123 and solved the bounded buffer and the sleeping barber with them. Brinch Hansen (1973) and Hoare (1974) proposed monitors, and Lampson and Redell (1980) described the Mesa semantics that Java and POSIX follow. Courtois, Heymans and Parnas (1971) posed the readers-writers problem, and Patil (1971) the cigarette smokers.

Deadlock theory came from operating systems. Dijkstra described the deadly embrace and the banker's algorithm in 1965, and Habermann (1969) extended the banker to several resource types. Havender (1968) prevented deadlock in OS/360 by breaking single conditions, including ordered classes of resources. Coffman, Elphick and Shoshani listed the four conditions in "System deadlocks" (1971), and Holt (1972) gave the resource graph and graph reduction. Dijkstra published the dining philosophers in 1971, and Chandy and Misra gave their hygienic solution in 1984.

## Further reading

- Maurice Herlihy and Nir Shavit, *The Art of Multiprocessor Programming*: the filter, bakery and queue locks with proofs in the style of this chapter, and the measured behaviour of spin locks.
- Nancy A. Lynch, *Distributed Algorithms*: the most careful treatment of the shared-memory model, with the Burns-Lynch bound and the classic algorithms proved in full.
- Hagit Attiya and Jennifer Welch, *Distributed Computing: Fundamentals, Simulations and Advanced Topics*: a textbook proof of the register lower bound next to the main algorithms.
- James H. Anderson, Yong-Jik Kim and Ted Herman, "Shared-memory mutual exclusion: major research trends since 1986", Distributed Computing, 2003: a survey of local spinning, RMR bounds and adaptive and fast locks.
- Richard C. Holt, "Some deadlock properties of computer systems", ACM Computing Surveys, 1972: the graph model of deadlock, reduction and its proofs, at the source.
- Abraham Silberschatz, Peter B. Galvin and Greg Gagne, *Operating System Concepts*: the textbook account of semaphores, monitors, the classic problems and the banker's algorithm.
