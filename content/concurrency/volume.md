A concurrent program runs several processes, or threads, that share one memory. Two threads that add 1 to the same counter can lose an update. A web server keeps a cache that hundreds of threads read and a few update, and a reader must never see a half-written entry. A work-stealing scheduler hands tasks between cores millions of times a second, and a lock at that point would cost more than the tasks. A kernel frees a node of a list while another core may still be reading it. In each case the processes run at speeds that nobody controls, the scheduler can stop any of them at any step, and the program must be correct for every interleaving of their steps.

This volume is about the algorithms that coordinate such processes. It starts with locks: how to build mutual exclusion from plain reads and writes and from the atomic instructions of real machines, how to wait without wasting the memory system, and how to avoid deadlock. It then treats data structures that need no locks at all, the problem of freeing memory that other threads may still read, and transactional memory, which lets a programmer mark a block of code as atomic and leaves the rest to the system.

## Why concurrency gets a volume of its own

Sequential algorithms are judged by their steps and their memory. Here the questions change, and a few ideas recur in every chapter:

- **Every page states its model.** The same problem can be easy, costly or impossible depending on three choices. The *primitives*: atomic reads and writes, [test-and-set](/test-and-set/), [fetch-and-add](/fetch-and-add/), swap, [compare-and-swap](/compare-and-swap/) or [load-linked/store-conditional](/load-linked-store-conditional/). The *memory model*: [sequential consistency](/sequential-consistency/) unless the entry says otherwise; on x86 a store waits in a buffer, and [Dekker's algorithm](/dekker-algorithm/) and [Peterson's algorithm](/peterson-algorithm/) fail without a fence. The *progress property*: for locks, [deadlock freedom](/deadlock-freedom/) or [starvation freedom](/starvation-freedom/); without locks, [obstruction freedom](/obstruction-freedom/), [lock freedom](/lock-freedom/) or [wait freedom](/wait-freedom/).
- **Proofs name an invariant or a linearization point.** Mutual exclusion is a safety property, proved by an invariant that every step keeps. A concurrent object is correct when it is [linearizable](/linearizability/): each operation takes effect at one instant between its call and its return, and the proof names that instant, its [linearization point](/linearization-point/), for every operation.
- **Code runs in interleaving simulators, not in threads.** Each process is a Python generator that yields one shared step at a time, and an explorer runs every interleaving of a small case, or a scheduler picks them at random for larger ones. A run with real threads shows only the schedules that the machine happens to choose, and the bad ones are rare. In a simulator the bad schedule can be found, replayed and printed.
- **Primitives have a power.** The [consensus number](/consensus-number/) of a primitive is the largest number of processes for which it solves wait-free consensus. Registers have 1, test-and-set has 2, and compare-and-swap has infinity, so no wait-free algorithm builds compare-and-swap from test-and-set and registers. The [universal construction](/universal-construction/) turns any sequential object into a wait-free one, given consensus.
- **Lower bounds are about space and remote references.** A deadlock-free lock from reads and writes needs $n$ registers. On a multiprocessor the cost that matters is the number of [remote memory references](/rmr-complexity/), and queue locks such as the [MCS lock](/mcs-lock/) bring it down to $O(1)$ per passage.
- **Freeing memory is part of the algorithm.** A lock-free structure removes a node while other threads may still hold a pointer to it. Freeing it too early causes a [use after free](/use-after-free/) or the [ABA problem](/aba-problem/), and never freeing it leaks. [Safe memory reclamation](/safe-memory-reclamation/) is its own problem with its own trade-offs.

## How this volume relates to the others

- **Volume 3, data structures**, has the sequential [stack](/stack/), [queue](/queue/), [linked list](/linked-list/), [skip list](/skip-list/) and [hash table](/hash-table/) that the lock-free structures of Chapter 22.2 copy. Its chapter on concurrent structures has the lock-based versions, such as the [two-lock queue](/two-lock-queue/) and [lock striping](/lock-striping/).
- **Volume 7, graphs**, gives the [directed graphs](/directed-graph/) and [cycle detection](/cycle-detection/) behind the resource and wait-for graphs of deadlock.
- **Volume 16, logic and constraints**, defines [safety](/safety-property/) and [liveness](/liveness-property/) properties and [fairness](/fairness/). Mutual exclusion and linearizability are safety properties; every progress condition of this volume is a liveness property.
- **Volume 20, parallel algorithms**, studies how to split work among processors, with models such as the [PRAM](/pram-model/) that let the processors run in step. Its [work-stealing](/work-stealing/) schedulers keep their tasks in the deques of Chapter 22.2, and its [barriers](/barrier-synchronization/) are one more way to make processes wait for each other.
- **Volume 21, distributed algorithms**, has the [consensus problem](/consensus-problem/) and the [FLP theorem](/flp-impossibility/). The same valency argument shows that registers cannot solve consensus for two processes.
- **Volume 26, systems**, uses locks in schedulers and databases: [priority inversion](/priority-inversion/), [two-phase locking](/two-phase-locking/) and [serializability](/serializability/), which transactional memory borrows, and [reference counting](/reference-counting/), one of the ways to reclaim memory.

## The chapters

**Chapter 22.1, Mutual exclusion**, covers race conditions, deadlock and starvation, the [mutual exclusion problem](/mutual-exclusion-problem/) and its progress properties, the algorithms from reads and writes from Dekker and Peterson to the [bakery algorithm](/lamport-bakery-algorithm/) and the lower bound of $n$ registers, the [spin locks](/spin-lock/) and queue locks of real machines, [semaphores](/semaphore/), [condition variables](/condition-variable/) and [monitors](/monitor/), the classic synchronization problems, and deadlock prevention, avoidance and detection.

**Chapter 22.2, Lock-free structures**, covers [non-blocking algorithms](/non-blocking-algorithm/) and linearizability, the atomic primitives and the three progress conditions, the [Treiber stack](/treiber-stack/) and the [Michael-Scott queue](/michael-scott-queue/), ring buffers and work-stealing deques, lock-free lists, hash tables and skip lists, the consensus hierarchy, helping and the universal construction, and atomic snapshots and counting networks.

**Chapter 22.3, Memory reclamation**, covers the use after free and the ABA problem, tagged pointers and type-stable memory, grace periods and [read-copy-update](/read-copy-update/), [epoch-based reclamation](/epoch-based-reclamation/), [hazard pointers](/hazard-pointers/), the schemes based on eras and intervals, lock-free reference counting, and schemes that restart readers instead of protecting them.

**Chapter 22.4, Transactional memory**, covers the [transaction](/transaction/) and its conflicts, [opacity](/opacity/) and the atomicity of code outside transactions, the building blocks of [software transactional memory](/software-transactional-memory/), validation and its hazards, the main STM algorithms such as [TL2](/tl2/), contention managers, [hardware transactional memory](/hardware-transactional-memory/) with [lock elision](/transactional-lock-elision/), and nesting and composition of transactions.

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course on concurrency, read these entries in this order. From Chapter 22.1: the [race condition](/race-condition/), the mutual exclusion problem, Peterson's algorithm, the bakery algorithm, the spin lock, the MCS lock, the semaphore and [lock ordering](/lock-ordering/). From Chapter 22.2: linearizability, compare-and-swap, lock freedom, the Treiber stack, the Michael-Scott queue and the consensus number. From Chapter 22.3: the ABA problem, hazard pointers and epoch-based reclamation. From Chapter 22.4: the transaction, opacity and TL2. Do the exercises marked ◦ and • at the end of these chapters. That is a complete first unit on concurrency.

Read the chapters in order. Chapter 22.1 sets the model that the rest uses. Chapter 22.3 frees the nodes of the structures of Chapter 22.2, and Chapter 22.4 uses the locks of Chapter 22.1 and the linearizability and compare-and-swap of Chapter 22.2. If you already know the basics, start with the opening page of each chapter. It names the groups in the chapter and the order to read them.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained. Entries in other volumes are cited by number too.

## What you need first

You should be able to read pseudocode and follow a proof by invariant and a proof by contradiction. From Volume 1 you need [asymptotic notation](/asymptotic-notation/). From Volume 3 you need linked lists, stacks, queues and hash tables, and from Volume 7 directed graphs and cycle detection. The notions of safety, liveness and fairness from Volume 16 help throughout. Some idea of how a processor runs instructions, with caches and a store buffer, helps with the spin locks and the memory model, but every entry that needs it explains what it uses.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
