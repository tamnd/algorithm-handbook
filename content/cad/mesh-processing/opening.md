A scanner returns a million points with triangles between them, and the file has holes where the light did not reach. A CAD system exports a part as an STL file, in which every triangle lists its three corners again, so a cube has 36 points instead of 8. A printer needs a closed surface with a clear inside, a simulation needs triangles of good shape, an animator needs to bend the surface by a few handles, and a texture needs the surface laid flat. Mesh processing is the work between these steps: store and compress the mesh, clean it, check it, smooth it, measure it, deform it, cut it, flatten it and build a better one.

Most of the chapter works on a triangle mesh with flat faces, so every predicate is a sign of a polynomial in the coordinates, and exact rational arithmetic is possible. The guarantees come in five kinds, and the pages keep them apart. Some are identities that hold for every mesh: the angle defects of a closed mesh sum to $2\pi\chi$, a Tutte map with a convex boundary and positive weights is one to one, and Edgebreaker codes a genus 0 mesh in exactly $2t - 1$ bits. Some are proved in exact arithmetic and can fail with floating point predicates, which is the [robustness problem](/geometric-robustness-problem/): the triangle-triangle test, exact Booleans, and the exact geodesic methods. Some are global optima of a relaxed or convex problem: discrete conformal equivalence has a unique solution when one exists, and the globally optimal direction field is optimal only for its relaxed problem. Some are approximations with a stated model, such as the heat method, which converges as the mesh is refined, or Metro, whose estimate is too small by at most the sample spacing. The rest is local or measured: ARAP and variational shape approximation never raise their energy but stop at a local minimum, and smoothing quality, remeshing quality, segmentation and the shape of a filled hole are measured on examples, not proved.
