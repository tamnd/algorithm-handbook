## History

The counting side of the subject is older than computers. Maxwell gave the rule in 1864 that a stiff plane frame of $n$ joints needs $2n - 3$ bars, and $3n - 6$ in space, and saw that the count can fail at special positions. Henneberg described in 1911 how plane trusses are built up joint by joint. Pollaczek-Geiringer proved in 1927 that the plane count, applied to every subset of joints, characterizes generic minimal rigidity, and Laman proved it again in 1970. Distance geometry has its own line: Cayley wrote the volume determinant in 1841, Menger used it in 1928 to characterize metric spaces that embed in $\mathbb{R}^d$, and Schoenberg gave the Gram matrix test in 1935.

Solving constraints on a screen began with Sutherland's Sketchpad in 1963, which ordered the variables in one pass when it could and fell back on relaxation when it could not. Lin, Gossard and Light in 1981, and Light and Gossard in 1982, solved dimension equations by Newton-Raphson iteration and used the Jacobian to find missing and redundant dimensions. Kramer's book of 1992 solved assemblies by degrees of freedom analysis. Rule-based solvers wrote a draftsman's steps as rules: Sunde in 1988, Verroust, Schonek and Roller in 1992, and Brüderlin, who proved his rewrite rules complete in 1993.

Rigidity theory grew into a theory of matroids and graphs. Asimow and Roth showed in 1978 that rigidity and infinitesimal rigidity agree at generic placements, and Lovász and Yemini gave the matroid view of the plane counts in 1982. Tay proved in 1984 that body-and-bar frameworks are generically rigid exactly when the multigraph contains $D = d(d + 1)/2$ edge-disjoint spanning trees. Jacobs and Hendrickson published the pebble game in 1997, and Lee and Streinu extended it to all $(k, \ell)$ counts in 2008.

The decomposition solvers of CAD came at the same time. Owen in 1991 split the constraint graph at separation pairs, and Fudos and Hoffmann published the bottom-up merge of clusters and its quadratic bound in 1997; Joan-Arinyo and coauthors proved in 2002 that the two methods solve the same graphs. Hoffmann, Lomonosov and Sitharam found dense sets by network flow in 1997, and in 2001 defined DR-plans and gave the Frontier algorithm. Michelucci and Foufou proposed in 2006 to test each step at a random witness configuration.

The question of unique placement came last. Saxe showed in 1979 that placing a weighted graph is NP-complete on the line and strongly NP-hard in every dimension. Hendrickson gave the necessary conditions for generic global rigidity in 1992, and Connelly found that $K_{5,5}$ in space meets them and is not globally rigid. Jackson and Jordán proved in 2005 that the conditions suffice in the plane. Connelly's sufficient condition by stress matrices, published in 2005, was shown to be necessary by Gortler, Healy and Thurston in a paper posted in 2007 and published in 2010.

## Further reading

- Jack Graver, Brigitte Servatius and Herman Servatius, *Combinatorial Rigidity*, American Mathematical Society, 1993. Frameworks, infinitesimal rigidity, the rigidity matroid and the plane counts.
- Meera Sitharam, Audrey St. John and Jessica Sidman (editors), *Handbook of Geometric Constraint Systems Principles*, CRC Press, 2018. The whole field, from decomposition plans to rigidity, stresses and solution counts.
- Glenn A. Kramer, *Solving Geometric Constraint Systems: A Case Study in Kinematics*, MIT Press, 1992. Degrees of freedom analysis for assemblies of rigid bodies.
- Ioannis Fudos and Christoph M. Hoffmann, "A graph-constructive approach to solving systems of geometric constraints", *ACM Transactions on Graphics* 16(2), 1997. The cluster-merging solver, its proof, its running time and root identification.
- Christoph M. Hoffmann, Andrew Lomonosov and Meera Sitharam, "Decomposition plans for geometric constraint systems, Parts I and II", *Journal of Symbolic Computation* 31(4), 2001. DR-plans, the measures that compare planners, and the Frontier algorithm.
- Leo Liberti, Carlile Lavor, Nelson Maculan and Antonio Mucherino, "Euclidean distance geometry and applications", *SIAM Review* 56(1), 2014. Cayley-Menger determinants, Gram matrices, hardness and the main solution methods.
