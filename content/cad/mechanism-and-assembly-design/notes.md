## History

Kinematics became its own subject in the nineteenth century. Willis wrote *Principles of Mechanism* in 1841 and gave the rule for epicyclic trains that carries his name. Reuleaux treated machines as chains of pairs in 1875. Aronhold in 1872 and Kennedy in 1886 found the three-centers theorem on their own, and Grashof gave his rotatability condition in 1883. The search for an exact straight line ended when Peaucellier's inversor, posed in 1864, was rebuilt by Lipkin in 1871. Roberts in 1875 and Chebyshev in 1878 found that three four-bars trace the same coupler curve. Kempe claimed in 1876 that linkages can draw any algebraic curve. His argument had gaps, which were closed more than a century later.

Synthesis began with Burmester, whose 1888 *Lehrbuch der Kinematik* gave the geometry of four and five positions. It moved to algebra and computers in the 1950s. Hrones and Nelson printed an atlas of coupler curves in 1951, Freudenstein published his equation in 1954, and Freudenstein and Sandor ran synthesis on a digital computer in 1959. Roth and Freudenstein attacked the nine-point path problem in 1963 with an early form of continuation. Wampler, Morgan and Sommese solved it numerically in 1992 and found 1442 coupler curves, each traced by three cognate linkages. Optimization over many points grew alongside, from least squares fits in the 1960s to the evolutionary methods used now.

Gear theory is older. Camus showed in 1733 that conjugate teeth must satisfy what is now called the fundamental law of gearing, and Euler proposed the involute in the middle of the eighteenth century. The involute won because a shift of the centers does not change its ratio, and because one straight-sided rack can generate every gear of a given module and pressure angle. The practical limits on contact ratio, undercut and pressure angle come from shop experience and standards rather than from theorems.

Assembly planning is the youngest part. Bourjault's 1984 thesis turned a liaison graph into precedence questions. De Fazio and Whitney shortened the list of questions in 1987, and Homem de Mello and Sanderson set out the AND/OR graph of plans in papers of 1986 to 1991. Geometry then entered. Wilson and Latombe built the non-directional blocking graph in 1994, while Kavraki, Latombe and Wilson in 1993 and Kavraki and Kolountzakis in 1995 showed that partitioning becomes NP-complete in the plane once motions are richer or both parts must be connected. Halperin, Latombe and Wilson unified these planners in the motion space framework of 2000. Design for assembly, led by Boothroyd and Dewhurst, scores the parts of a design and asks which of them could be removed.

## Further reading

- Oene Bottema and Bernard Roth, *Theoretical Kinematics* (1979). The classic treatment of displacement poles, Burmester theory and the algebra of planar and spatial motion.
- J. Michael McCarthy and Gim Song Soh, *Geometric Design of Linkages*, second edition (2011). Precision-point synthesis from dyads to spatial chains, with the polynomial systems written out.
- Robert L. Norton, *Design of Machinery*. A textbook that covers Grashof, loop closure, instant centers, cams and gear trains with many worked numbers.
- Faydor L. Litvin and Alfonso Fuentes, *Gear Geometry and Applied Theory*, second edition (2004). The reference for conjugate surfaces, generation by enveloping and undercutting.
- Daniel E. Whitney, *Mechanical Assemblies* (2004). Liaisons, precedence, the De Fazio-Whitney method and design for assembly from the engineering side.
- Dan Halperin, Jean-Claude Latombe and Randall H. Wilson, "A general framework for assembly planning: the motion space approach", *Algorithmica* (2000). The geometric planners, from blocking graphs to general motions, in one setting.
