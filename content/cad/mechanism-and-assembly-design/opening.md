Take four bars with lengths 2, 7, 5 and 6, fix the bar of length 6 to the ground, and pin the others into a loop. When a motor turns the short bar through full turns, the bar of length 5 rocks back and forth through $56.34°$. It swings out while the crank turns $201.54°$ and back while it turns $158.46°$, so the return is $1.27$ times faster than the outward stroke. The [Grashof condition](/grashof-condition/) $2 + 7 \le 5 + 6$ says that the short bar can turn fully. A quadratic from the [vector loop](/vector-loop-closure/) gives the output angle for each crank angle, and the [transmission angle](/transmission-angle/) between coupler and output ranges from $34.05°$ to $81.79°$, a little below the usual lower limit of $40°$. Mechanism design asks this in both directions: from the bars to the motion, and from a wanted motion to the bars.

The first and largest of the chapter's three parts is about linkages. Analysis gives the positions, the velocities and the curves that coupler points trace. Synthesis finds the lengths that give a wanted function, path or sequence of positions. A four-bar has only a few free lengths, so it can meet a task exactly only at a few precision points. [Freudenstein's equation](/freudenstein-equation/) makes three such points a linear system. Burmester theory handles up to five positions of a rigid body, and the nine-point path problem has 1442 solution curves, found numerically. Beyond the exact counts, synthesis is optimization, which finds local optima.

The second part is cams and gears. A cam gives the follower any motion law the designer writes down, and the law fixes the peak velocity, acceleration and jerk. The pressure angle then fixes how large the cam must be. Gear teeth must keep a constant speed ratio. The [fundamental law of gearing](/fundamental-law-of-gearing/) says when they do, and the involute keeps the ratio exact even when the shafts move apart. Then the contact ratio, the undercut limit and the [Willis formula](/willis-formula/) size the teeth and the trains.

The third part is assembly. A product of $n$ parts has $n!$ orders of assembly, and most of them fail because a part is closed in before it can be placed. The planners store all feasible plans in a graph with at most one node per connected set of parts, and test each step by asking which sets of parts can move apart along which motions. For one translation that test takes polynomial time. For richer motions, deciding whether any subassembly can be removed is NP-complete in the plane.

The chapter keeps three kinds of guarantee apart. The theorems of plane kinematics, such as the Grashof condition, the cognates and the precision-point equations, are exact. A transmission angle between $40°$ and $140°$, a cam pressure angle below $30°$ and a contact ratio above $1.2$ are rules of practice. The assembly planners are exact only for their motion model: a plan that needs a turn of a part is invisible to a planner that allows only translations.

## A first reading

Take these twelve entries in order: the four-bar linkage, the Grashof condition, vector loop closure, the instant center of velocity, the coupler curve, kinematic synthesis, Freudenstein's equation, cam follower motion laws, the fundamental law of gearing, the involute gear tooth, the liaison graph and the non-directional blocking graph. They cover analysis, the synthesis tasks, the cam, the tooth and the polynomial test for one step of an assembly plan.

Leave for later the advanced and research entries: Burmester theory, nine-point synthesis, Kempe's theorem, gear generation by enveloping and the motion space approach. They lean on algebraic geometry, envelopes or arrangements.
