Take the cube $[0, 2]^3$ and cut away the cube $[1, 3]^3$ that overlaps one of its corners. A designer writes this as one line, a difference of two boxes, and a [CSG tree](/constructive-solid-geometry/) stores exactly that. A machine tool, a renderer or a finite element program needs more: the faces of the result, here 24 unit squares on nine planes, and how they meet. The volume is $8 - 1 = 7$, the center of mass is $(13/14, 13/14, 13/14)$, and the three new faces meet along concave edges. Turning the one-line description into these faces and numbers, correctly and for every input a designer can type, is the subject of solid modeling, and this chapter gives its algorithms.

The first question is what a solid is. Requicha's answer, the [r-set](/r-set/), is a bounded, closed set that equals the closure of its interior and has a finite description. The ordinary intersection of two r-sets can be a face or an edge with no volume, so modelers use the [regularized set operations](/regularized-set-operations/), which take the closure of the interior of the result and drop such dangling pieces. Then come the representations: trees of operations, [boundary representations](/boundary-representation/) with faces, edges and vertices, cells on a grid, and functions whose sign tells inside from outside. Each one makes some questions easy and others hard, and much of the chapter converts one into another.

The second theme is the Boolean operation on boundaries. Its core is [set membership classification](/set-membership-classification/): split a point set into the parts inside, on and outside a solid. With planar faces this is exact arithmetic on small determinants. With curved faces, the new edges are the curves where two [parametric surfaces](/parametric-surface/) meet, and computing them is a numerical problem with its own failures: a method that traces a curve misses a small closed loop unless it adds a loop test, and a method that subdivides finds every piece but stops at a tolerance. The chapter keeps these guarantees apart.

The third theme is robustness. The algorithms are proved on the [real RAM](/real-ram/), where every number is exact. Run with floating point, an [orientation test](/orientation-test/) can say that $p, q, r$ turn left and that $q, r, p$ turn right, and a Boolean operation built on such answers can produce a boundary that is not closed. The [geometric robustness problem](/geometric-robustness-problem/) entry shows the failures on small inputs, and the cures are exact computation of the signs and a symbolic perturbation that removes degenerate cases. Every guarantee in the chapter names its model: exact or rounded, general position or degenerate input, proved or measured.

The chapter ends with things a designer uses every day: offsets and fillets, mass properties, and features such as holes and pockets. Here many results are measured rather than proved. A feature recognizer is judged on test parts, and no naming scheme for faces is known to survive every edit.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups, read in order.

1. **Solids and their representations.** R-sets, [manifold solids](/manifold-solid/), CSG, B-reps, [spatial occupancy enumeration](/spatial-occupancy-enumeration/), [function representation](/function-representation/), and the [conversion from B-rep to CSG](/brep-to-csg-conversion/).
2. **Boundary data structures and their topology.** The [winged edge](/winged-edge-data-structure/), the [radial edge](/radial-edge-data-structure/) for non-manifold models, the [Euler-Poincaré formula](/euler-poincare-formula/) and the [Euler operators](/euler-operators/).
3. **Classification and Boolean operations.** Set membership classification, [CSG ray casting](/csg-ray-casting/), [boundary evaluation](/boundary-evaluation/), [Boolean operations on B-reps](/solid-boolean-operations/) and [vertex neighborhood classification](/vertex-neighborhood-classification/).
4. **Curved faces and their intersections.** [Trimmed surfaces](/trimmed-surface/), [surface-surface intersection](/surface-surface-intersection/), and its two families of methods, [marching](/marching-surface-intersection/) and [subdivision](/subdivision-surface-intersection/).
5. **Offsets and blends.** [Offset surfaces](/offset-surface/), [blending surfaces](/blending-surface/), the [rolling-ball blend](/rolling-ball-blend/), [solid offsetting](/solid-offsetting/) and the [method of Rossignac and Requicha](/rossignac-requicha-offsetting/).
6. **Robustness.** The robustness problem, [exact geometric computation](/exact-geometric-computation/) and [simulation of simplicity](/simulation-of-simplicity/).
7. **Mass properties.** [Mass properties of solids](/mass-properties-of-solids/) and [Mirtich's algorithm](/mirtich-polyhedral-mass-properties/) for polyhedra.
8. **Features and naming.** [Feature-based modeling](/feature-based-modeling/), [feature recognition](/feature-recognition/), the [attributed adjacency graph](/attributed-adjacency-graph/) and the [persistent naming problem](/persistent-naming-problem/).

## A first reading

Take these ten entries in order: r-set, regularized set operations, constructive solid geometry, boundary representation, the Euler-Poincaré formula, set membership classification, boundary evaluation, surface-surface intersection, the geometric robustness problem and mass properties of solids. They give the definition of a solid, the two main representations, the counting rule for valid boundaries, the classification that drives every Boolean operation, the hard numerical step for curved faces, the reason the proofs do not survive floating point, and the first thing a user asks of a finished model.

Leave for later the advanced entries in groups 5 and 8 and the research entries, B-rep to CSG conversion and persistent naming. They assume the first reading and state fewer theorems.
