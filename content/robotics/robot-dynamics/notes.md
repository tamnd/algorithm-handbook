## History

The mechanics behind the chapter is old. Newton stated the laws of motion in the Principia of 1687. Euler worked on the rotation of rigid bodies for decades, and in his paper Nova methodus motum corporum rigidorum determinandi, published in 1776, he stated the balance of linear momentum and the balance of angular momentum as two separate laws, the Newton-Euler equations of a body. Gauss published his principle of least constraint in 1829, and Painlevé pointed out in 1895 that rigid bodies with Coulomb friction can have no consistent motion.

Robot dynamics as a field of computation began when arms had to be controlled in real time. The Lagrangian equations in the form of Uicker and Kahn cost about $n^4$ operations, too slow for the computers of the time. In 1980 Luh, Walker and Paul computed the joint torques by recursive Newton-Euler equations, and Hollerbach gave a recursive Lagrangian form and compared the costs of the formulations; both grow linearly in $n$. Walker and Orin compared four methods for forward dynamics in 1982, and their third, the composite rigid body method, was the fastest. Featherstone introduced articulated-body inertias in 1983 and solved forward dynamics in time linear in $n$. His book of 2008 put these algorithms, with spatial vectors, in one notation. Kane and Levinson showed the use of Kane's equations in robotics in 1983.

The structure of the equations then became a tool for control. Takegaki and Arimoto proved in 1981 that PD control with gravity compensation brings an arm to rest at its target, using only the energy balance. Khatib's operational space formulation of 1987 wrote the dynamics in the coordinates of the task, and Featherstone and Khatib proved in 1997 that the dynamically consistent inverse does not depend on the load. Spong gave the reduced model of robots with elastic joints in 1987. On the side of parameters, Khosla and Kanade wrote the equations linearly in the inertial parameters in 1985, Atkeson, An and Hollerbach estimated the parameters of loads and links in 1986, and in 1990 Gautier and Khalil, and Mayeda, Yoshida and Osuka, found the base parameters that data can identify.

Legged robots and simulators shaped the last part of the chapter. Stewart and Trinkle wrote contact with friction in impulses over a time step in 1996, so that each step is a linear complementarity problem. Anitescu and Potra proved in 1997 that the velocity form always has a solution, and Stewart proved in 1998 that the scheme converges as the step shrinks, which resolves Painlevé's problem. Udwadia and Kalaba gave the closed form of Gauss's principle in 1992. Orin and Goswami named the centroidal momentum matrix in 2008, and Carpentier and Mansard computed the analytical derivatives of the recursive Newton-Euler algorithm in 2018.

## Further reading

- Roy Featherstone, *Rigid Body Dynamics Algorithms* (Springer, 2008). The reference for spatial vectors and for the recursive algorithms, with exact operation counts and the sparse factorization of $M$.
- Kevin M. Lynch and Frank C. Park, *Modern Robotics: Mechanics, Planning, and Control* (Cambridge University Press, 2017). A textbook that derives the dynamics with twists and wrenches, close to the notation of this volume.
- Mark W. Spong, Seth Hutchinson and M. Vidyasagar, *Robot Modeling and Control*, second edition (Wiley, 2020). The Euler-Lagrange derivation, the properties of the manipulator equation and the flexible joint model, aimed at control.
- Bruno Siciliano, Lorenzo Sciavicco, Luigi Villani and Giuseppe Oriolo, *Robotics: Modelling, Planning and Control* (Springer, 2009). A broad textbook with the operational space formulation and identification.
- David E. Stewart, "Rigid-body dynamics with friction and impact", *SIAM Review* 42(1), 2000. A survey of contact models, the Painlevé paradox and time stepping.
