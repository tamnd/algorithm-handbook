A mobile robot in a warehouse must take a box from a shelf and bring it to a packing table. A camera reports the pose of the box as a rotation and a translation. Inverse kinematics turns the pose of the gripper into six joint angles, and the manipulator equation gives the torques that move the arm along a timed trajectory without exceeding the motor limits. Two fingers squeeze the box, and the grasp holds only if the contact forces stay inside their friction cones. The base, which cannot move sideways, drives a path to the table while a particle filter tracks its pose against a map built earlier. Three other robots use the same aisles, and they agree on who goes first. Each step rests on a model: of rotations, of the arm, of the sensors, of friction. The robot is never exactly the model.

This volume is about those algorithms and their theorems. Its eight chapters hold 264 entries. The first four chapters are about one robot arm: how to describe rotations and poses, how joint angles place the hand, how torques move the joints, and how to time a motion. The last four go out into the world: locating a robot and mapping its surroundings, grasping and pushing objects, wheeled and legged locomotion, and teams of robots.

## Why robotics gets a volume of its own

Robotics uses the linear algebra of Volume 12, the optimization of Volume 15, the filters of Volumes 25 and 28, the motion planners of Volume 29 and the feedback design of Volume 36. What it adds is a body that moves in three-dimensional space, touches things and does not know exactly where it is. A few ideas come back in every chapter:

- **Rotations are not vectors.** The rotations form the group [SO(3)](/special-orthogonal-group/), not a vector space. By [Stuelpnagel's theorem](/stuelpnagel-theorem/), every chart of rotations by three numbers has a singular or a repeated point, which is why [Euler angles](/euler-angles/) suffer [gimbal lock](/gimbal-lock/). [Unit quaternions](/unit-quaternion-rotation/) have no singularity but cover each rotation twice. Poses and their [uncertainties](/uncertainty-on-lie-groups/) are handled through the exponential and logarithm maps.
- **Exact, local or probabilistic.** Closed-form inverse kinematics exists for arms that meet [Pieper's criterion](/pieper-criterion/), and a general 6R arm has [at most 16 solutions](/six-r-inverse-kinematics-sixteen-solutions/). Numerical methods such as [Newton-Raphson](/newton-raphson-inverse-kinematics/) and [damped least squares](/damped-least-squares-inverse-kinematics/) are local: they can miss a solution that exists. The [recursive Newton-Euler algorithm](/recursive-newton-euler-algorithm/) is exact for the rigid-body model. Sampling planners such as [RRT](/rapidly-exploring-random-tree/) are probabilistically complete only.
- **Estimates are approximate.** [EKF SLAM](/ekf-slam/) linearizes and can become overconfident. [Pose graph optimization](/pose-graph-optimization/) is a nonlinear least squares problem whose iterations can stop at a local minimum. [SE-Sync](/se-sync/) certifies a global optimum, but only when its semidefinite relaxation is tight.
- **Contact changes the equations.** At a contact either the gap or the normal force is zero, so the [rigid contact model](/rigid-contact-complementarity/) is a complementarity problem. With Coulomb friction it can have no solution or several, the [Painlevé paradox](/painleve-paradox/). The [force-closure test](/force-closure-test/) is exact for linearized friction cones, which approximate the real cones.
- **Teams need conditions.** The [Laplacian consensus protocol](/laplacian-consensus-protocol/) converges to the average on a connected undirected graph. [Prioritized planning](/prioritized-multi-robot-planning/) is fast but incomplete, while the feasibility of [pebble motion on graphs](/pebble-motion-on-graphs/) is decidable in polynomial time.

[Configuration space](/configuration-space/) and the general [motion planning](/motion-planning/) problem live in Volume 29. The [extended Kalman filter](/extended-kalman-filter/) and the [particle filter](/particle-filter/) come from Volume 25, and the [Kalman filter](/kalman-filter/) from Volume 28. [Nonlinear least squares](/nonlinear-least-squares/) is in Volume 38, and the [matrix exponential](/matrix-exponential/) and [Lyapunov functions](/lyapunov-function/) are in Volume 36.

## The chapters

**Chapter 37.1, Rigid body motion**, describes rotations by matrices, [Rodrigues' formula](/rodrigues-rotation-formula/), angles and quaternions, and converts a matrix to a quaternion stably by [Shepperd's method](/shepperd-method/). [Chasles' theorem](/chasles-theorem/) says that every rigid motion is a [screw motion](/screw-motion/). [Twists](/rigid-body-twist/), [wrenches](/wrench/) and the [adjoint map](/adjoint-map-se3/) carry velocities and forces between frames, and the [exponential map of SE(3)](/se3-exponential-map/) turns a twist into a pose.

**Chapter 37.2, Robot kinematics**, computes the pose of the hand from the joint angles by the [Denavit-Hartenberg convention](/denavit-hartenberg-convention/) or the [product of exponentials](/product-of-exponentials-formula/). The [geometric Jacobian](/geometric-jacobian/) maps joint rates to hand velocity, loses rank at [singularities](/kinematic-singularity/) and maps hand forces back to torques by [duality](/jacobian-statics-duality/). [Inverse kinematics](/inverse-kinematics/) is solved in closed form by [Paden-Kahan subproblems](/paden-kahan-subproblems/), algebraically by the [Raghavan-Roth method](/raghavan-roth-method/), or locally by iteration.

**Chapter 37.3, Robot dynamics**, derives the [manipulator equation](/manipulator-equation/) and its two properties used in control, [skew symmetry](/skew-symmetry-of-manipulator-dynamics/) and [linearity in the parameters](/linearity-in-dynamic-parameters/). With [spatial vectors](/spatial-vector-algebra/), the recursive Newton-Euler algorithm solves inverse dynamics and the [articulated-body algorithm](/articulated-body-algorithm/) forward dynamics, both in $O(n)$ time. [Centroidal dynamics](/centroidal-dynamics/) serves legged robots, and [Stewart-Trinkle time stepping](/stewart-trinkle-time-stepping/) simulates contact with impulses.

**Chapter 37.4, Trajectory generation and optimization**, starts with [polynomials](/quintic-polynomial-trajectory/) and the [trapezoidal velocity profile](/trapezoidal-velocity-profile/), which is time-optimal for one axis under velocity and acceleration limits. [Time-optimal path parameterization](/time-optimal-path-parameterization/) finds the fastest timing along a fixed path by [Bobrow's algorithm](/bobrow-time-optimal-algorithm/) or by [reachability analysis](/topp-reachability-analysis/). [CHOMP](/chomp/), [STOMP](/stomp/) and [control-limited DDP](/control-limited-ddp/) optimize path and timing together, and find local solutions.

**Chapter 37.5, Localization and SLAM**, builds on the [Bayes filter](/bayes-filter/). [Monte Carlo localization](/monte-carlo-localization/) represents the pose by particles. [EKF SLAM](/ekf-slam/) keeps one Gaussian over the robot and the map, with an update that costs $O(m^2)$ for $m$ landmarks. [FastSLAM](/fastslam/) factors the problem over robot paths, and graph methods from [GraphSLAM](/graphslam/) to [iSAM2](/isam2/) solve the [full SLAM](/full-slam/) problem by sparse least squares.

**Chapter 37.6, Grasping and manipulation**, defines [form closure](/form-closure/) and [force closure](/force-closure/) through the [grasp matrix](/grasp-matrix/) and friction cones, and measures grasps by the [Ferrari-Canny metric](/ferrari-canny-metric/). [Goldberg's algorithm](/goldberg-part-orienting-algorithm/) orients a polygonal part by squeezes without sensors, in polynomial time. The chapter also holds the controllers of an arm in contact: [computed-torque control](/computed-torque-control/), [impedance control](/impedance-control/) and [image-based visual servoing](/image-based-visual-servoing/).

**Chapter 37.7, Mobile and legged robots**, models wheeled vehicles by the [unicycle](/unicycle-model/) and the [kinematic bicycle](/kinematic-bicycle-model/) under [nonholonomic constraints](/nonholonomic-constraint/). The shortest forward path of a car is one of six [Dubins words](/dubins-path/). With reversing, Reeds and Shepp gave 48 [candidate words](/reeds-shepp-path/), later reduced to 46. [Pure pursuit](/pure-pursuit/) tracks a path and [hybrid A*](/hybrid-a-star/) plans one. For walking, the [zero moment point](/zero-moment-point/) and the [capture point](/capture-point/) are exact only for their models.

**Chapter 37.8, Multi-robot systems**, starts from [consensus](/laplacian-consensus-protocol/) and [formation control](/formation-control/). Tasks are shared by [market-based allocation](/market-based-task-allocation/) and the [consensus-based bundle algorithm](/consensus-based-bundle-algorithm/), and [optimal reciprocal collision avoidance](/optimal-reciprocal-collision-avoidance/) lets robots avoid each other with no messages. The [distributed Lloyd algorithm](/cortes-coverage-algorithm/) spreads robots over a region and never increases the coverage cost.

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course, read these entries in this order: SO(3), Rodrigues' formula, unit quaternions, the [homogeneous transformation matrix](/homogeneous-transformation-matrix/), [forward kinematics](/forward-kinematics/), the Denavit-Hartenberg convention, the geometric Jacobian, inverse kinematics, damped least squares inverse kinematics, the manipulator equation, the recursive Newton-Euler algorithm, the trapezoidal velocity profile, the quintic polynomial trajectory, the Bayes filter, Monte Carlo localization, EKF SLAM, force closure, the unicycle model, pure pursuit and the Laplacian consensus protocol.

If you know the basics, start with a chapter's opening page. Read Chapter 37.1 first, since every later chapter uses its rotations, poses, twists and wrenches. Chapter 37.2 builds on it, and Chapter 37.3 uses the Jacobians of Chapter 37.2. Chapter 37.4 needs Chapter 37.2, and Chapter 37.3 for timing under torque limits. Chapter 37.5 needs Chapter 37.1 and probability, but little of Chapters 37.2 to 37.4. Chapter 37.6 uses wrenches, Jacobians and dynamics. The wheeled robots of Chapter 37.7 stand mostly on their own, while its legged robots use the centroidal and floating-base dynamics of Chapter 37.3. Chapter 37.8 uses the vehicle models of Chapter 37.7 and the graphs of Volume 7. A few entries point forward: [operational space control](/operational-space-formulation/) in Chapter 37.3 uses computed-torque control from Chapter 37.6, and [dead reckoning](/dead-reckoning/) in Chapter 37.5 uses the unicycle model of Chapter 37.7.

Each entry stands on its own. Its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry where each prerequisite is explained. Entries in other volumes are cited by number too.

## The exercises

Each chapter ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ check that you read the entries closely; you should be able to do them in a few minutes. Exercises marked • ask you to use an idea in a new place; each takes some thought, and some need a short program. Exercises marked ★ are harder; some are results from research papers. Hints for many exercises are in the appendix *Hints to the exercises* at the end of the volume.
