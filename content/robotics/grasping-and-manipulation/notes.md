## History

The mechanics of grasping took its modern form in the 1980s. Mishra, Schwartz and Sharir wrote the closure of a grasp in 1987 as a convex-hull condition: the origin of wrench space must lie inside the hull of the contact wrenches. Steinitz's theorem then bounds the number of fingers needed. Markenscoff, Ni and Papadimitriou sharpened the counts in 1990, with four frictionless fingers in the plane and seven in space. In 1988 Nguyen gave the two-finger condition for force closure with friction and introduced regions in which each finger may land independently. The same issue of the journal carried Montana's equations for two smooth bodies rolling on each other.

Grasp quality came next. Ferrari and Canny proposed in 1992 to measure a grasp by the largest ball around the origin inside its wrench space, with the finger forces bounded either in sum or one by one. Many other measures followed, and the review of Roa and Suárez in 2015 sorts them by what they depend on. Rimon and Blake studied cages in the 1990s, where the fingers trap an object without holding it still. Since the 2000s much work on choosing grasps uses data and learning, and the survey of Bohg, Morales, Asfour and Kragic in 2014 sets these methods beside the analytic ones.

Control of the arm grew in parallel. Luh, Walker and Paul computed the model torques online in 1980, the method now called computed-torque control. Takegaki and Arimoto showed in 1981 with an energy-like Lyapunov function that PD feedback plus gravity compensation reaches any set point. In 1987 Slotine and Li gave an adaptive law that needs neither joint accelerations nor the inverse of the estimated inertia. For contact, Raibert and Craig proposed hybrid position and force control in 1981, using Mason's natural and artificial constraints, and Hogan proposed impedance control in 1985. Whitney's quasi-static analysis of 1982 explained when a peg jams or wedges in its hole. Espiau, Chaumette and Rives wrote image-based visual servoing as a task function with the interaction matrix in 1992, and the tutorial of Hutchinson, Hager and Corke in 1996 set it beside the position-based scheme.

Manipulation without grasping and without sensors is mostly Mason's school. Mason analyzed quasi-static pushing in 1986 and gave the voting rule for the sense of rotation. Goyal, Ruina and Papadopoulos described the limit surface of a sliding object in 1991. Erdmann and Mason oriented parts by tilting a tray in 1988, and Goldberg showed in 1993 that squeezes alone orient every polygonal part up to symmetry, with a search that finds a shortest plan. Planning with grasps and placements as discrete choices goes back to Alami, Siméon and Laumond in 1990, and Kaelbling and Lozano-Pérez joined symbolic and motion planning in a hierarchical planner in 2011.

## Further reading

- Kevin M. Lynch and Frank C. Park, *Modern Robotics* (2017), chapters 11 and 12, covers robot control, friction cones, form closure and force closure with linear-program tests.
- Domenico Prattichizzo and Jeffrey C. Trinkle, "Grasping", chapter 38 of the *Springer Handbook of Robotics*, second edition (2016), gives the contact models, the grasp matrix and the classes of grasps.
- Seth Hutchinson, Gregory D. Hager and Peter I. Corke, "A tutorial on visual servo control" (1996), derives the interaction matrix and compares the image-based and position-based schemes.
- Máximo A. Roa and Raúl Suárez, "Grasp quality measures: review and performance" (2015), compares the measures of grasp quality on common examples.
- Jeannette Bohg, Antonio Morales, Tamim Asfour and Danica Kragic, "Data-driven grasp synthesis, a survey" (2014), surveys the analytic and learned methods for choosing a grasp.
