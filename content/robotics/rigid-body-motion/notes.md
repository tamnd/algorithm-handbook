## History

The geometry of rigid motion is older than robots by two centuries. Euler stated in 1775, in a paper on the general motion of rigid bodies printed in 1776 in the Novi Commentarii of the Saint Petersburg Academy, that every motion of a sphere about its centre leaves some diameter in place; this is the rotation theorem, and he proved it by geometry on the sphere. Giulio Mozzi described in 1763, in a book printed in Naples, the instantaneous motion of a rigid body as a turning about an axis and a sliding along it. Michel Chasles stated the theorem for finite and for infinitely small displacements in a short note of 1830 in the Bulletin of Férussac.

Olinde Rodrigues studied the composition of rotations and the geometry of rigid displacements in 1840, and the formula for a rotation from its axis and angle carries his name, although Cheng and Gupta (1989) trace its essence back to Euler. Hamilton found the quaternions in 1843 and published them from 1844, and his rule $q\,(0, v)\,q^*$ is the quaternion form of a rotation. Cayley's paper of 1846 on skew determinants gave the rational map from skew-symmetric matrices to orthogonal ones that now carries his name. Clifford sketched the biquaternions in 1873, and Study developed dual quaternions and his kinematic mapping of rigid motions in 1891. Robert Ball built the theory of screws on Chasles' theorem, with twists and wrenches as its first notions, in his Treatise on the Theory of Screws of 1900.

The space age turned these ideas into algorithms. Attitude control of spacecraft needed a way to store orientations, and Stuelpnagel's survey in SIAM Review in 1964 proved that no three-parameter chart of the rotation group is free of singular points, citing Hopf's result of 1940 that sets the bound of five numbers for a one-to-one nonsingular description. Shepperd, at the Charles Stark Draper Laboratory, published in 1978 a short note on the conversion from a matrix to a quaternion that divides by the largest of four candidates. Shoemake brought quaternions to computer animation in 1985 with spherical linear interpolation.

In robotics, Brockett used exponentials of twists in 1984 to write the kinematics of an arm as a product of exponentials, and the textbooks of Murray, Li and Sastry (1994) and of Lynch and Park (2017) made the Lie group view standard. For uncertainty, Smith and Cheeseman (1986) and Smith, Self and Cheeseman (1988) defined the compounding operators and their first-order covariances, which became the basis of the stochastic map and of EKF SLAM. Long, Wolfe, Mashner and Chirikjian (2012) showed that the banana-shaped cloud of a driving robot is close to Gaussian in exponential coordinates, and Barfoot and Furgale (2014) worked out how to associate uncertainty with poses in three dimensions. Sola, Deray and Atchuthan (2018) collected the tools for state estimation in a short tutorial.

## Further reading

- Kevin M. Lynch and Frank C. Park, Modern Robotics: Mechanics, Planning, and Control, Cambridge University Press, 2017. Chapter 3 covers rotations, homogeneous transformations, twists, wrenches and the exponential coordinates used throughout this chapter, with the same notation.
- Richard M. Murray, Zexiang Li and S. Shankar Sastry, A Mathematical Introduction to Robotic Manipulation, CRC Press, 1994. A more mathematical treatment of SO(3), SE(3), twists and screws, with careful proofs.
- Joan Sola, Jeremie Deray and Dinesh Atchuthan, A micro Lie theory for state estimation in robotics, 2018. A short tutorial on exponential and logarithm maps, adjoints, Jacobians and uncertainty on SO(3) and SE(3).
- Joan Sola, Quaternion kinematics for the error-state Kalman filter, 2017. Quaternion algebra, the Hamilton and JPL conventions, conversions and the integration of the kinematic equation.
- Timothy D. Barfoot, State Estimation for Robotics, Cambridge University Press, 2017. Matrix Lie groups and the representation of uncertain poses for estimation.
- John Stuelpnagel, On the parametrization of the three-dimensional rotation group, SIAM Review 6(4), 1964. The classic survey of rotation parameters and their singularities.
