## History

The geometry of wheeled motion came first as mathematics. Dubins (1957) proved that a shortest path of bounded curvature between two poses is made of at most three pieces, arcs of minimum radius and lines. Reeds and Shepp (1990) allowed the car to reverse and found a sufficient family of 48 words with at most five pieces and two cusps, and Sussmann and Tang (1991) cut it to 46. Campion, Bastin and d'Andréa-Novel (1996) classified wheeled robots by their degrees of mobility and steerability.

Path tracking and local obstacle avoidance grew with real vehicles. Pure pursuit came from the road-following work at Carnegie Mellon in the 1980s, and Coulter (1992) wrote the derivation that most later code follows. Borenstein and Koren (1991) gave the vector field histogram, Fox, Burgard and Thrun (1997) the dynamic window approach, and Fiorini and Shiller (1998) the velocity obstacle. The DARPA challenges brought two more methods: the Stanley controller steered the car that won the Grand Challenge of 2005, and Hybrid A* planned the parking and U-turns of Junior in the Urban Challenge of 2007 (Dolgov, Thrun, Montemerlo and Diebel, 2008). Pivtoraiko and Kelly (2005) introduced state lattices. For covering and exploring, Connolly (1985) posed next-best-view planning on an octree, Yamauchi (1997) drove a robot to the nearest frontier of an evidence grid, and Choset and Pignon (1998) gave the boustrophedon decomposition.

Legged balance starts with McGhee and Frank (1968), who measured static stability by the distance from the projected center of mass to the edge of the support polygon. Vukobratović and Stepanenko (1972) introduced the zero moment point for dynamic walking. Raibert, Brown and Chepponis (1984) balanced one-legged hopping machines with a controller in three parts, and Raibert's book *Legged Robots That Balance* (1986) carried it to bipeds and quadrupeds. McGeer (1990) built walkers that go down a slope with no power. Blickhan (1989) showed that a spring-mass model reproduces running and hopping, and Full and Koditschek (1999) called such a model a template.

Simple models then became controllers. Kajita and colleagues gave the 3D linear inverted pendulum (2001) and walking pattern generation by ZMP preview control (2003). Westervelt, Grizzle and Koditschek (2003) formulated the hybrid zero dynamics for walkers with impacts. Pratt, Carff, Drakunov and Goswami (2006) defined the capture point, Koolen and colleagues (2012) generalized it, and Englsberger and colleagues (2015) called the same quantity the divergent component of motion. Kanoun, Lamiraux and Wieber (2011) put inequality tasks at any priority with a cascade of quadratic programs, and Escande, Mansard and Wieber (2014) gave a solver for the whole hierarchy. Di Carlo and colleagues (2018) ran convex MPC on the MIT Cheetah 3. For quadrotors, Lee, Leok and McClamroch (2010) gave geometric tracking control on $SE(3)$, and Mellinger and Kumar (2011) planned minimum snap trajectories in the flat outputs.

## Further reading

- Kevin M. Lynch and Frank C. Park, *Modern Robotics: Mechanics, Planning, and Control* (2017). Nonholonomic constraints, wheeled mobile robots and their control, in the language of screws and twists.
- Steven M. LaValle, *Planning Algorithms* (2006). Dubins and Reeds-Shepp curves, nonholonomic planning and lattice planners, with full derivations.
- Roland Siegwart, Illah R. Nourbakhsh and Davide Scaramuzza, *Introduction to Autonomous Mobile Robots*, second edition (2011). Wheel kinematics, obstacle avoidance and navigation for wheeled robots.
- Marc H. Raibert, *Legged Robots That Balance* (1986). The hopping machines and the controller that started dynamic legged robotics.
- Shuuji Kajita, Hirohisa Hirukawa, Kensuke Harada and Kazuhito Yokoi, *Introduction to Humanoid Robotics* (2014). The zero moment point, the linear inverted pendulum and preview control for biped walking.
