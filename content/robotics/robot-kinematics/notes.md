## History

Counting the freedoms of a linkage is a nineteenth-century problem. Chebyshev gave a count for planar linkages in 1869 in his work on parallel motions, Grübler stated the planar rule in 1883, and Kutzbach gave the spatial form in 1929. Bennett found in 1903 a spatial loop of four revolute joints that moves although the count says it cannot, the first of many overconstrained mechanisms. Denavit and Hartenberg proposed in 1955 to describe lower-pair mechanisms by four parameters per joint and a product of matrices, and their notation became the standard for robot arms in the 1970s and 1980s.

The first computer-controlled arms raised the inverse problem. Pieper's Stanford thesis of 1968 showed that an arm with three consecutive intersecting axes has a closed-form solution built on a quartic, described Newton iteration for general arms, and left the general six-joint arm open. Whitney proposed in 1969 to command the velocity of the end effector and to resolve it into joint rates through the inverse of the Jacobian, which he called resolved motion rate control. Liégeois added the null space projection for redundant arms in 1977, and Nakamura, Hanafusa and Yoshikawa gave the task-priority form in 1987, which Siciliano and Slotine extended to any number of levels in 1991. Yoshikawa proposed his measure of manipulability in 1985. Wolovich and Elliott and, independently, Balestrino, De Maria and Sciavicco proposed the transpose method in 1984, and Wampler and Nakamura and Hanafusa brought damped least squares to arms in 1986.

The general 6R arm took two decades. Duffy and Crane reduced it in 1980 to a polynomial of degree 32. Tsai and Morgan found 16 solutions by homotopy continuation in 1985 and conjectured that 16 is the bound, and Primrose proved it in 1986. Lee and Liang reached a polynomial of degree 16 in 1988, Manseur and Doty gave an arm with 16 real solutions in 1989, and Raghavan and Roth published their elimination in 1993; Manocha and Canny made it fast and accurate as an eigenvalue problem in 1994. For parallel robots the hard direction is the other one. Gough built a six-legged platform to test tyres in the 1950s and Stewart proposed one for flight simulators in 1965; the general platform was shown to have at most 40 complex assemblies by Ronga and Vust and by Raghavan, Husty gave an elimination to one polynomial of degree 40 in 1996, and Dietmaier found a platform with 40 real ones in 1998.

The geometric view grew alongside. Brockett introduced the product of exponentials in 1984, and Paden's Berkeley thesis of 1986 broke closed-form inverse kinematics into a few subproblems, named after him and Kahan; Murray, Li and Sastry made both standard in 1994. Hayati showed in 1983 that the Denavit-Hartenberg model jumps near parallel axes and changed it for calibration. Computer animation brought cheap methods for long chains: Wang and Chen applied cyclic coordinate descent to arms in 1991, Buss compared the Jacobian methods in a widely read survey in 2004, and Aristidou and Lasenby introduced FABRIK in 2011.

## Further reading

- Kevin M. Lynch and Frank C. Park, Modern Robotics: Mechanics, Planning, and Control, Cambridge University Press, 2017. Chapters 2, 4, 5 and 6 cover degrees of freedom, forward kinematics by the product of exponentials, the Jacobian and numerical inverse kinematics.
- Bruno Siciliano, Lorenzo Sciavicco, Luigi Villani and Giuseppe Oriolo, Robotics: Modelling, Planning and Control, Springer, 2009. Denavit-Hartenberg kinematics, the analytic Jacobian, redundancy resolution and kineto-statics duality.
- Richard M. Murray, Zexiang Li and S. Shankar Sastry, A Mathematical Introduction to Robotic Manipulation, CRC Press, 1994. The screw view of kinematics, with the Paden-Kahan subproblems in their standard form.
- Mark W. Spong, Seth Hutchinson and M. Vidyasagar, Robot Modeling and Control, second edition, Wiley, 2020. A gentle text with the classic Denavit-Hartenberg convention and many worked arms.
- J.-P. Merlet, Parallel Robots, second edition, Springer, 2006. The standard reference for parallel manipulators, their kinematics and their singularities.
