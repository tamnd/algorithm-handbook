A software update arrives on a phone. Before it installs, the phone checks 64 bytes that came with it against a public key that was built into the phone at the factory. If the check passes, the update came from the holder of the matching secret key and nobody changed a single bit on the way. A browser does the same with the certificate of every web site, and a Bitcoin node with every transaction. This is a [digital signature](/digital-signature/): anyone can check it with the public key, and only the holder of the secret key can make it.

The idea is old. Diffie and Hellman proposed it in 1976, and RSA gave the first scheme in 1978. What took longer was to say what "only the holder can make it" means. The accepted answer, from Goldwasser, Micali and Rivest in 1988, is a game: the adversary gets the public key and signatures on messages of its choice, and wins if it outputs a valid signature on any new message. A scheme is secure if no efficient adversary wins with more than negligible probability, under a named assumption such as the hardness of discrete logarithms. Many of the schemes in this chapter have that kind of proof only in the [random oracle model](/random-oracle-model/), where the hash is treated as a random function, and some have no proof at all.

The failures that matter in practice are rarely failures of the hard problem. Sony signed PlayStation 3 software with ECDSA and the same nonce every time, and two signatures gave the key. Java 15 to 18 accepted the ECDSA signature $r = s = 0$ for every message. Implementations of RSA with $e = 3$ that parsed the padding loosely accepted cube roots made by anyone. Much of the chapter is about such details: nonces, range checks, encodings and keys chosen by an adversary.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in eight groups, read in order.

1. **What a signature is.** The goal, the formal [signature scheme](/signature-scheme/) with its sizes, and what [non-repudiation](/non-repudiation/) can and cannot give.
2. **Security notions and real forgeries.** The attacks from [key-only](/key-only-attack/) to [adaptive chosen-message](/chosen-message-attack/), [existential](/existential-unforgeability/) and [strong unforgeability](/strong-unforgeability/), and a table of [forgeries](/signature-forgery/) that happened.
3. **Hash-and-sign with RSA and Rabin.** The [hash-and-sign paradigm](/hash-and-sign-paradigm/), [RSA signatures](/rsa-signature/), [full-domain hash](/rsa-fdh/), [PSS](/rsa-pss/) and [Rabin-Williams](/rabin-signature/), where the encoding decides how good the proof is.
4. **Discrete-log signatures and their nonces.** [ElGamal](/elgamal-signature/), [DSA](/dsa/) and [ECDSA](/ecdsa/), the [nonce reuse attack](/nonce-reuse-attack/) and [deterministic nonces](/deterministic-nonce-generation/).
5. **Schnorr signatures and their proofs.** The [Fiat-Shamir heuristic](/fiat-shamir-heuristic/), the [Schnorr signature](/schnorr-signature/), the [forking lemma](/forking-lemma/) that proves it, [Ed25519](/ed25519/) and [batch verification](/batch-verification/).
6. **Pairings and many signers.** [BLS](/bls-signature/), [aggregate signatures](/aggregate-signature/), [multisignatures](/multisignature-problem/) and [MuSig](/musig/), [threshold signing](/threshold-signing-problem/) and [threshold signatures](/threshold-signature/), and [adaptor signatures](/adaptor-signature/).
7. **Hash-based signatures.** From [Lamport](/lamport-signature/) and [Winternitz](/winternitz-one-time-signature/) one-time keys through the [Merkle signature scheme](/merkle-signature-scheme/) to [LMS](/leighton-micali-signature/), [XMSS](/xmss/) and [SPHINCS+](/sphincs-plus/), with [forward-secure signatures](/forward-secure-signature/).
8. **Signatures with special properties.** [Blind](/blind-signature/), [ring](/ring-signature/), [group](/group-signature/), [designated verifier](/designated-verifier-signature/), [undeniable](/undeniable-signature/) and [proxy](/proxy-signature/) signatures, and [signcryption](/signcryption/).

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these thirteen entries in order: digital signature, signature scheme, existential unforgeability, hash-and-sign, RSA signature, RSA-PSS, DSA, ECDSA, nonce reuse attack, Schnorr signature, Ed25519, Lamport signature and Merkle signature scheme. Most of them are marked *Intro* or *Core*. Skip the security proofs on a first pass, but read the statement of each one: which adversary, which notion, which assumption, and whether the proof needs a random oracle. Leave the forking lemma, the multi-party schemes and group 8 for a second pass.

The worked examples in the entries use toy parameters, such as RSA with $N = 3233$ or a group of order 1019. They show the arithmetic and the attacks. They are not secure, and real keys need parameters of the sizes the entries give.

## Before you start

You need [public-key cryptography](/public-key-cryptography/) and [cryptographic hash functions](/cryptographic-hash-function/) with [collision resistance](/collision-resistance/). For the RSA group you need the [RSA cryptosystem](/rsa-cryptosystem/), [modular exponentiation](/modular-exponentiation/) and the [Chinese remainder theorem](/chinese-remainder-theorem/). For the discrete-log groups you need the [discrete logarithm problem](/discrete-logarithm-problem/), the [modular inverse](/modular-inverse/) and [elliptic curve cryptography](/elliptic-curve-cryptography/). Schnorr signatures build on [sigma protocols](/sigma-protocol/) and the [Schnorr identification protocol](/schnorr-identification-protocol/). BLS needs the [bilinear pairing](/bilinear-pairing/), threshold signatures need [Shamir secret sharing](/shamir-secret-sharing/), and the hash-based schemes need [hash chains](/hash-chain/) and [Merkle trees](/merkle-tree/).
