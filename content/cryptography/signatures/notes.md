## History

Diffie and Hellman described digital signatures in "New directions in cryptography" in 1976, as a use of a trapdoor one-way function, but they had no function to offer. RSA supplied one in 1978, and Rabin gave a variant in 1979 whose security rests on factoring. In the same year Lamport described one-time signatures from any one-way function, and Merkle's thesis put many one-time keys under a hash tree, the idea that his paper "A certified digital signature" (CRYPTO 1989) made widely known. Chaum's blind signatures (Crypto 1982) were the first signatures with an extra property, built for anonymous electronic cash.

ElGamal published a signature based on discrete logarithms in 1984, and Fiat and Shamir showed in 1986 how to turn an identification protocol into a signature by hashing. Schnorr combined the two ideas in 1989, with a short signature in a prime-order subgroup, and patented it. The patent shaped the next decade: NIST proposed DSA in 1991 as a variant outside the patent and published it as FIPS 186 in 1994, and Vanstone proposed ECDSA in 1992. Meanwhile Goldwasser, Micali and Rivest (SIAM Journal on Computing, 1988) defined existential unforgeability under adaptive chosen-message attacks and built the first scheme that meets it under a standard assumption.

Practical schemes got their proofs in the random oracle model. Bellare and Rogaway defined the model in 1993 and proved full-domain hash, and in 1996 they gave PSS with a tight reduction to RSA. Pointcheval and Stern proved Schnorr signatures with the forking lemma in 1996, and Seurin showed in 2012 that, under the one-more discrete log assumption, no algebraic reduction avoids the loss of about $q_h$. ECDSA has no proof from the discrete log problem alone. Boneh, Lynn and Shacham used pairings for short signatures in 2001, and aggregation followed in 2003. Real breaks came from the details: Bleichenbacher's $e = 3$ forgery in 2006, the PlayStation 3 nonce in 2010, and Java's acceptance of $r = s = 0$ in 2022.

Deployment moved toward Schnorr after the patent expired in 2008. Ed25519 appeared in 2011 and became RFC 8032 in 2017, and RFC 6979 (2013) fixed the nonce problem for DSA and ECDSA. Bitcoin added Schnorr signatures (BIP 340) with Taproot in November 2021, and MuSig2 and FROST (RFC 9591, 2024) made multi-party Schnorr practical. Ethereum's beacon chain has used BLS aggregation since December 2020. The threat of quantum computers brought hash-based signatures back: XMSS became RFC 8391 in 2018, LMS RFC 8554 in 2019, both were approved in NIST SP 800-208 in 2020, and SPHINCS+ became SLH-DSA in FIPS 205 in August 2024.

## Further reading

- *Introduction to Modern Cryptography* by Katz and Lindell (third edition, 2020). Good for the definitions of EUF-CMA and SUF-CMA and for clean proofs of hash-and-sign, Lamport and Schnorr signatures.
- *A Graduate Course in Applied Cryptography* by Boneh and Shoup (version 0.6, 2023). Good for the deeper material: tight and loose reductions, the random oracle model, BLS and the post-quantum schemes.
- *Handbook of Applied Cryptography* by Menezes, van Oorschot and Vanstone (1996). Good for the classical schemes worked as algorithms, with the attack and break classification and toy examples.
- *Security arguments for digital signatures and blind signatures* by Pointcheval and Stern (Journal of Cryptology, 2000). Good for the forking lemma in its original form and its use on Schnorr and blind signatures.
- *High-speed high-security signatures* by Bernstein, Duif, Lange, Schwabe and Yang (2012). Good for the design choices behind Ed25519: deterministic nonces, complete formulas and batch verification.
- *The SPHINCS+ signature framework* by Bernstein, Hülsing, Kölbl, Niederhagen, Rijneveld and Schwabe (ACM CCS 2019). Good for how a stateless hash-based scheme fits together and for its security analysis.
