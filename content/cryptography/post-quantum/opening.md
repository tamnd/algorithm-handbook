A large quantum computer running [Shor's algorithm](/shor-algorithm/) would factor integers and compute discrete logarithms in polynomial time, at every key size. That breaks [RSA](/rsa-cryptosystem/), [Diffie-Hellman](/diffie-hellman/) and [elliptic curve cryptography](/elliptic-curve-cryptography/), and with them most of the key exchanges and signatures in use today. [Grover search](/grover-search/) does much less harm to symmetric ciphers and hashes: it searches $2^k$ keys in about $\frac{\pi}{4}2^{k/2}$ steps, but the steps run in series, and NIST judges that AES-128 still meets its lowest security category. So the task is to replace public-key encryption, key exchange and signatures with schemes built on problems for which no fast quantum algorithm is known.

[Post-quantum cryptography](/post-quantum-cryptography/) is that replacement. It runs on ordinary computers. Its problems come from lattices, error-correcting codes, systems of quadratic equations, isogenies between elliptic curves, and hash functions. The work is no longer only research. NIST published ML-KEM, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA as FIPS 203, 204 and 205 on August 13, 2024. Browsers and servers now agree on keys with the hybrid X25519MLKEM768, in which the client sends 1216 bytes instead of 32, and OpenSSH 10.0 made a hybrid with ML-KEM-768 its default key exchange. The reason for the hurry is [harvest now, decrypt later](/harvest-now-decrypt-later/): traffic recorded today can be decrypted once a quantum computer exists, so data with a long life needs post-quantum key exchange now.

The chapter also keeps the failures. Rainbow (a NIST finalist), GeMSS (an HFE variant and an alternate) and SIKE (in round 4) were all broken by classical attacks after they reached the late rounds of the NIST process. Each security claim in the chapter names its assumption, and every parameter set small enough to work by hand is a toy, not a secure choice.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in nine groups, read in order.

1. **The quantum threat and the move to new schemes.** Why the change is needed, [the NIST process](/nist-pqc-standardization/) and its five [security categories](/nist-security-levels/), [crypto agility](/crypto-agility/), and the [quantum random oracle model](/quantum-random-oracle-model/) in which most proofs are done.
2. **Lattice problems.** [Learning with errors](/learning-with-errors/), the [short integer solution](/short-integer-solution/) problem, their ring and module forms, rounding, and the [NTRU problem](/ntru-problem/). Two entries give the worst-case to average-case reductions.
3. **Lattice encryption and key encapsulation.** From [Regev's one-bit scheme](/regev-encryption/) to [ML-KEM (Kyber)](/crystals-kyber/), with FrodoKEM, NewHope, Saber, NTRU and NTRU Prime on the way.
4. **Lattice signatures.** [Fiat-Shamir with aborts](/fiat-shamir-with-aborts/) and [ML-DSA (Dilithium)](/crystals-dilithium/), then Gaussian and trapdoor sampling, the [GPV signature](/gpv-signature/) and [Falcon](/falcon/).
5. **Attacks on lattice and noise problems.** The knapsack breaks, the [core-SVP](/core-svp-hardness/) cost model, and the BKW and Arora-Ge algorithms.
6. **Code-based encryption.** [Syndrome decoding](/syndrome-decoding-problem/), [McEliece](/mceliece-cryptosystem/), Niederreiter, BIKE and [HQC](/hqc/).
7. **Information set decoding.** [Prange's algorithm](/prange-algorithm/) and its refinements, the attacks that size code-based keys.
8. **Multivariate signatures and Picnic.** The [MQ problem](/multivariate-quadratic-problem/), [unbalanced oil and vinegar](/unbalanced-oil-and-vinegar/), the broken HFE and [Rainbow](/rainbow-signature/), and Picnic, which needs only a block cipher and a hash.
9. **Isogenies.** The [supersingular isogeny problem](/supersingular-isogeny-problem/), [SIDH](/sidh/) and the [Castryck-Decru attack](/castryck-decru-attack/) that broke it, and [CSIDH](/csidh/).

Hash-based signatures, the fifth family, are in Chapter 23.4: see [hash-based signatures](/hash-based-signatures/) and [SLH-DSA (SPHINCS+)](/sphincs-plus/).

## Before you start

You need the basics of [public-key cryptography](/public-key-cryptography/), [key encapsulation](/key-encapsulation-mechanism/), the [Fujisaki-Okamoto transform](/fujisaki-okamoto-transform/), [digital signatures](/digital-signature/), the [Fiat-Shamir heuristic](/fiat-shamir-heuristic/) and the [random oracle model](/random-oracle-model/) from earlier chapters of this volume. For the lattice groups you need [lattices](/lattice/), the [shortest vector problem](/shortest-vector-problem/), [LLL](/lll-algorithm/) and [BKZ](/bkz-algorithm/) from the number theory volume, and the [number-theoretic transform](/number-theoretic-transform/) for fast polynomial products. The code groups use [linear codes](/linear-code/), [parity-check matrices](/parity-check-matrix/) and [Goppa codes](/goppa-code/). Arithmetic mod $q$ and in polynomial rings such as $\mathbb{Z}_q[x]/(x^n + 1)$ is used throughout. Of quantum computing, you need only what [Shor's algorithm](/shor-algorithm/) and Grover search do, not how.
