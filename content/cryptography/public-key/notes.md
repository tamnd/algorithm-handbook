## History

Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman described public-key cryptography in "New directions in cryptography" (IEEE Transactions on Information Theory, November 1976), without a concrete trapdoor for encryption. Rivest, Shamir and Adleman found one at MIT in 1977 and published RSA in the Communications of the ACM in February 1978. Merkle and Hellman offered a second candidate the same year, the knapsack, which Shamir broke in 1982, and after Lagarias and Odlyzko's lattice attack of 1985, no knapsack scheme has survived. Rabin's report of January 1979 gave a scheme whose inversion is provably as hard as factoring, which RSA cannot claim. At GCHQ, Ellis had the idea in 1970, Cocks wrote down a form of RSA in 1973 and Williamson found the key agreement method in 1974, but this work became public only in December 1997.

Goldwasser and Micali changed the question in 1982 (journal version 1984). They defined semantic security, proved it equivalent to indistinguishability, and showed that it needs [probabilistic encryption](/probabilistic-encryption/); their scheme on quadratic residues was the first with a proof. Blum and Goldwasser cut its expansion in 1984. ElGamal published his discrete-log scheme in 1985, and Miller (1985) and Koblitz (1987) moved such schemes to elliptic curves.

Active attacks came next. Naor and Yung (1990) built the first scheme secure against lunchtime chosen-ciphertext attacks, Rackoff and Simon (1991) defined the adaptive notion, and Dolev, Dwork and Naor (1991) defined non-malleability. Bellare and Rogaway proposed OAEP at Eurocrypt 1994 with a proof in the random oracle model. In 1998 two results arrived together: Bleichenbacher's attack on PKCS #1 v1.5 at CRYPTO, and Cramer and Shoup's scheme, the first practical one proved IND-CCA2 without random oracles. Fujisaki and Okamoto gave their transform in 1999. In 2001 Shoup found a gap in the OAEP proof, Fujisaki, Okamoto, Pointcheval and Stern proved RSA-OAEP secure under the RSA assumption, and Shoup's proposal for an ISO standard introduced the split into KEM and DEM.

Implementations became a subject of their own. Kocher's timing attack appeared in 1996, and Boneh, DeMillo and Lipton presented the fault attack at Eurocrypt 1997, after Lenstra's memo of 1996 had reduced it to one gcd. Brumley and Boneh made timing attacks remote in 2003. Padding oracles kept returning, from ROBOT (USENIX Security 2018) to the Marvin attack (ESORICS 2023), until TLS 1.3 removed RSA key transport.

Structured encryption grew alongside. Rivest, Adleman and Dertouzos asked for computation on encrypted data in 1978. Okamoto-Uchiyama and Naccache-Stern (1998) and Paillier (1999) gave additive schemes, and Gentry's 2009 construction gave the first fully homomorphic one. Shamir asked for identity-based encryption in 1984; after the MOV reduction of 1993 and Joux's three-party exchange of 2000, Boneh and Franklin, and independently Cocks, built it in 2001, and Sahai and Waters introduced attribute-based encryption in 2005.

## Further reading

- Jonathan Katz and Yehuda Lindell, *Introduction to Modern Cryptography*, third edition (CRC Press). The definitions of IND-CPA and IND-CCA, the number-theoretic assumptions, the KEM and DEM paradigm, and proofs for ElGamal, Goldwasser-Micali, Paillier and hybrid encryption.
- Alfred J. Menezes, Paul C. van Oorschot and Scott A. Vanstone, *Handbook of Applied Cryptography* (1996). Chapter 8 works RSA, Rabin, ElGamal, Goldwasser-Micali, Blum-Goldwasser and the knapsack with numeric examples.
- Oded Goldreich, *Foundations of Cryptography, Volume 2: Basic Applications* (2004). The rigorous treatment of semantic security, chosen-ciphertext security and non-malleability, built on the theory of Volume 1.
- Dan Boneh and Victor Shoup, *A Graduate Course in Applied Cryptography*, version 0.6 (2023). Semantic security, ElGamal under DDH, key encapsulation and chosen-ciphertext secure encryption such as Cramer-Shoup, with concrete bounds.
- Dan Boneh, *Twenty Years of Attacks on the RSA Cryptosystem*, Notices of the AMS 46(2) (1999). A short survey of the elementary attacks, small public and private exponents, Wiener's attack and implementation attacks.
- Darrel Hankerson, Alfred Menezes and Scott Vanstone, *Guide to Elliptic Curve Cryptography* (2004). The arithmetic and the standard schemes on curves, including ECIES.
