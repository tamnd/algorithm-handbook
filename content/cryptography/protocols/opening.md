Alice and Bob are on the phone and want to settle a bet with a coin toss. If Alice tosses and reports the result, Bob must trust her. If Alice calls her guess first, Bob can then announce whatever result beats it. In 1981 Manuel Blum showed a way out. Alice seals a random bit in a [commitment](/commitment-scheme/) and sends it, Bob says a random bit out loud, Alice opens her commitment, and the coin is the XOR of the two bits. Neither can steer the result while both finish. But Alice sees the result first and can hang up, and Cleve proved in 1986 that no protocol with $r$ rounds removes that advantage below $\Omega(1/r)$. The whole chapter has this shape: a task that would be easy with a trusted third party, a protocol that replaces the third party, and a precise statement of what a cheating party can still do.

A protocol is a set of rules for parties who exchange messages, some of whom may not follow the rules. Its security claim always names the adversary. A *semi-honest* party follows the protocol and studies what it saw afterwards; a *malicious* party sends whatever helps it. Some results need an honest majority of the parties, some hold for any number of corrupted parties, and some only with abort. Security is stated by *simulation*, where a real run must look like an ideal run with a trusted party, or by a *game*, where an attacker must win some contest. Many proofs use the [random oracle model](/random-oracle-model/), and the entries say when. Read the model before the result: [Yao's garbled circuits](/yao-garbled-circuits/) are secure against a semi-honest party and need [cut-and-choose](/cut-and-choose/) or other checks before they are secure against a malicious one.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in nine groups, read in order.

1. **Authentication and key distribution.** [Entity authentication](/entity-authentication/), [challenge-response](/challenge-response-authentication/) with HOTP and TOTP, the [key distribution center](/key-distribution-center/), the [Needham-Schroeder lineage](/needham-schroeder/) with a model checker that finds Lowe's attack, and [Kerberos](/kerberos/).
2. **Secure channels in use.** The [TLS 1.3 handshake](/tls-handshake/), the goals of [secure messaging](/secure-messaging/), and the [Signal protocol](/signal-protocol/).
3. **Commitments and coin flipping.** The [commitment scheme](/commitment-scheme/), the [Pedersen commitment](/pedersen-commitment/), and [fair coin flipping](/fair-coin-flipping/).
4. **Zero knowledge and identification.** The [zero-knowledge proof](/zero-knowledge-proof/), the [proof of knowledge](/proof-of-knowledge/), the [sigma protocol](/sigma-protocol/), and the identification schemes of [Schnorr](/schnorr-identification-protocol/) and of [Feige, Fiat and Shamir](/feige-fiat-shamir-identification/).
5. **Secret sharing and threshold cryptography.** [Secret sharing](/secret-sharing/), [Shamir's scheme](/shamir-secret-sharing/), [verifiable secret sharing](/verifiable-secret-sharing/), and [threshold cryptography](/threshold-cryptography/).
6. **Secure computation and its models.** [Secure multiparty computation](/secure-multiparty-computation/), [Yao's millionaires' problem](/yao-millionaires-problem/), the [semi-honest model](/semi-honest-model/), the [malicious model](/malicious-adversary-model/), and [universal composability](/universal-composability/).
7. **Generic protocols.** [Oblivious transfer](/oblivious-transfer/), [OT extension](/oblivious-transfer-extension/), [Yao's garbled circuits](/yao-garbled-circuits/), [cut-and-choose](/cut-and-choose/), and the [GMW](/gmw-protocol/) and [BGW](/bgw-protocol/) protocols.
8. **Privacy protocols.** [Private set intersection](/private-set-intersection/), [private information retrieval](/private-information-retrieval/), [anonymous communication](/anonymous-communication/), the [dining cryptographers protocol](/dining-cryptographers/), and the [mix network](/mix-network/).
9. **Succinct proofs and verifiable randomness.** The [zk-SNARK](/zk-snark/), the [zk-STARK](/zk-stark/), and the [verifiable random function](/verifiable-random-function/).

## A first reading

A first pass can take twelve entries. Read [entity authentication](/entity-authentication/) and [challenge-response](/challenge-response-authentication/) to see why a fresh nonce and the name of the prover must go into the answer. Read the [TLS handshake](/tls-handshake/) as the protocol you use every day. Then read the [commitment scheme](/commitment-scheme/) and [fair coin flipping](/fair-coin-flipping/), the [zero-knowledge proof](/zero-knowledge-proof/) with its graph isomorphism example, and the [Schnorr identification protocol](/schnorr-identification-protocol/), whose small numbers you can check by hand. From the sharing group take [Shamir's scheme](/shamir-secret-sharing/). Finish with [secure multiparty computation](/secure-multiparty-computation/), the [semi-honest model](/semi-honest-model/), [oblivious transfer](/oblivious-transfer/) and [Yao's garbled circuits](/yao-garbled-circuits/).

Skip on a first pass the Needham-Schroeder model checker, universal composability, OT extension, BGW, the mix network and the last group. They are research level and lean on the first-pass entries. The Signal protocol reads best after the double ratchet in Chapter 23.5.
