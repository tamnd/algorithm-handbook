## History

Protocols for networks came first. Roger Needham and Michael Schroeder published "Using encryption for authentication in large networks of computers" (Communications of the ACM, 1978), with a symmetric protocol through a key server and a public-key protocol. Denning and Sacco (1981) showed that an old session key lets an attacker replay the symmetric one, and timestamps fixed it. Kerberos, built at MIT's Project Athena and described by Steiner, Neuman and Schiller in 1988, grew from the symmetric protocol. Gavin Lowe found the attack on the public-key protocol in 1995 and found it again with the FDR model checker in 1996, seventeen years after the protocol appeared.

The tools of the chapter came from a few years around 1980. Adi Shamir and George Blakley each published threshold secret sharing in 1979. Manuel Blum flipped a coin by telephone in 1981, Michael Rabin defined oblivious transfer the same year, and Even, Goldreich and Lempel gave the 1-out-of-2 form in 1985. Chor, Goldwasser, Micali and Awerbuch defined verifiable secret sharing in 1985; Feldman (1987) and Pedersen (1991) gave the schemes still in use, and Desmedt and Frankel (1989) started threshold cryptosystems. Cleve (1986) proved that a coin flip with $r$ rounds always leaves a bias of order $1/r$.

Goldwasser, Micali and Rackoff defined zero knowledge at STOC in 1985. Goldreich, Micali and Wigderson showed in 1986 that every language in NP has a zero-knowledge proof if one-way functions exist. Fiat and Shamir (1986) and Feige, Fiat and Shamir (1987) turned zero knowledge into identification, and Schnorr (1989) gave the short discrete-log protocol behind many signatures. Cramer's thesis (1996) gave sigma protocols their name.

Secure computation started with Andrew Yao, who posed the millionaires' problem in 1982; the garbled circuit construction goes back to his paper "How to generate and exchange secrets" (1986) and his talks of that time, though he never wrote it out in full. Goldreich, Micali and Wigderson presented "How to play any mental game" at STOC in 1987, and at STOC in 1988 Ben-Or, Goldwasser and Wigderson, and independently Chaum, Crépeau and Damgård, gave information-theoretic protocols with an honest majority. Kilian (1988) showed that oblivious transfer alone suffices, Beaver (1991) introduced multiplication triples, and Ishai, Kilian, Nissim and Petrank made OT cheap by extension in 2003. Canetti defined universal composability in 2001. Garbled circuits shrank from free XOR (Kolesnikov and Schneider, 2008) to half gates (Zahur, Rosulek and Evans, 2015) and three halves (Rosulek and Roy, 2021).

David Chaum founded anonymous communication with mix networks (1981) and the dining cryptographers (1988). Chor, Goldreich, Kushilevitz and Sudan defined private information retrieval in 1995. Micali, Rabin and Vadhan defined verifiable random functions in 1999. Succinct proofs moved from theory to deployment with the quadratic arithmetic programs of Gennaro, Gentry, Parno and Raykova (2013) and Groth's proof of three group elements (2016), and Ben-Sasson, Bentov, Horesh and Riabzev gave transparent STARKs in 2018. TLS 1.3 became RFC 8446 in 2018, and the Signal specifications of Marlinspike and Perrin appeared in 2016.

## Further reading

- Oded Goldreich, *Foundations of Cryptography, Volume 2: Basic Applications* (2004). The definitions of zero knowledge and secure computation and the full GMW compiler, with proofs.
- Carmit Hazay and Yehuda Lindell, *Efficient Secure Two-Party Protocols* (2010). Simulation proofs for Yao's protocol, cut-and-choose and oblivious transfer in the semi-honest and malicious models.
- David Evans, Vladimir Kolesnikov and Mike Rosulek, *A Pragmatic Introduction to Secure Multi-Party Computation* (2018). Garbling, OT extension, GMW and PSI as they are built today; free online.
- Ronald Cramer, Ivan Damgård and Jesper Buus Nielsen, *Secure Multiparty Computation and Secret Sharing* (2015). The information-theoretic side: BGW, verifiable secret sharing and the UC framework.
- Dan Boneh and Victor Shoup, *A Graduate Course in Applied Cryptography*, free draft. Identification, sigma protocols and key exchange with game-based proofs.
- Colin Boyd, Anish Mathuria and Douglas Stebila, *Protocols for Authentication and Key Establishment*, second edition (2020). The Needham-Schroeder family, Kerberos and TLS, with their attacks.
