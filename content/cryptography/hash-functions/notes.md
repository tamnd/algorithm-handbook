## History

The first ideas came at the end of the 1970s. Gideon Yuval showed in 1979 how the birthday paradox lets a forger find two documents with one signature. In the same year Ralph Merkle described hash trees in his Stanford thesis, and Robert Morris and Ken Thompson described salted, iterated password hashing for Unix. Leslie Lamport proposed hash chains for one-time passwords in 1981, and Martin Hellman published his time-memory tradeoff in 1980. Ten years later, at CRYPTO 1989, Merkle and Ivan Damgård each showed how to iterate a fixed-size compression function so that collisions of the hash give collisions of the compression function.

The 1990s were the decade of dedicated designs. Ronald Rivest published MD4 in 1990 and MD5 in 1991, and NIST published SHA in 1993 and replaced it with SHA-1 in 1995. Preneel, Govaerts and Vandewalle classified the ways to build a compression function from a block cipher in 1993. Hans Dobbertin found collisions of MD4 in 1996, the year that RIPEMD-160 and Tiger appeared at Fast Software Encryption. On the theory side, Bellare and Rogaway made the random oracle model explicit in 1993, and Bellare, Canetti and Krawczyk defined HMAC in 1996. Canetti, Goldreich and Halevi showed in 1998 that a scheme can be secure with a random oracle and insecure with every real hash.

The attacks of 2004 and 2005 changed the field. Antoine Joux found multicollisions in iterated hashes in 2004, and Kelsey and Schneier, then Kelsey and Kohno, turned the same structure into second preimages and herding. Xiaoyun Wang and her colleagues announced collisions of MD5 in 2004 and gave an attack on SHA-1 in 2005. Stevens, Lenstra and de Weger found chosen-prefix collisions of MD5 in 2007, and they were used to forge a certificate authority and, in the Flame malware, a Microsoft code-signing certificate. The first SHA-1 collision came in 2017 and a chosen-prefix collision in 2020. The theory answered with precise definitions: Black, Rogaway and Shrimpton proved the block-cipher constructions in the ideal cipher model in 2002, Rogaway and Shrimpton set out how the three properties relate in 2004, and Coron, Dodis, Malinaud and Puniya applied the indifferentiability of Maurer, Renner and Holenstein to hash functions in 2005.

NIST announced a competition for a new hash in 2007. Bertoni, Daemen, Peeters and Van Assche had introduced the sponge the same year and proved it indifferentiable in 2008, and their Keccak won in October 2012, ahead of BLAKE, Grøstl, JH and Skein; FIPS 202 followed in August 2015. SHA-2 itself was never broken and remains the default. BLAKE2 (2012) and BLAKE3 (2020) carried the BLAKE design into fast software hashes with keyed and tree modes.

Password hashing grew in parallel. PBKDF2 appeared in RFC 2898 in 2000, and Provos and Mazières presented bcrypt in 1999. Oechslin's rainbow tables of 2003 showed how fast unsalted hashes fall. Colin Percival's scrypt of 2009 made memory the cost, Alwen and Serbinenko gave the cumulative memory measure in 2015, and Argon2 won the Password Hashing Competition in 2015 and became RFC 9106 in 2021.

## Further reading

- Alfred J. Menezes, Paul C. van Oorschot and Scott A. Vanstone, *Handbook of Applied Cryptography*, CRC Press, 1996. Chapter 9 is the classic reference for the constructions, padding, fixed points and the attacks known in 1996.
- Jonathan Katz and Yehuda Lindell, *Introduction to Modern Cryptography*, third edition, CRC Press. The clearest textbook account of collision resistance, the Merkle-Damgård transform, birthday attacks, HMAC and the random oracle model, with full proofs.
- Dan Boneh and Victor Shoup, *A Graduate Course in Applied Cryptography*. Its Chapter 8, on message integrity from collision-resistant hashing, covers the birthday attack, Merkle-Damgård and the random oracle model with proofs.
- Phillip Rogaway and Thomas Shrimpton, "Cryptographic hash-function basics: definitions, implications, and separations for preimage resistance, second-preimage resistance, and collision resistance", FSE 2004. The short paper to read for exactly how the properties imply one another and where they do not.
- Jean-Philippe Aumasson, Willi Meier, Raphael C.-W. Phan and Luca Henzen, *The Hash Function BLAKE*, Springer, 2014. A book on one SHA-3 finalist, from its design choices to its cryptanalysis.
