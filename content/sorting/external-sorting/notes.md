## History

External sorting is older than the stored-program computer. Punched-card sorters split decks of cards by one digit at a time, and sorting files on magnetic tape was one of the main jobs of the business computers of the 1950s. Most of the methods of this chapter come from that era. Merging runs from several tapes, the balanced merge, and forming runs with a heap by replacement selection were all known by the end of the 1950s; Knuth's Section 5.4 tells their early history and gives the snowplow argument that explains why replacement selection makes runs about twice as long as memory.

The tape sorts grew more refined as people tried to keep every drive busy. B. K. Betz and W. C. Carter described the cascade merge in 1959. R. L. Gilstad described the polyphase merge in 1960, with its uneven distributions of runs related to the Fibonacci numbers. Sheldon Sobel published the oscillating sort in 1962, for drives that could read backward and so never needed to rewind. Knuth's analysis of these methods in Sections 5.4.2 to 5.4.5 compares them by the amount of data they move, for each number of tapes.

When disks replaced tapes, the problem changed. A disk can read any block, so a merge can take its runs from one device, and the fan-in is limited only by memory. Alok Aggarwal and Jeffrey Scott Vitter made this precise in 1988. Their model has a memory of $M$ items and blocks of $B$ items, and they proved that sorting takes $\Theta\big(\frac NB \log_{M/B} \frac NB\big)$ block transfers, which the multiway merge sort and a distribution sort both achieve. Vitter and Elizabeth Shriver introduced the parallel disk model, with $D$ independent disks, in 1994. Disk striping, which Kenneth Salem and Hector Garcia-Molina studied in 1986 and which RAID arrays use, is not optimal there when $D$ is large. Mark Nodine and Vitter gave the first deterministic optimal sorts for many disks: Balance Sort in 1993 and Greed Sort in 1995. Vitter and David Hutchinson later gave distribution sort with randomized cycling, a simpler method with small constants.

The last step was to remove the parameters. In 1999 Matteo Frigo, Charles Leiserson, Harald Prokop and Sridhar Ramachandran defined the cache-oblivious model and gave funnelsort, which meets the sorting bound without knowing $M$ or $B$. Gerth Stølting Brodal and Rolf Fagerberg gave a simpler lazy funnelsort in 2002, and in 2003 they showed that the tall-cache assumption that funnelsort uses cannot be dropped. Roman Dementiev and Peter Sanders showed in 2003 how a practical sorter can overlap I/O and computation on many disks at once.

## Further reading

- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 3: Sorting and Searching*, second edition (1998). Section 5.4 covers every tape sort of the chapter, replacement selection and the merge with a tree of losers, with tables of their costs.
- Jeffrey Scott Vitter, *Algorithms and Data Structures for External Memory*, Foundations and Trends in Theoretical Computer Science 2(4), 2008. The standard survey of the external memory and parallel disk models: merge and distribution sorts, striping, the lower bounds and permuting.
- Alok Aggarwal and Jeffrey Scott Vitter, *The Input/Output Complexity of Sorting and Related Problems*, Communications of the ACM 31(9), 1988. The paper that set out the model and proved the sorting bound; short and readable.
- Raghu Ramakrishnan and Johannes Gehrke, *Database Management Systems*, third edition. Chapter 13 shows external sorting as a database system does it, with buffers, replacement selection and the cost of passes.
- Matteo Frigo, Charles E. Leiserson, Harald Prokop and Sridhar Ramachandran, *Cache-Oblivious Algorithms*, Proceedings of the 40th Annual Symposium on Foundations of Computer Science, 1999. The model and funnelsort.
