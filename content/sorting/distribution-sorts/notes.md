## History

Distribution sorting is older than the computer. Punched-card sorting machines dropped each card into one of ten pockets by the digit punched in one column, and an operator sorted a key of several columns by running the deck through the machine once per column, from the last column to the first. That is LSD radix sort. Sorting from the first column also worked, but the operator then had to keep many piles apart. Knuth credits H. H. Seward, in his master's thesis at MIT in 1954, with the computer form: count the digits first, then place each record directly. This distribution counting is the counting sort of today.

For binary computers, Paul Hildebrandt and Harold Isbitz published radix exchange sort in 1959. It splits the keys by their top bit with exchanges from both ends, two years before Hoare published quicksort with the same exchange pattern around a pivot. Bucket sort grew from the address calculation and multiple list insertion of Knuth's Section 5.2.1; its textbook form, with $n$ buckets and a proof of expected linear time, is the one in Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest and Stein.

Strings drove the next round of work. Peter McIlroy, Keith Bostic and Douglas McIlroy engineered MSD radix sort for the BSD library in 1993, with an explicit stack and an in-place permutation that they named American flag sort, after Dijkstra's Dutch national flag problem. Jon Bentley and Robert Sedgewick presented multikey quicksort at SODA in 1997, showed that it is the same recursion as a ternary search tree, and proved that it reads $O(n \log n + D)$ characters in expectation. Ranjan Sinha and Justin Zobel built burstsort on the burst trie in 2003 to cut cache misses on large sets of strings. Engineered sorts for numbers appeared in the same years: Karl-Dietrich Neubert's flashsort in 1998, Thomas Standish's proxmap sort in his 1998 textbook, and Steven Ross's spreadsort in 2002, now in the Boost libraries.

The theory of integer sorting on the word RAM asks how fast $n$ integers that each fit in a word can be sorted, for every word length. David Kirkpatrick and Stefan Reisch gave an early bound below $n \log n$ in 1984. Michael Fredman and Dan Willard's fusion trees gave $O(n \log n / \log \log n)$ deterministically. Arne Andersson, Torben Hagerup, Stefan Nilsson and Rajeev Raman asked *Sorting in linear time?* and answered yes in expectation for words of at least $(\log n)^{2 + \varepsilon}$ bits, with $O(n \log \log n)$ in general. Yijie Han reached $O(n \log \log n)$ deterministic time in linear space at STOC 2002, and Han and Mikkel Thorup reached $O(n \sqrt{\log \log n})$ expected time at FOCS 2002. Thorup also proved that sorting and priority queues are equivalent on the word RAM, so each sorting bound gives a priority queue bound. Whether integers can be sorted in linear time for every word length is still open.

## Further reading

- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 3: Sorting and Searching*, second edition (1998). Section 5.2 introduces distribution counting, and Section 5.2.5 covers radix sorting with its history from the card sorters on.
- Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest and Clifford Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, fourth edition (2022). Chapter 8 gives counting sort, radix sort with the choice of digit size, and bucket sort with its expected-time proof.
- Robert Sedgewick and Kevin Wayne, *Algorithms*, fourth edition (2011). Section 5.1 covers LSD, MSD and three-way string sorts as working code, with measurements and a table of their costs.
- Peter M. McIlroy, Keith Bostic and M. Douglas McIlroy, *Engineering Radix Sort*, Computing Systems 6(1), 1993. How to make MSD radix sort fast and in place in a real library.
- Jon L. Bentley and Robert Sedgewick, *Fast Algorithms for Sorting and Searching Strings*, Proceedings of the Eighth Annual ACM-SIAM Symposium on Discrete Algorithms, 1997. Multikey quicksort and ternary search trees, with the analysis in terms of $D$.
- Arne Andersson, Torben Hagerup, Stefan Nilsson and Rajeev Raman, *Sorting in Linear Time?*, Journal of Computer and System Sciences 57(1), 1998. The word RAM view of integer sorting, with packed sorting and signature sort.
