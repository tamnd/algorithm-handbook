## History

The oldest question in the chapter came from sport. In 1883 Lewis Carroll pointed out that a knockout tennis tournament gives the second prize unfairly: the player who loses the final need not be the second best, since the second best may have met the winner in an early round. Józef Schreier gave a method in 1932 that finds the second best with $n + \lceil \lg n \rceil - 2$ games, and Hugo Steinhaus conjectured that no method does better. S. S. Kislitsyn proved it in 1964. Ira Pohl showed in 1972 that the minimum and the maximum together need exactly $\lceil 3n/2 \rceil - 2$ comparisons.

C. A. R. Hoare published FIND, the algorithm now called [quickselect](/quickselect/), in the *Communications of the ACM* in July 1961, as Algorithm 65, next to Partition and Quicksort. It finds the item of any rank in linear expected time but quadratic time in the worst case. Whether selection could be done in linear time in the worst case stayed open until 1973, when Manuel Blum, Robert Floyd, Vaughan Pratt, Ronald Rivest and Robert Tarjan published *Time bounds for selection*. Their paper gave the [median of medians](/median-of-medians/) pivot, a refined version that makes at most $5.4305n$ comparisons, and lower bounds of the form $n + \min(k, n - k + 1) - 2$, so the order of growth of selection was settled at once.

The constants then became the question. Floyd and Rivest showed in 1975 that a pivot taken from a random sample gives $n + \min(k, n - k) + o(n)$ comparisons on average, and Walter Cunto and Ian Munro proved in 1989 that no method does better on average. For the worst case, Arnold Schönhage, Mike Paterson and Nicholas Pippenger found the median in $3n + o(n)$ comparisons in 1976, and Dorit Dor and Uri Zwick brought this below $2.95n$ in 1999. Samuel Bent and John John proved a lower bound of $2n - o(n)$ in 1985, and Dor and Zwick raised it to $(2 + 2^{-80})n$ in 2001. The exact constant is still unknown.

Other results filled in the picture. Abdollah Hadian and Milton Sobel gave the upper bound for the $i$-th smallest when $i$ is small in 1969, and Frank Fussenegger and Harold Gabow a lower bound by counting in 1979. Greg Frederickson and Donald Johnson found the optimal method for [sorted matrices](/sorted-matrix-selection/) in 1984. David Dobkin and Ian Munro gave the counting lower bound for [multiple selection](/multiple-selection/) in 1981, and Kanela Kaligosi, Kurt Mehlhorn, Ian Munro and Peter Sanders came within lower-order terms of it in 2005. In practice, David Musser's [introselect](/introselect/) of 1997 made the worst-case guarantee cheap: it runs as fast as quickselect until the pivots go bad.

## Further reading

- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 3: Sorting and Searching*, second edition (1998). Section 5.3.3 treats minimum-comparison selection: tournaments, small order statistics and the lower bounds, with their history.
- Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest and Clifford Stein, *Introduction to Algorithms*, fourth edition (2022). Chapter 9 gives the minimum and maximum, randomized selection and the linear-time worst-case method with careful proofs.
- Manuel Blum, Robert W. Floyd, Vaughan Pratt, Ronald L. Rivest and Robert E. Tarjan, *Time bounds for selection*, Journal of Computer and System Sciences 7(4), 1973. The original paper on linear-time selection, and still readable.
- Robert W. Floyd and Ronald L. Rivest, *Expected time bounds for selection*, Communications of the ACM 18(3), 1975. The sampling method, with its analysis and the program as Algorithm 489.
- Dorit Dor and Uri Zwick, *Selecting the median*, SIAM Journal on Computing 28(5), 1999. The method behind the best known worst-case bound for the median, fewer than $2.95n$ comparisons.
- H. A. David and H. N. Nagaraja, *Order Statistics*, third edition (2003). The statistician's view of order statistics, medians and quantiles of random samples.
